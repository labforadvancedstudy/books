# L4 — Gender in Society

Level 3 covered sexuality and relationships — orientation, relationship forms, reproduction, STIs, consent. Level 4 addresses gender at the societal scale — how gender organizes labor, family structure, politics, law, and institutions; how it has changed over recent generations; and how debates over gender are playing out in contemporary societies. Gender analysis combines empirical social science, historical inquiry, political theory, and ongoing cultural negotiation. Understanding gender in society requires distinguishing what can be documented empirically from what is legitimately contested, while taking both seriously.

## Conceptual Foundations

Gender as a social category is distinguished from biological sex (discussed at L1) though they interact. Gender encompasses the roles, expectations, practices, and identities that societies construct around sex differences, as well as patterns of inequality, power, and opportunity organized along gendered lines.

Influential theoretical approaches include:

- **Gender as social structure** (Barbara Risman): Gender operates at individual, interactional, and institutional levels. Structural analysis shows how gendered institutions reproduce patterns independently of individual intent.
- **Doing gender** (West and Zimmerman): Gender is performed in everyday interactions rather than simply possessed. Interactional accountability to gender expectations shapes behavior.
- **Gender as performative** (Judith Butler): Gender identity emerges through repeated performative acts; it does not preexist its expression.
- **Hegemonic masculinity** (R.W. Connell): Multiple masculinities exist in hierarchy; hegemonic masculinity is culturally dominant and legitimates male dominance over women and subordinated masculinities.
- **Intersectionality** (Kimberlé Crenshaw): Gender intersects with race, class, sexuality, disability, and other categories producing distinct experiences. Analysis cannot separate these axes without distortion.

Each framework has strengths and empirical support in specific contexts; each has been criticized and refined. Contemporary social science of gender typically draws on multiple frameworks.

Empirical study of gender involves survey research, ethnography, historical analysis, experimental methods, and administrative data. Major data sources include labor statistics, time use surveys, health surveys, electoral data, and corporate and institutional records.

## Gender in the Labor Market

Labor force participation by women has increased dramatically over the 20th and early 21st centuries in most developed economies. U.S. female labor force participation rose from roughly 33 percent in 1948 to peak around 60 percent in 2000, and has since plateaued or slightly declined. Many European countries and Japan have seen continued increases; U.S. plateau has been attributed to insufficient work-family support policies relative to peers.

The gender pay gap — women's median earnings as a share of men's — varies by country, measurement, and age cohort. U.S. median female annual earnings are roughly 82 percent of male median earnings; adjusted estimates (controlling for occupation, hours, experience) reduce but do not eliminate the gap. OECD countries show substantial variation; Iceland, Belgium, Norway lead in smallest gaps; Korea, Japan, and Latvia among the largest.

Major drivers of the gender pay gap include:

- **Occupational segregation**: Women concentrated in lower-paying occupations (care work, education, administrative support)
- **Vertical segregation**: Women underrepresented in leadership; "glass ceiling" restricting advancement
- **Hours worked**: Women average fewer paid work hours due to caregiving responsibilities
- **Motherhood penalty**: Women's earnings decline substantially after childbirth; fathers often see small "fatherhood premium"
- **Discrimination**: Direct and indirect discrimination in hiring, compensation, and promotion, documented in audit studies

Occupational segregation has decreased but persists. Computing, engineering, and skilled trades remain male-dominated. Nursing, teaching, and social work remain female-dominated. Some formerly male occupations (banking, pharmacy) have become female-dominated or integrated.

The care economy — paid and unpaid care work — is highly feminized globally. Women perform about three-quarters of unpaid care work globally (ILO data). Formalizing and adequately compensating care work is a major policy and social movement focus.

Labor market gender dynamics vary substantially by sector, geography, and intersection with other characteristics. Women of color in the U.S. face larger wage gaps than white women. LGBTQ+ workers face both earnings and employment discrimination in many contexts.

## Family and Household Formation

Household structures have diversified substantially. The "male breadwinner, female homemaker" family structure dominant in mid-20th century industrialized economies has given way to dual-earner households, single-parent households, cohabitation, same-sex families, chosen family structures, and multigenerational arrangements.

Marriage rates have declined in most developed countries and are delayed. U.S. median age at first marriage has risen from about 21 for women and 23 for men in 1960 to about 28 and 30 in the 2020s. European countries show similar patterns. East Asian countries show lower marriage rates and dramatically low birth rates (South Korea below 0.8 total fertility, Japan around 1.3, with implications for population aging).

Divorce rates have been relatively stable or declining in many developed countries after peaking in late 20th century. Selection into marriage has shifted — those who marry are increasingly educated and economically prepared, producing more stable marriages on average. Earlier-cohort and less-educated marriages showed higher divorce rates.

Cohabitation is common. Many couples cohabit before marriage; some cohabitate as alternative to marriage. Legal recognition of cohabiting partnerships varies widely (common-law marriage, civil unions, registered partnerships with varying legal consequences).

Same-sex marriage has been legalized in over 30 countries as of 2024, with varying paths and timelines. Acceptance is uneven globally; some countries have moved in opposite directions with criminalization.

Fertility patterns are transforming. Total fertility has fallen below replacement (2.1 children per woman) in most of Europe, East Asia, North America, and Latin America, and is declining elsewhere. Replacement-level fertility persists mainly in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia and the Middle East. Consequences include population aging, workforce dynamics, and debates about pronatalist policies.

Reasons for fertility decline include delayed family formation, higher education, increased women's labor force participation, changing preferences about child-rearing, economic insecurity, housing costs, lack of family-supportive policies, and cultural shifts. Pronatalist policies (baby bonuses, parental leave, childcare) have had modest effects in high-income countries.

## Division of Household Labor

Within households, domestic labor and caregiving remain unequally distributed on gender lines, though the gap has narrowed. Time use studies show women across developed countries doing more housework, childcare, and elder care than men, even in dual-earner households.

The division converges slowly. U.S. data shows men have increased housework from roughly 4 hours weekly in 1965 to around 10 hours currently; women have decreased from around 27 hours to around 17 hours. The remaining gap of roughly 7 hours is substantial but much smaller than mid-20th century.

"Second shift" analyses (Hochschild) and subsequent research document mental load — planning, scheduling, and coordinating family life — that often falls on women even when specific tasks are more evenly shared. The mental load is cognitively and emotionally demanding and less visible than physical tasks.

Parental leave policies have significant effects on household labor division. Dedicated paternal leave that cannot be transferred to mothers (Scandinavian models, some adopted elsewhere) produces increased father involvement that persists beyond the leave period. U.S. lack of mandated paid leave contrasts with most peer countries.

Childcare availability and affordability shape parents' labor market participation. Public childcare systems (France, Scandinavia, Québec) support female labor force participation; U.S. private childcare market produces high cost and spotty availability, contributing to mothers' reduced labor force attachment.

Elder care demands are increasing with population aging. Adult daughters disproportionately provide elder care; implications include labor market interruption and personal wellbeing concerns. Formal long-term care systems vary enormously across countries.

## Gender and Education

Educational attainment by gender has transformed. Women have achieved parity with men in secondary education completion and surpass men in tertiary enrollment and completion in most developed countries. U.S. bachelor's degree completion by age 25-29 is now approximately 41 percent for women versus 34 percent for men.

Field-of-study segregation persists. Women outnumber men in education, psychology, health professions, humanities, and biological sciences. Men predominate in engineering, physics, computer science, and economics. These patterns vary across countries — gender segregation in STEM fields is in fact stronger in more gender-equal countries (the "gender equality paradox"), with multiple interpretations contested.

Secondary education performance differs. Boys on average score higher on math (with gap narrowing and varying by country) and lower on reading across OECD countries. These average differences are small compared with within-gender variation. Educational trajectories are shaped by academic performance, interests, and structural opportunities interacting.

Higher education has reshaped gender trajectories. College-educated women earn substantially more than non-college-educated women, and the gender gap is smaller among college-educated workers. Higher education is associated with later marriage, fewer children, and more egalitarian partnerships on average.

Missing men from higher education has become a topic of policy concern in many developed countries. Causes include gender differences in academic preparation, competing options (trades, military, workforce), and possibly school environments favoring traits more common in girls. Solutions are contested.

## Politics and Public Representation

Women's political representation has expanded over the past century. Women hold about 26 percent of national parliamentary seats globally (IPU data), with substantial variation. Rwanda, Cuba, Nicaragua, Mexico, and UAE are among countries with over 50 percent women in parliament. The U.S. Congress is about 28 percent women. Nordic countries have long led in representation.

Gender quotas — legislated or voluntary party quotas — have driven much of the increase. Most developed countries now have some form of gender-based representation requirement. Effects on policy outcomes are studied with mixed results; presence alone does not ensure women-friendly policy, though representation is related to various policy outcomes.

Women's presence in executive leadership (heads of state or government) has slowly increased but remains modest. Women have led dozens of countries in recent decades; representation continues to be a focus of attention in contexts including the U.S. presidency.

Corporate leadership shows persistent gaps. Women CEO representation in Fortune 500 companies has risen but remains low (around 10 percent in recent years). European quota requirements for corporate boards (Norway 2003 pioneered, followed by many others) have increased board representation; effects on broader corporate leadership and outcomes are studied.

Political attitudes differ by gender in systematic ways. Women in many developed countries lean politically more progressive than men on social issues and some economic issues; men often lean more conservative. These patterns vary across countries and cohorts.

Voting behavior has shown a growing gender gap in many countries. The U.S. has seen substantial gender gaps in presidential elections with women voting more Democratic. Youth gender gaps have widened in some countries (young women shifting leftward, young men trending rightward or stagnating) with active research and debate about causes.

## Gender-Based Violence

Gender-based violence remains a major public health and human rights concern globally. WHO data indicates roughly 1 in 3 women experience physical or sexual intimate partner violence in their lifetime. Most severe intimate partner violence is male-perpetrated against female partners, though violence occurs in all relationship configurations.

Intimate partner violence effects include physical injury, death (homicide), chronic health effects, mental health consequences, children's exposure effects, and economic impacts. Femicide — gender-motivated killing of women — is a significant phenomenon globally, with particularly high rates in parts of Latin America and the Middle East.

Sexual assault and harassment are widely underreported. Estimates of lifetime prevalence of sexual assault against women range from 20-30 percent in many populations. Harassment in workplaces, schools, and public spaces is common. #MeToo movement since 2017 brought unprecedented attention to these issues and produced policy changes in many jurisdictions.

Legal and policy responses include criminalization of domestic violence, protective orders, specialized courts, trauma-informed law enforcement, survivor services (shelters, counseling, legal support), and prevention programs. Effectiveness varies by jurisdiction and implementation.

Men and boys also experience gender-based violence, including intimate partner violence by female partners, violence by other men, and violence within some cultural contexts (hazing, bullying based on perceived gender nonconformity). Male sexual assault is significantly underreported. Comprehensive approaches address violence affecting all genders while recognizing asymmetric patterns.

Prevention programs have accumulated evidence. Bystander intervention training, school-based programs addressing attitudes and norms, economic empowerment combined with gender norm interventions, and perpetrator-focused programs have shown various effects. Primary prevention shifting norms over generations complements response services.

## Masculinities and Men's Issues

Men's studies and research on masculinities has developed substantially. Multiple masculinities exist within any society; they vary by class, race, sexuality, age, and cultural context. Hegemonic masculinity — dominant cultural forms of masculinity — both advantages men broadly and constrains men's behavior and wellbeing.

Male-specific health and wellbeing patterns include higher death rates across most causes except a few, lower help-seeking for mental health, higher rates of substance use disorders, higher workplace fatalities, higher suicide rates (male suicide rates are 3-4x female rates in many countries though female attempts are higher), and specific cancer risks.

Men's shorter average life expectancy (approximately 5 years less than women's in many developed countries, though gap varies) reflects biological, behavioral, and structural factors. Men's underuse of preventive healthcare and mental health services is a public health concern.

Fathering has transformed substantially. Engaged fathering — substantial participation in childcare beyond traditional breadwinning — has increased. Father-involvement effects on children's development are documented. Fathers still face workplace and cultural barriers to engaged parenting in many contexts.

Men's wellbeing concerns include loneliness (particularly in later life with fewer close friendships than women), economic displacement in some sectors (manufacturing decline), educational underperformance in some contexts, and difficulties adapting to changing gender expectations. These issues are taken seriously without dismissing structural advantages that accrue to men in many contexts.

Some advocacy frames men's issues in opposition to women's issues (men's rights movement); others (pro-feminist men's movements, positive masculinity initiatives) frame men's issues as complementary to gender equality broadly. Reasonable debate about how to address men's concerns continues.

## Transgender and Gender Diverse People

Transgender people — those whose gender identity differs from sex assigned at birth — and non-binary people have gained visibility and legal recognition in many countries. Transgender population estimates vary but often fall in the range of 0.5-1.5 percent of adult populations in studies; youth identification as transgender or non-binary has risen substantially in recent years in many studies.

Gender-affirming care — social transition, hormonal therapy (hormone therapy for adults; puberty blockers and hormone therapy for adolescents in some contexts), surgical interventions for adults — is provided under various frameworks. Evidence on outcomes in adults consistently shows improved mental health in most patients who receive desired care; evidence on pediatric care is more contested and scrutinized.

Medical and regulatory frameworks vary significantly. Professional organizations (WPATH, Endocrine Society, Australasian Professional Association for Transgender Health) have guidelines. Countries differ in age criteria, eligibility requirements, insurance coverage, and legal recognition. Some countries have moved to restrict gender-affirming care for minors (Sweden, UK Cass Review, several U.S. states) while others have expanded access.

Legal recognition of gender varies widely. Some countries allow self-declaration of gender (Argentina, Malta, Ireland, parts of Europe); others require medical documentation or surgeries for legal gender change; many countries have no formal recognition of gender change or formally prohibit it.

Transgender people face documented discrimination and elevated risks in multiple domains — employment, housing, healthcare, violence exposure, mental health, and suicidality. Support from family, communities, and institutions is associated with better outcomes.

Political and cultural debates around transgender inclusion — particularly in youth care, sports, prisons, and single-sex spaces — have intensified. Reasonable people disagree on various policy questions while underlying empirical and ethical considerations continue to be studied. Respect for transgender people as persons, and thoughtful analysis of specific policy contexts, are not incompatible.

## Gender, Law, and Policy

Legal frameworks for gender equality have expanded substantially. Most developed countries have prohibition on gender discrimination in employment, equal pay laws, parental leave provisions, protections against sexual harassment, and similar frameworks. Enforcement quality varies.

CEDAW (Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women), adopted 1979, is a major international framework. Most UN member states have ratified. Implementation is monitored by CEDAW Committee. Some countries have reservations limiting CEDAW obligations.

Reproductive rights have been significant gender-policy areas. Legal access to contraception is nearly universal among developed countries but varies in lower-income countries and among cultural contexts. Legal access to abortion varies enormously — expansive laws in much of Europe, Canada, and parts of Asia and Latin America; restrictive laws in many other countries; U.S. has seen substantial contraction since Dobbs decision in 2022 removing federal constitutional protection.

Family law — marriage, divorce, custody, inheritance — varies across legal systems. Feminist legal analysis has documented gendered effects in family law; reform in many jurisdictions has addressed explicit gender inequities, though implicit effects remain.

Gender mainstreaming — integrating gender analysis into all policy areas — has been adopted formally by EU, UN agencies, and many national governments. Effectiveness varies by implementation seriousness.

## Gender Norms and Culture

Cultural representations of gender are both reflective and constitutive. Media, advertising, entertainment, sports, education materials, and popular culture all transmit and shape gender expectations. Media literacy is increasingly recognized as part of civic education.

Men and masculinity in media have evolved slowly. Women's representations have diversified substantially since late 20th century, though persistent patterns remain. Intersectional representations — particularly of women of color, LGBTQ+ characters, disabled characters — have expanded but remain limited.

Gender norms vary across cultures but overlap substantially. Values studies (World Values Survey, Pew surveys) show both cultural variation and shared patterns. Traditional gender norms are strongest in many parts of Middle East, North Africa, and parts of sub-Saharan Africa; most egalitarian in Nordic countries. East Asian, South Asian, Latin American, and European countries fall between these poles with substantial within-region variation.

Generational change is significant. Younger cohorts in most countries endorse more egalitarian gender attitudes than older cohorts. Some polarization has emerged recently with young men and women diverging in views in some surveys, particularly visible in South Korea, U.S., and parts of Europe.

Cultural backlash against gender progress is visible in various contexts. Right-wing populist movements in multiple countries have explicitly opposed feminist and LGBTQ+ rights agendas. Religious conservative movements have engaged in global advocacy. Counter-movements defend and extend gender equality.

## Gender and Work Futures

Changing technologies and economies shape gender outcomes. Automation affects male-dominated and female-dominated occupations differently; service sector growth may favor female-dominated employment; AI may disrupt many white-collar occupations including those where women have made advances.

The rise of remote work during and after COVID-19 has gendered implications. Women's remote work participation has been higher than men's in most developed countries. Effects on career advancement, work-life integration, and geographic mobility are being studied; early evidence suggests mixed effects for women's careers.

Care economy recognition — paid and unpaid — is a growing focus. Universal care policy frameworks (childcare, elder care, paid leave) are advocated as both economic infrastructure and gender equality investments. U.S. has lagged peer countries substantially; legislative proposals have faced political obstacles.

Global economic participation of women continues expanding in many emerging economies; gender gaps are closing in education and some labor market dimensions. Progress is uneven; some countries have seen setbacks (Afghanistan under Taliban, Iran protests) and others sustained advance.

## Why This Level Matters

Gender is not a peripheral concern but an organizing principle of societies. Its current transformations matter because:

- **Economic outcomes depend on gendered structures**: Labor markets, care provision, and productivity all depend on how gender organizes work
- **Family and demographic futures are being reshaped**: Fertility, household formation, caregiving patterns shape aging societies
- **Political trajectories reflect gender dynamics**: Voting, representation, and policy preferences all have gendered patterns
- **Violence prevention is urgent**: Gender-based violence remains a massive public health and human rights problem
- **Individual lives are shaped by gender**: Opportunities, constraints, health, wellbeing all have gendered patterns
- **Contested questions require evidence**: Reasonable people disagree on specific policies; better evidence and thoughtful deliberation matter

Studying gender in society rigorously means holding together empirical research, normative considerations, and recognition of legitimate disagreement. Neither dismissing gender as unimportant nor reducing all analysis to gender captures the complex reality.
