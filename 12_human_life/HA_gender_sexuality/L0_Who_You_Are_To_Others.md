## The Form You Fill Out

A doctor's form asks your sex. A dating app asks your gender. A new acquaintance assumes things about your sexuality from what they can see. These are three separate questions, related but not identical. You have been answering versions of them your entire life, usually without thinking about it.

For most people, the answers are obvious and consistent. You know what body you have. You know how you want to be addressed. You know who you are attracted to. The answers line up in the usual way and you move on with your life.

For some people, the answers are more complicated. This is not a 21st-century invention. It is an observation that has been made across cultures and centuries, with varying levels of social acceptance.

## The Biology That Mostly Works One Way

In most humans, at conception, you get either two X chromosomes (XX, typical female development) or an X and a Y (XY, typical male development). The Y chromosome carries the SRY gene, which, when expressed, triggers the development of testes rather than ovaries. Testes produce testosterone. Testosterone drives male-typical anatomical development. Absence of testosterone results in female-typical development.

This is the clean textbook version, and it describes a large majority of cases. It is also not the whole picture. A small but nonzero percentage of the population (estimates range from ~0.018% to ~1.7%, depending on what conditions are counted) has some form of difference of sex development (DSD or intersex condition). The categories are:

- Chromosomal variation (e.g., XXY Klinefelter syndrome, XO Turner syndrome, XYY, mosaicism)
- Hormonal variation (e.g., complete androgen insensitivity syndrome, congenital adrenal hyperplasia)
- Gonadal or anatomical variation (including true hermaphroditism, which is rare)

These are not rhetorical examples. They are documented medical realities that predate any modern political conversation. They are also not always visible — some are not detected until puberty or infertility workup.

The biology is mostly binary. It is not universally binary.

## The Gender That Lives in How You Move Through the World

Gender is not the same as biological sex, though for most people the two are aligned. Gender is the felt sense of who you are — man, woman, or something else — and how you present that to others.

For the vast majority of people (approximately 99%+, depending on survey methodology), gender identity aligns with biological sex. This is called "cisgender." For some people (around 0.5-1%, again with methodological variation), it does not — this is called "transgender" or "nonbinary" depending on how it is described.

What causes gender identity to form one way or the other is not fully understood. There is some evidence of prenatal hormonal influence, some evidence of genetic contribution, some contribution from social environment. None of these fully explain variance. The honest scientific answer as of the mid-2020s is "partly biological, partly developmental, not yet fully mapped."

This is an area where Evidence Tier discipline matters. The biology of intersex conditions is Textbook. The claim that gender identity is always and entirely biologically fixed or always and entirely socially constructed are both Speculative. The real picture is probably something in between and will need more research to resolve.

## The Attraction You Didn't Choose

Sexual orientation — who you are sexually and/or romantically attracted to — is another dimension. For most humans, this is set relatively early in development (before age 10, by most self-report data), is consistent across the lifespan, and is not consciously chosen.

About 90-95% of the population reports exclusive or predominant attraction to the other sex. About 2-5% reports exclusive or predominant attraction to the same sex. The rest report various forms of bisexuality or attraction patterns.

These numbers have been remarkably consistent across large representative surveys in different countries over the past 50 years, with some upward drift in younger cohorts reporting non-heterosexual attraction — which could mean actual behavioral change, better survey methodology, reduced stigma in reporting, or all three.

Same-sex behavior has been observed in every culture ever surveyed and in hundreds of animal species. It is not a modern invention. Social acceptance of it varies enormously.

## The Family Shape

How humans form families varies. The stereotyped modern Western family — a lifelong monogamous married couple raising their biological children — is one pattern among many humans have practiced and currently practice. Historically and cross-culturally, arrangements include serial monogamy, polygyny, polyandry (rare), extended-family households, single parenting, adoption, chosen family, and more.

The research on child outcomes across family structures is messier than partisans on any side want to admit. What matters most, across studies, is stability, safety, and responsive caregiving — which can be delivered by many configurations of adults.

## The Biology You Don't Control

Puberty happens to you. Menstruation happens to you. Ejaculation happens to you. Menopause happens to you. Fertility declines happen to you. Erectile function and arousal change with age.

The body does these things on its own schedule, influenced by health, genetics, and environment. You are not choosing them. You are navigating them.

Modern medicine has given us some control knobs: hormonal contraception, IVF, hormone replacement therapy, gender-affirming medical care, puberty blockers for specific clinical situations. Each of these is a tool with a well-studied evidence base and a set of tradeoffs. Different tools have different bodies of evidence. The book will engage the evidence honestly, including the places where it is still being built.

## The Consent That Changes Everything

Sexual behavior between people requires consent. This seems obvious. It has not always been treated as obvious. Marital rape was not criminalized in most Western countries until the 1970s-90s. Coercion and power differentials continue to complicate consent in practice.

The last 30 years have seen a significant cultural shift toward affirmative consent — not just "no means no" but "yes means yes." This is a better model and still an imperfect one. Consent requires conditions (not under coercion, not under the influence beyond capacity, not in a power imbalance that precludes real choice). The ethics and the law are still working through this.

## The First Lesson

Sex, gender, and sexuality are three separate things that happen to mostly line up in most people. When they line up the expected way, life is simpler. When they don't, life is more complicated in ways that deserve serious engagement, not dismissal.

This book will try to describe what is known with the confidence it deserves, and what is contested with the honesty it deserves, and refuse to borrow the tone of either the traditionalist dismissal or the ideological certainty currently competing in public discourse. The evidence, carefully read, is more interesting than either position.
