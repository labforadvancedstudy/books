## Beyond Identity: Attraction and Partnership

L2 covered gender identity — who someone is. L3 covers sexuality — who they are attracted to, how they form intimate bonds, and how they reproduce. Sexuality and gender are distinct. Sexual orientation describes patterns of attraction (to same sex, opposite sex, both, or none); gender identity describes internal sense of being male, female, or other. Most combinations occur: gay men, lesbian women, heterosexual men, heterosexual women are the largest categories; bisexual, asexual, and other orientations also represented across all gender identities.

This level explores the biology, psychology, and sociology of attraction, relationships, and reproduction — all central to human experience and much researched, but also among the topics where cultural and policy views vary widely.

## Sexual Orientation

**Sexual orientation** refers to patterns of emotional, romantic, and sexual attraction. Primary categories:
- **Heterosexual (straight)**: attracted primarily to people of other gender.
- **Homosexual (gay, lesbian)**: attracted primarily to people of same gender.
- **Bisexual**: attracted to both/multiple genders.
- **Asexual**: little or no sexual attraction to others (~1-2% of population).
- **Pansexual**: attraction not restricted by gender.
- **Other**: more fine-grained identities including demisexual, queer, questioning.

**Prevalence** (varying by definition and survey methodology):
- **Same-sex attraction**: 5-15% of adults reported in large surveys.
- **Same-sex behavior**: narrower, ~4-8%.
- **Lesbian/gay identification**: ~2-4% consistently.
- **Bisexual identification**: ~2-5%, higher in younger cohorts.
- **LGBTQ+ identification overall** in Gen Z: ~20%+ in recent US surveys, with bisexual predominating.

Rising identification over generations likely reflects both reduced stigma (people more willing to identify) and possibly some genuine increase in behavior.

**Biological basis**:
- **Heritability**: twin studies suggest 30-50% of variance in sexual orientation is genetic. Not a single gene; polygenic.
- **Prenatal hormones**: theorized role; evidence suggestive but not conclusive.
- **Fraternal birth order effect**: each older brother slightly increases odds of male homosexuality; suggested maternal immune response mechanism.
- **Neuroanatomy**: some structural differences in brains of gay vs. straight men, lesbian vs. straight women noted in research, though significance debated.
- **Not a choice**: overwhelming evidence that adult sexual orientation is not a choice and not changeable by intention or therapy ("conversion therapy" is considered harmful pseudoscience by all major mental health organizations).

**Development**: most people report awareness of attractions in early adolescence, with LGBTQ+ people often first becoming aware of their orientation in early-to-mid teens. Coming out is a process, not a moment; varies by safety of environment.

## Attraction and Desire

What drives sexual attraction?

**Evolutionary perspectives**: attraction patterns often interpreted as signals of fertility and health. Cross-cultural findings on physical attractiveness include facial symmetry, waist-to-hip ratio preferences (modestly consistent but culturally variable), clear skin, health indicators.

**Sociocultural perspectives**: attraction shaped by exposure, cultural norms, media representations. "Beauty standards" vary across cultures and time.

**Learning and familiarity**: mere exposure increases attraction. Proximity and repeated contact build attraction in many cases.

**Individual variation**: personality, shared values, humor, intelligence, kindness — all matter for relationships, sometimes more than initial physical attraction.

**Sexual response cycle** (Masters and Johnson): excitement, plateau, orgasm, resolution. Refinements by Kaplan (adding desire) and Basson (circular rather than linear model, particularly relevant for many women). Sexual desire varies enormously within and between individuals; context matters more than single biological drive.

## Intimate Relationships

Humans form pair bonds of varying duration in nearly every culture. Forms vary:

**Monogamous marriage**: socially normative in most industrialized societies. Sexual and romantic exclusivity expected (violated often in practice).

**Serial monogamy**: sequential exclusive relationships — common pattern in contemporary West.

**Polygyny**: one husband, multiple wives — historically common, persists in some cultures especially in parts of Africa, Middle East, Central Asia.

**Polyandry**: one wife, multiple husbands — rare; some Himalayan societies.

**Polyamory / consensual non-monogamy**: multiple relationships with knowledge and consent of all parties. Minority practice in Western societies, more openly discussed since 2000s.

**Cohabitation without marriage**: increasingly common, especially in Northern Europe and US.

**Arranged marriages**: still common in South Asia, parts of Africa, Middle East; less so in East Asia and West. Outcome data is mixed — arranged marriages are not worse on average for satisfaction, though the mechanisms are different.

**Same-sex marriage and partnership**: legally recognized in ~30+ countries since Netherlands' 2001 legalization; fastest-expanding legal change in social policy.

## What Makes Relationships Work

Decades of research (Gottman, Finkel, others) on relationship quality and stability:

**Positive predictors**:
- **Positive-to-negative interaction ratio**: ~5:1 during conflict in stable couples.
- **Emotional attunement**: attending to partner's bids for connection.
- **Repair attempts**: de-escalation after conflict.
- **Shared meaning**: values, goals, life vision alignment.
- **Communication skills**: listening, expressing needs clearly.
- **Commitment**: investment in the relationship as identity.

**Negative predictors ("Four Horsemen" per Gottman)**:
- **Criticism**: attacking character rather than behavior.
- **Contempt**: sneering, eye-rolling — most predictive of divorce.
- **Defensiveness**: deflecting rather than owning.
- **Stonewalling**: shutting down, withdrawing.

**Other factors**:
- **Similarity**: education, values, religion associated with stability; opposites don't reliably attract.
- **Financial stress**: major predictor of conflict and dissolution.
- **Extended family relationships**: supportive in-laws help; toxic ones strain.
- **Children**: reduce marital satisfaction for most couples, especially after first child; rebound somewhat after children leave.
- **Sexual compatibility**: desired frequency, preferences, emotional connection around sex all matter.

## Divorce and Separation

Divorce rates:
- **US**: ~40-50% of first marriages end in divorce; ~60% of second marriages. Rate declining since 1980 peak, partly due to declining marriage rate overall.
- **Europe**: highly variable — Russia 70%+, Belgium ~60%, Italy much lower. Trend rising in Catholic-majority countries.
- **Japan**: rising from very low base.
- **Rapid divorce-rate rise** in some Middle Eastern and Asian countries as women's economic independence grows and divorce is destigmatized.

**Causes**: incompatibility, infidelity, financial issues, substance abuse, domestic violence, changed life goals. "Growing apart" commonly cited.

**Effects on adults**: short-term distress common; long-term outcomes mixed. Most rebuild well within 2-5 years.

**Effects on children**: substantial short-term impact; long-term effects smaller than often assumed, modulated by quality of co-parenting and resources. Amato's meta-analyses: modest but real effects on academic and emotional outcomes.

## Sexual Behavior

What people do sexually is studied but under-reported honestly:

**Frequency**: varies enormously. US adults median ~1 time per week to less; declining over recent decades (especially young adults). Japan and Korea report notably low frequencies.

**Coital frequency decline**: data from US General Social Survey and others show young adults are having less sex than previous generations at same ages. Debates about causes — smartphones, dating app dynamics, relationship structure, financial stress, mental health.

**Masturbation**: near-universal among men, majority of women. Not associated with relationship problems when not compulsive.

**Pornography**: widely used; ~85%+ of men, ~35%+ of women in varying surveys. Effects debated — concerns about relationship effects, body image, expectations; research evidence mixed and varies by use patterns.

**Sexual dysfunction**: erectile dysfunction, premature ejaculation, anorgasmia, low desire, painful sex. Affects substantial minority of adults. Causes physical, psychological, relational. Treatments include medication (PDE5 inhibitors for ED), therapy, couple counseling.

## Reproduction Biology

Human reproductive biology:

**Female fertility**: peaks mid-20s, significant decline after ~35, steeper after ~38-40. Menopause average age ~51. Fertility ends well before menopause typically.

**Male fertility**: no abrupt menopause; gradual declines in semen quality and genetic integrity from ~40s. Father's age increases some risks for offspring (schizophrenia, autism, miscarriage).

**Conception**: per-cycle probability for healthy young couples ~20-25%. Falls with age. After 12 months trying without success, infertility evaluation indicated.

**Infertility**: affects ~10-15% of couples. Causes divided roughly equally among female factors (ovulation, tubal, uterine), male factors (sperm), and combined/unexplained.

**Assisted reproductive technologies (ART)**:
- **Intrauterine insemination (IUI)**: simpler, lower success per cycle.
- **In vitro fertilization (IVF)**: fertilization outside body, embryo transfer. Since Louise Brown 1978; millions of births since. Per-cycle live birth rates ~30-40% for younger women, lower for older.
- **ICSI**: direct injection of sperm; for male-factor infertility.
- **Egg and embryo freezing**: increasingly used; allows delay of pregnancy.
- **Donor eggs, sperm, embryos**: enable pregnancy for some otherwise infertile situations.
- **Gestational surrogacy**: ethically and legally complex; varies by jurisdiction.

## Pregnancy and Birth

Pregnancy average 40 weeks from last menstrual period. Divided into trimesters:

**First trimester**: organ formation (most critical period for environmental exposures); most miscarriages occur here (~15-20% of clinical pregnancies).

**Second trimester**: growth; quickening (felt movement); detailed ultrasound screenings.

**Third trimester**: growth continues; preparation for birth; risk of preterm labor.

**Prenatal care**: regular visits, screening for gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, fetal growth; blood tests, ultrasounds, genetic testing (NIPT for chromosomal, amniocentesis if indicated).

**Labor and delivery**: spontaneous onset after ~37-42 weeks. Stages: dilation, delivery of baby, delivery of placenta. Medical pain management (epidurals), surgical delivery (C-section, now ~30%+ of US births), interventions for complications.

**Postpartum**: recovery, breastfeeding establishment (or not), maternal-infant bonding, postpartum depression risk (~10-15%).

**Maternal mortality** (deaths from pregnancy-related causes): ~200/100,000 globally; ~10/100,000 in wealthy countries; up to 500+/100,000 in high-risk settings. US has anomalously high rate for income level (~20/100,000), with major Black-white disparity.

## Contraception and Abortion

Contraception options:
- **Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs)**: IUDs (hormonal or copper), implants. Most effective reversible methods (99%+).
- **Hormonal**: pills, patches, rings, injections. Effective with use.
- **Barrier**: condoms (also STI protection), diaphragms. Lower effectiveness but on-demand.
- **Natural family planning**: tracking fertility. Lower effectiveness even at best.
- **Emergency contraception**: post-intercourse pills (up to 72-120 hours).
- **Sterilization**: tubal ligation, vasectomy. Highly effective, intended permanent.

Access varies: LARCs and contraception widely available in most wealthy countries; barriers remain in many. Developing countries show rising use but unmet need for millions.

**Abortion**: legal status varies enormously — liberally available in much of Europe, East Asia, parts of Latin America (changing rapidly); heavily restricted or banned in some US states since 2022 Dobbs decision, much of Latin America (despite recent liberalizations in some), Middle East, parts of Africa. Evidence consistent: restrictions reduce safe abortions but not total abortions; unsafe abortions cause ~50,000+ maternal deaths annually globally. Abortion is a major political and ethical issue in many societies; positions correlate strongly with religious, political, and cultural views. Factual point: most abortions globally are early-pregnancy (first trimester); later-pregnancy abortions are rare and almost always involve severe fetal anomaly or maternal health threat.

## Sexual Health

**Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)**:
- **Chlamydia, gonorrhea**: curable bacterial infections; rising incidence in recent years.
- **Syphilis**: resurging; major concern in some populations.
- **HPV**: very common; some strains cause cervical and other cancers; vaccines highly effective (preventing 90%+ of cases when given before exposure).
- **HIV**: ~1 million new infections/yr globally; treatable if accessed. Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) highly effective for prevention.
- **Herpes**: very common; manageable, not curable.

Testing, prevention (condoms, PrEP, vaccination), and treatment are public health priorities; adherence varies.

## Sexual Violence and Consent

**Sexual violence**: ~1 in 3 women experiences physical or sexual violence in lifetime globally (WHO); male victimization less reported but also substantial. Intimate partner violence and sexual assault by acquaintances more common than stranger assault.

**Consent**: active, ongoing, enthusiastic agreement is the standard framing in contemporary education. Cannot be given while incapacitated; cannot be coerced. Legal definitions vary by jurisdiction.

Responses include criminal law, workplace policies, education, cultural shifts like #MeToo (2017+). Implementation and outcomes uneven.

## Why This Level Matters

Sexuality and relationships are central to human well-being — perhaps the central personal domains across adult life. They are also the domains most shaped by cultural rules, legal frameworks, and technology (contraception, IVF, dating apps, pornography, etc.). Policy debates on abortion, reproductive technology, age of consent, same-sex relationships, gender-based violence, and family structure all involve empirical facts overlaid with deeply held values.

Understanding the empirical landscape — what's common, what's biologically constrained, what varies culturally, what the evidence says about interventions — is necessary for thoughtful personal decisions and for civic engagement on these issues. The facts alone don't resolve the value questions, but ignorance guarantees poor answers to both.
