*Chromosomes, gonads, hormones, secondary characteristics*

## Sex Is Not a Single Switch

In mammals, biological sex is determined by a cascade of development that begins with chromosomes, propagates through gonads and hormones, shapes the reproductive tract, produces secondary sexual characteristics at puberty, and continues to affect physiology throughout life.

These steps usually (but not always) line up coherently, producing the two common configurations:

- **Typical female**: XX chromosomes, ovaries, uterus and vagina, estrogen-dominant endocrinology in adulthood, capable of menstruation, pregnancy, lactation.
- **Typical male**: XY chromosomes, testes, prostate and penis, testosterone-dominant endocrinology in adulthood, capable of producing sperm.

Most humans fall into one of these two configurations. The developmental machinery has enough variability that a small but non-trivial minority — perhaps 0.5–1.7% by some estimates, though the upper bound depends on which conditions are included — show variation at one or more levels. These are **intersex conditions** (also called differences of sex development, DSDs) and are addressed below.

## Chromosomes

Human somatic cells have 46 chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes plus two sex chromosomes. The sex chromosomes are X or Y. The typical combinations:

- **XX**: typical female chromosomal sex
- **XY**: typical male chromosomal sex
- **X** alone (Turner syndrome, XO): atypical female presentation with short stature, infertility, variable other features
- **XXY** (Klinefelter syndrome): typical male external appearance but reduced testosterone, infertility, gynecomastia variable
- **XYY**: typical male appearance, minor developmental variation in some
- **XXX** (triple X): typical female appearance, often minimal phenotype
- **46,XX/46,XY chimerism or mosaicism**: two cell lines present, sometimes with mixed reproductive features

The Y chromosome carries the **SRY** gene, which in typical development triggers the bipotential embryonic gonad to become a testis rather than an ovary around week 7 of gestation. SRY mutations can produce XY females (absent or nonfunctional SRY) or XX males (SRY translocated to an X chromosome).

These are all rare but real. A chromosomal picture alone is not sufficient to determine anatomical sex.

## Gonads

Embryonic gonads are bipotential — they can develop into either testes or ovaries depending on genetic signals. With SRY, they form testes by week 8 and begin producing testosterone and anti-Müllerian hormone. Without SRY, they default to ovaries.

The hormones then shape the **internal reproductive tract**:

- **Testosterone** drives development of the Wolffian duct system — epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles.
- **Anti-Müllerian hormone** suppresses the Müllerian duct system in typical male development.
- In typical female development, neither hormone is present at the critical time, so the Müllerian ducts develop into fallopian tubes, uterus, and upper vagina, and the Wolffian ducts regress.

**External genitalia** develop from the same bipotential structures:

- Genital tubercle → clitoris (female) or penis (male, larger under testosterone's influence)
- Urethral folds → labia minora (female) or fused along the penis (male)
- Labioscrotal swellings → labia majora (female) or fused scrotum (male)

If testosterone is absent, reduced, or the tissue cannot respond to it (androgen insensitivity syndrome, 5α-reductase deficiency), external appearance can be feminine or ambiguous despite XY chromosomes and internal testes. Conversely, conditions that expose a female (XX) fetus to excess androgens (such as congenital adrenal hyperplasia, CAH) can produce masculinized external genitalia.

These are the conditions that produce "ambiguous genitalia" at birth. Modern medical practice is more cautious about early surgical intervention than a generation ago — many societies and professional bodies now counsel delaying cosmetic genital surgery until the individual can participate in decisions, absent urgent medical indication.

## Hormones in Adulthood

At puberty, the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis activates, producing sustained high levels of sex hormones:

- **Testosterone** in typical males: 300–1,000 ng/dL. Drives muscle mass, bone density, body hair, voice deepening, libido, and ongoing sperm production.
- **Estrogen and progesterone** in typical females: cycling levels over the menstrual cycle. Drive breast development, fat distribution, pubic hair, and reproductive cycling.

Both sexes produce both hormone classes at different levels. Typical male levels of estradiol are low but functionally important (for bone and brain). Typical female testosterone is roughly 1/10 that of typical males and is important for libido and energy.

**Puberty** typically begins around age 8–13 in girls, 9–14 in boys. The first-observable sign is breast budding in girls and testicular enlargement in boys. The full sequence takes 3–5 years. Timing of puberty is affected by genetics, nutrition, body fat, environmental factors; mean age of first menstruation (menarche) has been declining in many industrialized countries over decades, likely due to better nutrition and earlier body fat accumulation.

## Secondary Sexual Characteristics

Secondary sexual characteristics emerge at puberty and produce much of the visible dimorphism of adult humans:

- **Body composition**: adult males average more muscle mass, lower body fat, higher bone density, and a shifted fat distribution toward the abdomen.
- **Height**: adult males average ~13 cm taller than females in most populations, mostly due to longer pubertal growth.
- **Voice**: the larynx enlarges more in males, dropping the voice by about an octave.
- **Facial and body hair**: androgen-driven; much greater in males.
- **Breast development**: females only in typical development.

These are averages. Individual variation is large. The distributions of height, muscle mass, and endurance in males and females overlap substantially.

**Athletic performance**: across nearly all elite athletic categories, adult post-pubertal males have higher performance than females by 5–30% depending on the sport. The gap is large enough that professional and collegiate sports are typically sex-segregated. The biology is unambiguous: testosterone during development builds larger, more muscular bodies with higher oxygen-carrying capacity. Whether and how trans women who transitioned post-puberty should compete in women's sports is contested and the subject of ongoing sport-by-sport policy development.

## Intersex Conditions

Intersex / DSD covers a heterogeneous group:

- **Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS)**: XY, testes internal, androgen receptors nonfunctional, external appearance and often identity female.
- **Partial androgen insensitivity syndrome**: variable degree of masculinization.
- **5α-reductase deficiency**: XY, born with ambiguous or feminine external genitalia, sometimes virilizing at puberty.
- **Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH)**: XX, exposed to excess adrenal androgens in utero; various degrees of genital masculinization; requires lifelong steroid treatment.
- **Klinefelter, Turner, and other chromosomal variations**: as above.
- **Ovotesticular DSD**: both ovarian and testicular tissue present.
- **Swyer syndrome**: XY with nonfunctional SRY, female external and internal phenotype, streak gonads.

Prevalence estimates vary widely depending on which conditions are included. The restrictive definition (clearly ambiguous genitalia at birth) is about 0.02%. The broader definition (any chromosomal, gonadal, or anatomical variation from typical male or female) reaches 1.7% in some estimates that include common conditions like late-onset CAH.

Intersex conditions are a real and specific medical category. They do not erase the overwhelming statistical bimodality of biological sex but are important both clinically and for anyone reasoning about the biology of sex with more than a cartoon model.

## Sex Differences Beyond Reproduction

Sex has physiological effects beyond reproduction:

- **Metabolism**: rates of drug metabolism, alcohol tolerance, body water fraction differ.
- **Immune function**: females have stronger immune responses on average — better at clearing infections, more prone to autoimmune diseases.
- **Cardiovascular disease**: different risk profiles and presentation; heart attack symptoms in women often differ from the classic "crushing chest pain" pattern and were historically underrecognized.
- **Longevity**: females live longer than males in nearly every human population by ~5 years.
- **Some diseases**: multiple sclerosis and lupus are much more common in females; Parkinson's and autism spectrum conditions more in males.
- **Drug response**: some drugs have different efficacy or toxicity profiles by sex. Historically medical research underrepresented females, leading to dosing recommendations that sometimes didn't fit.

Sex as a biological variable is increasingly incorporated into medical research and clinical practice. This is uncontroversial scientifically.

## What This Chapter Does Not Cover

This chapter deliberately separates biological sex (the subject here) from **gender identity** (a person's internal sense of their gender) and **gender expression** (how a person presents socially). Those are distinct concepts handled at L2 and L3. Biological sex is not gender. Gender is not biological sex. The relationship between them — including in trans, nonbinary, and culturally variable gender systems — is real, complex, and important, but is not resolved by the biology of sex development alone.

## Why This Level Matters

Biological sex is one of the most well-understood aspects of human biology, with a rigorous literature on its molecular, chromosomal, gonadal, hormonal, and anatomical development. It is also an area where public discussion routinely mixes biological, psychological, and cultural layers, producing confusion on all sides.

The clear biology, stated plainly, is: human development usually produces two sexes through a coherent developmental cascade; some people have variations in that cascade; the variations are real but rare; and biological sex has real consequences for medicine, physiology, and physical performance that are not culturally constructed.
