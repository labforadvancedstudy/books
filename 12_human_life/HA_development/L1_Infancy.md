# Level 1: Infancy
*The first two years: brain, attachment, language*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Fastest Change You Will Ever See

A newborn human is, by mammalian standards, strikingly helpless. A newborn foal can stand within an hour and run within a day. A newborn human can barely lift its head.

This is not a defect. It is a tradeoff. The human brain at birth is about 25% of adult size and triples in volume over the first two years. That growth continues (slower) until the early 20s. Being born before the brain is mostly developed means brains can adapt to the specific environment and language the child encounters, at the cost of years of dependency.

The first two years — infancy — are the densest period of change in a human lifespan. By age two, a child:

- Has grown from ~3.5 kg to ~12 kg (more than tripled body weight).
- Went from unable to lift the head to walking, climbing, and running.
- Went from no words to using sentences.
- Has formed specific attachment relationships with caregivers.
- Has developed recognition memory, basic object permanence, early emotional regulation.
- Has a brain with roughly as many synapses per neuron as it will ever have, before pruning begins.

Most of this unfolds on a remarkably reliable schedule, with variation around the mean but consistent across cultures for typical children.

## The Brain

At birth, the newborn brain has about 86 billion neurons (same as adult) but relatively few synapses. Over the first two years, synaptogenesis explodes — peak synaptic density is reached around age 2–3, higher than adult levels.

Then **pruning** begins. Synapses that are actively used stabilize; those that aren't are eliminated. The brain that emerges from pruning is more specialized and efficient than the maximally-connected brain of a toddler. Pruning continues through adolescence.

Myelination — the insulation of nerve fibers that allows faster signaling — progresses over years, starting in brainstem and spinal cord at birth and reaching the prefrontal cortex in the mid-20s. This is one reason executive function matures so late.

Sensitive periods are windows during which the brain is especially receptive to certain inputs. Binocular vision requires coordinated input from both eyes in the first year or two; a child with untreated strabismus or cataracts during this window may never develop normal depth perception even after correction. Language acquisition is easier before puberty; bilingualism is much easier before about age 7 than after.

Sensitive periods are not absolute. Plasticity continues throughout life. But the bar is lower and the mechanism more dynamic during infancy.

## Motor Development

Motor milestones unfold on a well-characterized timeline with substantial individual variation:

- **2–4 months**: head control, rolling over.
- **6–8 months**: sitting unsupported, reaching for objects, transferring between hands.
- **8–10 months**: crawling, pincer grasp, pulling to stand.
- **12–15 months**: first independent steps, then walking.
- **18–24 months**: walking steadily, climbing, running, beginning to stack blocks.

These milestones are the outcome of maturation of the corticospinal tract, cerebellum, and sensorimotor coordination, combined with practice in real environments. A child raised where floors are unsafe for crawling may skip crawling and go straight to walking. Cultural practices around swaddling and carrying affect early motor timing without affecting long-term outcomes.

Concerning signs (each doctor-evaluable): not rolling by 6 months, not sitting by 9 months, not walking by 18 months, persistent asymmetry in movement (one side strong, the other weak). These may indicate cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy, or other conditions requiring intervention.

## Attachment

The attachment relationship is the single most important psychosocial feature of infancy. The infant forms a specific, durable bond with one or a few primary caregivers — usually the mother, but capable of forming with any consistent, responsive caregiver.

Mary Ainsworth's **Strange Situation** (1978) identified patterns of attachment by observing 12-month-olds briefly separated from and reunited with their caregivers. Four main patterns emerged:

- **Secure** (~60% of middle-class Western samples): distressed by separation, comforted by reunion. Uses the caregiver as a "safe base" for exploration.
- **Insecure-avoidant**: minimal distress at separation, avoids contact at reunion.
- **Insecure-ambivalent/resistant**: extreme distress at separation; mixed seeking and resistance at reunion.
- **Disorganized**: contradictory or confused behaviors. Associated with abusive or severely inconsistent caregiving.

Secure attachment correlates with better social, emotional, and cognitive outcomes in later childhood. Insecure attachment is not destiny — many insecurely attached children grow into well-adjusted adults — but the pattern is real and developmentally significant.

The primary driver of secure attachment is **sensitive responsiveness** by the caregiver: noticing the infant's signals, interpreting them accurately, responding promptly and appropriately. This sounds obvious but is surprisingly hard under stress, depression, or adversity.

## Language Acquisition

Language development follows a remarkable schedule:

- **Birth**: preference for the mother's voice (learned in utero) and for the rhythm of the native language.
- **2–4 months**: cooing, vowel-like sounds.
- **6 months**: babbling with consonant sounds (ba-ba, ma-ma).
- **~12 months**: first clearly-articulated words.
- **18 months**: vocabulary spurt — words acquired at rapidly increasing rates.
- **~24 months**: two-word phrases ("more milk", "daddy go").
- **3–4 years**: basic grammar, complex sentences, questions.
- **5–6 years**: essentially fluent native-speaker mastery of core grammar and pragmatics.

Language is acquired by exposure, not by explicit teaching. Children do not need to be corrected on grammar to learn grammar — they extract the regularities of their native language from hearing it. The data they need is "input" (language addressed to them or overheard), not instruction.

The amount of high-quality input matters. Hart and Risley's (1995) "30-million word gap" study — which found large differences in the amount of speech addressed to children in different socioeconomic groups — has been debated methodologically, but the qualitative finding that more, richer linguistic input produces more vocabulary and faster language development has held up.

Multilingual exposure is not confusing. Infants exposed to two languages from birth acquire both, with a possibly small delay in each individual vocabulary but with clear bilingual fluency and some cognitive advantages in executive function.

## Social and Emotional Development

Infants develop social and emotional capacity in a specific order:

- **Newborn**: reflexive smiling, crying for physiological needs, orienting to faces and voices.
- **2–3 months**: social smiling, eye contact, responsive engagement.
- **6 months**: recognizing familiar vs. unfamiliar people; beginnings of stranger anxiety.
- **8–10 months**: separation anxiety (distress when primary caregiver leaves).
- **12–18 months**: social referencing (looking at caregiver's face to decide whether a new thing is safe); joint attention (following someone's gaze to share focus on an object).
- **18–24 months**: mirror self-recognition (the rouge test — passes around 18 months on average, indicating an emerging self-concept); beginnings of empathy; pretend play.

**Theory of mind** — understanding that others have beliefs and intentions different from one's own — emerges later, typically around age 4, though earlier precursors appear in infancy.

The emotional regulation infrastructure is developing throughout these years. Infants rely on caregiver co-regulation — soothing, being held, having emotions named and responded to — to develop their own regulatory capacity later. This is the mechanism by which attachment affects emotional outcomes.

## Physical Health

Infancy is a period of high vulnerability to specific medical conditions:

- **Prematurity**: birth before 37 weeks gestation. Major risk factor for developmental delays, cerebral palsy, and mortality. Neonatal intensive care has dramatically improved survival of extreme preterm infants.
- **Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS)**: unexplained death during sleep, peaking at 2–4 months. Rates dropped by ~70% with the "Back to Sleep" campaign (infants sleeping on their backs, on firm mattresses, without loose bedding).
- **Infectious disease**: vaccines are scheduled in infancy for good reason — the immune system is maturing and infections are more dangerous. Pertussis, meningitis, polio, measles all disproportionately harm infants.
- **Failure to thrive**: growth significantly below expected norms, usually from inadequate nutrition or caregiving.
- **Childhood accidents**: leading cause of death in children once infancy mortality drops. Prevention (car seats, safety gates, safe sleep, pool fencing) saves lives.

Well-child visits at the pediatrician follow a schedule covering screening, vaccination, anticipatory guidance, and monitoring of growth and development. In well-resourced systems, these visits are frequent in the first two years and space out thereafter.

## Why This Level Matters

The first two years set much of the trajectory for what follows. Secure attachment, rich language input, adequate nutrition, and responsive caregiving affect cognitive, emotional, and health outcomes measurably into adulthood.

The policy implications are substantial. High-quality parental leave, early childhood nutrition programs, maternal mental health support, and quality childcare are interventions with large return on investment measured in decades of life outcomes. Every country that has taken these seriously has seen measurable benefits. Countries that haven't have measurable costs.

Knowing the developmental schedule also helps identify concerns early. Most developmental delays, when caught in the first two years, respond better to intervention than if caught at age 5.
