# Level 1: Sensing
*The seven (plus) senses, and the interoception you barely notice*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## More Than Five

The old "five senses" is a convenient simplification that falls apart on inspection. Modern neuroscience identifies at least:

- **Vision** (sight)
- **Hearing** (audition)
- **Smell** (olfaction)
- **Taste** (gustation)
- **Touch** (somatosensation) — itself multiple: pressure, vibration, fine touch, pain, temperature
- **Proprioception**: where your body parts are in space, without looking. Close your eyes and touch your nose; proprioception made that work.
- **Vestibular**: balance and spatial orientation, from the semicircular canals in the inner ear.
- **Interoception**: internal signals — hunger, thirst, bladder fullness, heart rate, breath rate, nausea, temperature, emotion.

Count this as 8+ senses depending on how you split them. Some taxonomies go further (chronoception — the sense of time passing — is often listed; nociception, the pain system, is sometimes considered distinct from touch).

The point isn't the counting. It's that perception is far richer than the folk categories suggest, and interoception in particular shapes how you feel moment to moment in ways most people are unaware of.

## Vision — The Dominant Sense

The human visual system uses roughly 30% of cortical real estate and dominates conscious experience for sighted people. Key structures:

- **Cornea and lens** focus light onto the retina.
- **Retina** contains photoreceptors: ~120 million rods (sensitive to dim light, colorblind, dominant in periphery) and ~6 million cones (color-sensitive in three types — roughly blue, green, red — dominant in central vision).
- **Optic nerve** carries visual signals to the brain.
- **Lateral geniculate nucleus** (in the thalamus) routes visual input.
- **Primary visual cortex** (V1, at the back of the brain) does basic feature extraction.
- Higher visual areas (V2, V4, IT, MT, etc.) do color, motion, object, and face recognition in specialized regions.

Humans see roughly 400–700 nm wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation. Other animals see more: bees see ultraviolet; some fish see polarization; mantis shrimp have 12+ photoreceptor types. The human view is a narrow slice of physical reality.

Vision is also heavily top-down. What you "see" is the brain's best guess at what's in front of you, constructed from incomplete retinal information plus strong priors based on expectation and context. This is why visual illusions work — the illusion exploits the brain's inference machinery. In most cases, the inference is accurate enough to be useful. In some cases, it's wildly wrong and persistent.

## Hearing

The ear converts pressure waves in air into neural signals. Sound waves enter the outer ear, vibrate the eardrum, pass through three small bones (malleus, incus, stapes) that amplify the signal, and then enter the fluid-filled cochlea in the inner ear.

The cochlea's basilar membrane is tonotopically organized — high frequencies vibrate at one end, low frequencies at the other. Hair cells along the membrane transduce mechanical motion into neural signals. The auditory cortex receives the signals and decodes them into speech, music, environmental sounds.

Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz in youth, declining to perhaps 20 Hz to 12 kHz by old age (presbycusis — age-related hearing loss in the high-frequency range). Conversational speech occupies roughly 100–8,000 Hz. Dogs hear up to 45 kHz. Bats echolocate up to 200 kHz.

Binaural hearing (two ears) lets the brain compute location from inter-aural time and intensity differences. You can localize a sound source to within a few degrees in the horizontal plane — a skill that saves animals from predators and that audio engineers replicate in stereo sound.

Hearing loss is one of the most common age-related disabilities. About 15% of US adults have some degree; rates rise steeply with age. Noise exposure accelerates hearing loss — long-term exposure above about 85 dB (a busy street, a factory floor, loud music) causes permanent damage.

## Smell and Taste

Olfaction and gustation work together to produce flavor. Pure taste, on the tongue, detects five basic modalities: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami (savory, from glutamate). Possibly sixth (fat). Smell — via ~400 olfactory receptor types in the nose — adds thousands of discriminable chemicals.

What most people call "taste" is mostly smell. Pinch your nose and eat something strongly flavored; the experience flattens dramatically. The fast loss of flavor with a head cold works the same way.

Olfaction is unusual among senses in that it has direct connections to emotion-processing regions (amygdala, hippocampus) without the usual thalamic relay. This is thought to explain why smells trigger strong emotional memories — the "Proustian" effect.

The molecular mechanisms of olfaction were worked out in the 1990s (Linda Buck and Richard Axel, Nobel 2004). Each olfactory receptor neuron expresses one receptor gene; each receptor binds a subset of odorant molecules. Combinations of activated receptors code specific smells, so the system can distinguish many more odors than it has receptor types.

Loss of smell (anosmia) became widely known during COVID-19, which damaged olfactory neurons in many patients. Most recovered within weeks or months; a minority had persistent loss. Anosmia also accompanies some neurodegenerative diseases (Parkinson's, Alzheimer's) — sometimes as an early warning sign.

## Touch, Pain, Temperature

The somatosensory system uses mechanical, thermal, and chemical receptors distributed over the skin and throughout the body:

- **Mechanoreceptors**: multiple types (Meissner, Merkel, Pacinian, Ruffini) tuned to different stimuli — light touch, sustained pressure, vibration, stretch.
- **Thermoreceptors**: warm and cold receptors activate at different temperature ranges. The same receptor (TRPM8) that responds to cold also responds to menthol, which is why menthol feels cool.
- **Nociceptors**: respond to tissue damage. Signals pass through spinal cord pathways to pain-processing regions in the brain.
- **Proprioceptors**: muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs signal muscle length, tension, and joint angle.

Pain is constructed. The peripheral nociceptor signal is one input; the subjective experience is a brain construction that involves attention, expectation, context, and emotional state. This is why pain can be modulated by distraction, placebos, and stress. It's also why chronic pain can persist after injury heals — the pain system itself has changed.

Pain that's appropriate to tissue damage is **nociceptive**. Pain from nerve damage is **neuropathic**. Pain without clear peripheral cause, often amplified, is **nociplastic**. These respond to different treatments. Modern pain medicine tries to identify which category applies.

## Proprioception and Vestibular

Proprioception lets you walk down stairs without looking, type without watching your hands, bring a fork to your mouth in the dark. Muscle spindles and joint receptors feed continuously updated position and force data to the brain.

Vestibular sense is from the semicircular canals and otolith organs in the inner ear. The three semicircular canals detect angular acceleration in three planes. The otoliths detect linear acceleration and gravity. Together they tell the brain which way is up and how the head is moving.

Damage or disease (Meniere's, vestibular neuritis, benign paroxysmal positional vertigo) produces vertigo — a sickening sense that the world is spinning. It's one of the more unpleasant symptoms in medicine and often takes weeks of central compensation to resolve.

Proprioception and vestibular sense are usually unconscious. They rise to awareness only when they fail (you close your eyes and stagger; you spin around and get dizzy). But they are doing constant, essential work for every movement you make.

## Interoception

Interoception is the sense of the internal state of the body. Hunger, thirst, fatigue, bladder fullness, heart rate awareness, gut sensations, respiratory rate, core temperature, nausea, sexual arousal — all are interoceptive.

Interoception interacts with emotion. The insular cortex is the primary cortical region for both interoception and emotional feeling. A racing heart and a flushed face contribute to the experience of anxiety or anger; gut sensations contribute to disgust or unease; lowered heart rate variability correlates with depression.

Interoceptive accuracy (how well you can, e.g., count your own heartbeats without feeling your pulse) varies across individuals and has been correlated with emotional regulation, eating behavior, and several psychiatric conditions. It's a relatively new research area with more to learn.

Practical implication: much of what people call "a gut feeling" is probably interoceptive information the conscious mind hasn't explicitly labeled. Some of those signals are useful; others are noise or reflect past experience that doesn't apply to the present.

## Attention — The Switchboard

The senses together produce vastly more information than conscious awareness can process. Attention is the filter.

**Bottom-up attention**: salient stimuli (loud noises, sudden movement, bright lights) capture attention involuntarily.

**Top-down attention**: the brain selects what to attend to based on goals, expectations, and priorities.

Attention has limits. You cannot fully attend to two demanding streams at once — what feels like multitasking is usually rapid switching with costs. Sustained attention is finite; mental fatigue is real. Attention also systematically misses obvious things outside its current focus — the "invisible gorilla" phenomenon.

Attentional disorders (ADHD in its various forms) reflect real, measurable differences in attentional regulation. Medications that enhance dopaminergic and noradrenergic signaling can substantially improve function in many cases.

## Why This Level Matters

You experience the world through sensation. Everything you know about external reality came through these channels. The reliability of sensation, its limits, and its vulnerabilities to illusion and attention set the baseline for every other cognitive process.

Medicine, psychology, engineering (UI design, human factors, assistive technology), and art all depend on understanding the sensory systems. Many common human complaints — vertigo, chronic pain, tinnitus, anxiety, sensory overload — reflect sensory systems under stress or malfunctioning.
