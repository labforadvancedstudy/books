# Level 2: Moving
*Muscles, bones, balance, and locomotion — how the body goes from place to place*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Moving Animal

Humans are animals that move. Walking upright on two legs, manipulating objects with dexterous hands, running for hours, throwing with precision — our movement repertoire is unusual among mammals and deeply shaped our evolution, our anatomy, and our experience.

Level 1 covered **sensing**. Level 2 covers the reciprocal: **moving**. Every movement — blinking, walking, reaching, jumping — is the output of a multi-layer control system involving brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves, muscles, tendons, joints, and bones. Understanding how this system works explains why we can sit still for hours and then sprint, why skilled movement takes years to develop but then becomes automatic, and why injuries and aging affect movement the way they do.

## Skeletal System

The **skeleton** is the structural chassis. Humans have ~206 bones (more at birth, some fuse during growth). Categories:

- **Axial skeleton**: skull, vertebral column (~33 vertebrae), ribs (12 pairs), sternum. Protects the central nervous system and organs; supports the body's upright posture.
- **Appendicular skeleton**: arms, legs, and the girdles (shoulder and pelvic) that connect them to the axial skeleton. Enables movement.

Bones are living tissue — constantly being remodeled by osteoblasts (building) and osteoclasts (removing). They're strong in compression (like concrete) and have honeycombed interior structure (spongy bone) that is strong-for-weight. They also function as calcium reservoirs and house marrow where blood cells are made.

**Bone health** depends on mechanical loading, nutrition (calcium, vitamin D, protein), hormones, and activity. Disuse weakens bone (astronauts lose ~1% of bone mass per month in microgravity); weight-bearing exercise strengthens it. Osteoporosis — loss of bone density with age — increases fracture risk, especially in postmenopausal women.

## Joints

Where bones meet, **joints** permit (or prevent) movement. Types:

- **Fibrous joints**: little to no movement. Skull sutures, teeth in sockets.
- **Cartilaginous joints**: limited movement. Vertebral disks, pubic symphysis.
- **Synovial joints**: free movement. Most major joints (knee, elbow, shoulder, hip). Capsule, synovial membrane producing lubricating fluid, articular cartilage on bone ends.

Synovial joints vary in geometry:
- **Hinge** (elbow, knee, ankle): one axis of rotation.
- **Pivot** (neck — C1-C2): rotation around one axis.
- **Ball-and-socket** (hip, shoulder): three axes of rotation.
- **Saddle** (base of thumb): two axes plus rotation.
- **Condyloid** (wrist): two axes without true rotation.
- **Plane** (between small bones): sliding.

The shoulder, the most mobile joint in the body, trades stability for range of motion — dislocations are common. The hip, less mobile but more stable, is a more robust joint in normal activity but replacement is common after osteoarthritis. Ligaments reinforce joints, limiting motion to safe ranges; sprains tear ligaments.

## Muscle

Three muscle types:

- **Skeletal muscle**: attached to bones by tendons, drives voluntary movement. Striated (striped under microscope) from the regular array of contractile units (sarcomeres).
- **Smooth muscle**: in walls of organs (intestines, blood vessels, bladder). Involuntary control.
- **Cardiac muscle**: only in the heart. Striated and involuntary. Self-rhythmic.

This level focuses on skeletal muscle. It's ~40% of adult body mass — the largest tissue by mass.

**Contraction** works by the sliding of actin and myosin filaments past each other, driven by ATP hydrolysis, triggered by calcium release following a motor nerve signal. When many fibers contract together, the muscle shortens and develops force.

**Fiber types**:
- **Type I (slow oxidative)**: red, fatigue-resistant, aerobic, good for endurance.
- **Type IIa (fast oxidative-glycolytic)**: intermediate. Both endurance and power.
- **Type IIb/IIx (fast glycolytic)**: white, fast, powerful, fatigue quickly. Good for sprinting and lifting.

Muscles vary in fiber-type composition by function — postural muscles tend to be more Type I, fast-moving muscles more Type II. Training can shift fiber properties somewhat but not wholesale across the main types.

**Motor units** — one motor neuron plus all the muscle fibers it innervates — are the functional units. Fine control (eye muscles) has small motor units (one neuron per few fibers); power movements (gluteus maximus) have large motor units (hundreds of fibers per neuron).

## Movement Control

Several levels of the nervous system participate in movement:

**Cerebral cortex**: planning, decision-making, voluntary control. Primary motor cortex sends signals to produce specific movements. Premotor and supplementary motor areas plan sequences and combinations. Posterior parietal cortex integrates spatial information.

**Cerebellum**: "little brain" at the back of the skull. Coordinates timing, precision, and learning of movements. Cerebellar injury causes ataxia — uncoordinated, drunken-looking movement with preserved strength. The cerebellum learns from error, updating motor programs over repetition.

**Basal ganglia**: deep nuclei. Initiation and selection of movements. Parkinson's disease (degeneration of dopaminergic neurons projecting to basal ganglia) causes difficulty initiating movement, tremor, and rigidity.

**Brainstem**: handles reflexes, posture, eye movements, basic locomotor patterns.

**Spinal cord**: central pattern generators for rhythmic movements (walking), stretch reflexes (knee-jerk), and the final common path — every descending command must pass through motor neurons here.

**Motor neurons**: final output. Cell bodies in the spinal cord; axons extend to muscle.

Hierarchical control lets the cortex focus on what to do while lower levels handle the how. When you decide to reach for a cup, the cortex doesn't specify every muscle contraction — it sets the goal, the cerebellum and spinal cord fill in the details.

## Reflexes

**Reflexes** are automatic responses to specific stimuli, mediated by circuits that don't require conscious control.

- **Stretch reflex** (e.g., knee-jerk): muscle spindle detects stretch, sensory neuron excites motor neuron to the same muscle, muscle contracts. The simplest reflex — two neurons.
- **Withdrawal reflex**: pain stimulus to a limb, flexors on the same side contract to pull it away, extensors on the opposite side contract to stabilize balance.
- **Righting reflexes**: vestibular and visual inputs triggering postural adjustments.

Reflexes are both protective and building blocks. Walking involves chains of reflexes coordinated by central pattern generators in the spinal cord. Fine manipulation involves modulation of reflexes by descending cortical signals.

## Walking and Running

Bipedal walking is harder than it looks. Each step involves single-leg support while the other leg swings forward — a dynamically stable but inherently unstable situation. The center of mass must be controlled continuously to keep from falling.

**Gait phases**:
- **Stance phase** (~60% of cycle): foot on ground. Heel strike → foot flat → heel off → toe off.
- **Swing phase** (~40%): foot off ground. Initial swing → mid-swing → terminal swing.

Key biomechanical features:
- **Double support**: brief periods (start and end of stance) when both feet are on the ground.
- **Center of mass trajectory**: rises and falls in a sinusoidal pattern; the body acts like an inverted pendulum during stance.
- **Energy exchange**: kinetic and potential energy oscillate, exchanging through the step, making walking surprisingly efficient.

**Running** differs:
- **Aerial phase**: both feet off the ground (walking never has this).
- **Spring-mass dynamics**: legs act like springs, storing and releasing elastic energy in tendons (especially the Achilles).
- **No inverted pendulum**: energy exchange works differently; metabolic cost per kilometer is higher than walking.

Humans are exceptional endurance runners. Compared to other mammals, we're slow in sprint but efficient over long distances, with sweat-based cooling that outlasts most quadrupeds' panting cooling. This may have driven the evolution of long-distance running in early humans for persistence hunting.

## Manipulation — The Hand

The human hand is a remarkable tool. Key features:

- **Opposable thumb**: can touch the tips of all other fingers, enabling precision grip (thumb-to-fingertip) and power grip (all fingers around an object).
- **Dense sensory innervation**: fingertips have the highest density of touch receptors in the body after the lips.
- **Proportional representation in motor cortex**: the hand occupies a disproportionately large fraction of motor and sensory cortex — the reason for the classic "homunculus" with giant hands.
- **Finger independence**: within limits. The ring finger has poor independence because its flexor tendon shares a common origin with the middle finger's.

Hand function enables everything from fine manipulation (writing, surgery, playing instruments) to tool use (the enabler of much of human technology) to gesture (a substantial part of language).

Hand injuries and neurologic conditions affecting hand function (stroke, peripheral nerve injury, repetitive strain) are major disabilities — hand therapy is a specialty reflecting how much function depends on full dexterity.

## Balance

Staying upright — standing, walking, reacting to perturbations — requires continuous coordination. Three sensory systems contribute:

- **Vestibular**: inner ear detects head orientation and acceleration.
- **Visual**: reference to the horizon and surroundings.
- **Proprioceptive**: muscle spindles, joint receptors, skin touch sensors provide information about body position and movement.

When one system is impaired, others can partially compensate — but not fully. Vestibular disorders (vertigo, BPPV, Meniere's) cause dramatic balance problems. Loss of proprioception (severe peripheral neuropathy, spinal cord injury) makes people rely heavily on vision for balance, failing in the dark.

Balance training — tai chi, yoga, specific physical therapy programs — improves proprioception and reaction time. Fall prevention in older adults is major public health: falls cause 32,000+ deaths a year in the US and are the leading cause of injury death in over-65s.

## Motor Learning

Skilled movement — typing, driving, surgery, musical performance, athletic skills — is learned, not innate. Phases:

- **Cognitive phase**: you think through every step. Slow, effortful, error-prone. Explicit understanding dominates.
- **Associative phase**: movements are organized into sequences, errors decrease, conscious attention shifts to higher-level strategy.
- **Autonomous phase**: movement is fast, accurate, and doesn't require conscious attention. You drive while carrying on conversation; the pianist performs without thinking of each finger.

Motor learning requires **practice**, ideally **deliberate practice** — focused attention to elements that need improvement, rather than mindless repetition. Feedback matters — you need to know how close to target you are. Variability helps — slightly varied conditions during practice produce better retention and transfer than identical repetition.

Motor skills fade but never disappear completely. A decade away from cycling, you still can cycle. The consolidated motor patterns seem remarkably durable.

## Injury and Rehabilitation

Musculoskeletal injuries are common:

- **Sprains**: ligament tears. Ankle sprains especially frequent.
- **Strains**: muscle/tendon tears.
- **Fractures**: broken bones. Setting, immobilization, healing over weeks to months.
- **Dislocations**: joint surfaces pulled apart. Shoulder most common.
- **Overuse injuries**: tendinitis, stress fractures, chronic pain syndromes. Often from repetitive loading above tissue tolerance.
- **Sports injuries**: ACL tears, meniscus tears, rotator cuff problems.

**Rehabilitation** is a specialty that restores function after injury or illness. Key principles:

- **Controlled loading**: stressing tissues enough to drive healing but not enough to re-injure.
- **Range of motion**: preventing stiffness.
- **Strengthening**: progressive resistance.
- **Neuromuscular re-education**: retraining the nervous system to coordinate damaged systems.
- **Functional training**: practicing the actual movements the patient needs for work, sport, or daily life.

Physical therapists, occupational therapists, and athletic trainers deliver most rehabilitation. It's one of the highest-leverage areas of healthcare — a patient with a complete ACL tear who rehabs well can return to near-full function; one who doesn't loses function progressively.

## Why This Level Matters

Movement is so fundamental we often forget it's there. Impaired movement — from injury, stroke, neurological disease, or aging — is one of the greatest sources of disability and suffering. Understanding how movement works is the foundation for physical therapy, sports medicine, prosthetics, ergonomics, neurology, orthopedics, and the design of robots and prosthetic limbs that increasingly interface with human movement.

Every child learns to walk, run, climb, throw, and manipulate objects. Adults forget that this was learned — it feels like it was always there. But the same mechanisms that make a toddler tumble make an elder fall, and the same learning principles that gave you athletic skill as a teen can give you recovered skill after injury in middle age. Motor learning is lifelong if you practice.
