# Level 3: Metabolism and Energy
*Fueling the body — glucose, fat, protein, hormones, and the balance that keeps us alive*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## Why Metabolism Matters

L2 covered movement and nervous control — how the body acts. L3 turns to the fuel that makes all action possible. Every cell runs on chemical energy, ultimately from food. The body must extract energy, store it, transport it, match supply to demand second-by-second, and coordinate all this across organs that have very different fuel needs. Brain demands constant glucose. Heart favors fatty acids. Muscle shifts between depending on intensity. Gut processes incoming fuel. Liver is the central metabolic hub.

Failures of metabolism are among the biggest killers and disablers: type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease linked to obesity and lipids, cancer's metabolic rewiring, liver disease. Understanding metabolism is understanding most chronic disease.

## Cells and ATP

At the cellular level, **adenosine triphosphate (ATP)** is the energy currency. Breaking one phosphate bond releases ~30 kJ/mol, powering most cellular work — muscle contraction, pump gradients, biosynthesis, signaling. A typical human turns over ~50-75 kg of ATP per day; standing pool is ~250 g. ATP is not stored but continuously regenerated from food-derived fuels.

ATP production pathways:
- **Glycolysis**: glucose → pyruvate + 2 ATP. Anaerobic, cytoplasmic, fast but low yield.
- **Oxidative phosphorylation** (mitochondria): pyruvate → CO₂ + H₂O, ~30-32 ATP per glucose. Requires oxygen. Main fuel for most cells.
- **Fatty acid β-oxidation**: fat → acetyl-CoA → oxidative phosphorylation. Very high ATP yield per molecule.
- **Amino acid oxidation**: after deamination; used when other fuels are scarce.
- **Ketone body oxidation**: from fats in fasting or low-carb states; brain can use when glucose limited.

Different tissues have different preferences. Red blood cells (no mitochondria) rely entirely on glycolysis. Cardiac muscle uses ~70% fatty acid oxidation at rest. Brain normally uses glucose (~120 g/day) but shifts to ketones in prolonged fasting (~75% of brain energy from ketones after weeks).

## Macronutrients

Food provides three macronutrients plus micronutrients and water.

**Carbohydrates**: starches and sugars, ~4 kcal/g. Digested to glucose (mostly), fructose, galactose. Blood glucose normally maintained ~70-130 mg/dL by hormonal regulation. Rapid energy source.

**Fats**: triglycerides, ~9 kcal/g — highest energy density. Long-term storage and steady fuel. Saturated vs. unsaturated fats; omega-3 vs. omega-6 balance affects inflammation. Essential fatty acids (linoleic, α-linolenic) must be obtained from diet.

**Protein**: ~4 kcal/g. Primary role is building blocks (muscle, enzymes, antibodies, hormones) not fuel. Can be used for energy but turnover is the main story. ~0.8-1.6 g/kg/day recommended for adults; higher for athletes, elderly, pregnant. 9 essential amino acids must be dietary.

**Alcohol**: ~7 kcal/g — notable given prevalence. Metabolized primarily by liver; affects many pathways.

**Micronutrients**: vitamins (A, B-complex, C, D, E, K) and minerals (iron, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iodine, etc.). Required in small amounts. Deficiencies (iron-deficiency anemia, iodine deficiency, vitamin D deficiency) still widespread globally.

## Digestion

Food enters mouth, moves through esophagus to stomach (acid, pepsin, some breakdown), to small intestine (most absorption — pancreatic enzymes, bile, brush border), to colon (water absorption, bacterial fermentation), to elimination. Takes ~24-72 hours total.

Key steps:
- **Carbohydrates**: starch → maltose → glucose by amylases; disaccharides by brush-border enzymes; glucose absorbed via SGLT1 (sodium-coupled) and GLUT2.
- **Proteins**: pepsin in stomach, then pancreatic proteases; amino acids and small peptides absorbed.
- **Fats**: emulsified by bile, hydrolyzed by lipase, absorbed as free fatty acids and monoglycerides, reassembled into chylomicrons in intestinal cells, transported via lymph.

**Microbiome** (~10¹³-10¹⁴ bacteria, mainly colon) ferments indigestible fiber into short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate, butyrate) — important for colon health and systemic metabolic signaling. Diversity and composition linked to metabolic health.

## Hormonal Regulation

Four key hormones orchestrate metabolism:

**Insulin** (pancreatic β-cells): released after meals in response to rising glucose and amino acids. Signals "fed state": stores glucose as glycogen (liver, muscle), stores fat (adipose), promotes protein synthesis, inhibits lipolysis and gluconeogenesis. Critical for maintaining blood glucose within tight range.

**Glucagon** (pancreatic α-cells): released when glucose low. Opposite of insulin — stimulates glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, ketogenesis.

**Cortisol** (adrenal cortex): stress hormone; promotes glucose availability, protein catabolism, fat redistribution. Diurnal rhythm; elevated chronic cortisol in chronic stress associated with metabolic dysfunction.

**Epinephrine** (adrenal medulla, sympathetic nerves): fight-or-flight; rapid glucose mobilization, lipolysis, increased heart rate.

Other players: growth hormone (anabolic, lipolytic), thyroid hormones (set metabolic rate), leptin (adipose; satiety/energy-availability signal), ghrelin (gastric; hunger signal), PYY and GLP-1 (gut; post-meal satiety), FGF21 (liver; fasting signal). This regulatory network is complex; many chronic metabolic diseases involve its dysfunction.

## The Fed-Fasting Cycle

Metabolism oscillates between fed and fasting states continuously.

**Fed state** (~3-4 hours post-meal): insulin high. Glucose enters cells, glycogen stores fill, triglycerides synthesize, protein synthesis proceeds, gluconeogenesis suppressed.

**Early fasting** (~4-18 hours): glucose stores deplete; liver glycogen releases glucose. Insulin falls, glucagon rises.

**Prolonged fasting** (>24 hours): liver glycogen exhausted. Gluconeogenesis from amino acids and glycerol becomes major glucose source. Fat mobilizes heavily. Ketone bodies rise (from partial fat oxidation in liver), providing alternative brain fuel.

**Starvation** (days to weeks): adaptations to minimize lean mass loss. Brain shifts heavily to ketones (reduces amino acid demand for gluconeogenesis). Metabolic rate falls. Body composition and functional capacity decline progressively.

**Refeeding** after prolonged starvation can be dangerous (refeeding syndrome): rapid shifts in electrolytes, fluid balance with carbohydrate reintroduction. Medical supervision needed for severe cases.

## Exercise Metabolism

Muscle fuel choice shifts with intensity and duration:

- **Rest and light activity**: ~70% fat oxidation.
- **Moderate intensity (50-65% VO₂max)**: mix of fat and carbohydrate.
- **High intensity (>75% VO₂max)**: predominantly carbohydrate (glucose and glycogen).
- **Maximal / sprint**: anaerobic glycolysis + phosphocreatine (immediate energy from stored PCr, ~10-second supply).

**Glycogen stores** (~300-500 g total, ~25 g per kg muscle + ~100 g liver) limit high-intensity endurance — "bonking" or "hitting the wall" is glycogen depletion. Carbohydrate intake during long exercise (>90 min) delays bonking.

**Fat oxidation** has essentially unlimited supply in most people but can't support highest intensities. Endurance training increases fat oxidation capacity, sparing glycogen.

**Training adaptations**:
- **Mitochondrial biogenesis**: more and better mitochondria.
- **Capillarization**: more blood vessels in muscle.
- **Enzyme upregulation**: oxidative enzymes, fat oxidation pathway enzymes.
- **Glycogen storage capacity increases**.
- **Hypertrophy** (resistance training): muscle fiber growth.

## Obesity and Energy Balance

Body weight is determined by long-run energy balance (intake vs. expenditure). Both components are regulated and influenced by genetics, environment, behavior.

**Energy expenditure** has components:
- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)**: ~60-75% of total. Resting energy for vital functions. ~1200-1800 kcal/day for most adults.
- **Thermic effect of food**: ~10%. Energy for digestion.
- **Physical activity**: varies widely. ~15-30% typically; can be much higher in athletes or laborers.
- **Non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT)**: fidgeting, posture, standing. Varies up to ~2000 kcal/day between individuals.
- **Cold thermogenesis**: brown adipose tissue activation. Modest contribution in adults.

**Set-point theory**: body defends a weight range via metabolic and appetite adaptations. Weight loss reduces BMR disproportionately and increases hunger; maintenance of large loss is very difficult. This is not simply "willpower" — the physiology is real.

**Obesity prevalence**: ~40% of US adults, ~15% global, rising everywhere. Causes complex: dietary shifts, food environment, reduced activity, sleep, stress, microbiome, gene-environment interactions.

**Health consequences**: type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, some cancers, osteoarthritis, sleep apnea, liver disease, impaired fertility, mental health correlations.

**Interventions**:
- **Diet**: calorie restriction, various patterns (Mediterranean, low-carb, intermittent fasting). Initial losses comparable; long-term success limited.
- **Exercise**: critical for maintenance; alone produces modest loss.
- **Behavioral therapy**: improves adherence.
- **Pharmacotherapy**: GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide / Wegovy, tirzepatide / Mounjaro / Zepbound) producing unprecedented weight loss (~15-20% mean) in trials and real-world use since ~2021-22.
- **Bariatric surgery**: gastric bypass, sleeve gastrectomy. Substantial, durable loss for many; surgical risks and nutrient absorption concerns.

The GLP-1 revolution is reshaping obesity treatment. Remaining questions about long-term use, muscle loss, cost, equity.

## Diabetes

**Type 2 diabetes** (~90% of cases): insulin resistance (tissues respond less to insulin) combined with β-cell dysfunction (insulin secretion can't compensate). Blood glucose chronically elevated. Strongly associated with obesity, age, genetic background, ethnicity (higher in South Asian, Hispanic, African, Native American populations).

**Type 1 diabetes** (~5-10%): autoimmune destruction of β-cells; absolute insulin deficiency. Usually childhood or young adult onset; life-long insulin therapy required.

**Prediabetes**: impaired glucose tolerance or fasting glucose. ~35% of US adults. Many progress to diabetes; prevention possible.

**Complications** of chronic hyperglycemia:
- **Microvascular**: retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy.
- **Macrovascular**: cardiovascular disease, stroke.
- **Wound healing**: infections, amputations.
- **Overall**: ~6-8 year life expectancy reduction untreated; much less with good control.

**Treatment**:
- **Type 2**: metformin first-line; SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists (which also aid weight loss), sulfonylureas, insulin as needed. Lifestyle critical.
- **Type 1**: insulin (multiple injections or pump), continuous glucose monitoring, carbohydrate counting. Artificial pancreas systems (closed-loop) increasingly available.

## Lipids and Cardiovascular Disease

Blood lipids partition into lipoprotein particles:
- **LDL (low-density lipoprotein)**: delivers cholesterol to tissues. Elevated LDL is causal for atherosclerosis.
- **HDL (high-density lipoprotein)**: transports cholesterol back to liver. Higher correlates with lower CVD risk, though interventions raising HDL haven't clearly reduced events.
- **Triglycerides**: elevated associated with risk; causal role debated.

**Statins** (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors) lower LDL by 30-55%, reduce cardiovascular events substantially (25-40% relative risk reduction in high-risk populations). Among the most-studied drugs; widely prescribed.

**PCSK9 inhibitors**: powerful newer LDL-lowering for specific populations.

Diet effects on lipids: saturated fat modestly raises LDL; trans fats worse; fiber, plant sterols lower LDL. Dietary cholesterol has smaller effect than once thought.

## Metabolic Syndrome

Cluster of conditions — central obesity, elevated triglycerides, low HDL, elevated blood pressure, elevated fasting glucose — associated with elevated CVD and type 2 diabetes risk. Estimated ~30%+ of US adults meet criteria.

Interpretation: not a single disease but a pattern indicating metabolic dysregulation. Treatment targets components individually; lifestyle modifications address multiple.

## Thyroid and Metabolic Rate

**Thyroid hormones** (T3, T4) set the overall metabolic rate. Hypothyroidism (slow metabolism, weight gain, fatigue, cold intolerance) — most common cause Hashimoto's autoimmune. Hyperthyroidism (high metabolism, weight loss, heat intolerance, anxiety) — Graves' disease. Both common; thyroid screening inexpensive.

## Why This Level Matters

Metabolism sits at the core of physiology and chronic disease. Obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and related metabolic conditions affect billions globally and cost trillions in healthcare and lost productivity. Interventions ranging from diet and exercise to pharmacotherapy (GLP-1 agonists especially) to surgery are increasingly effective — but scale and equity of access remain challenges.

At the individual level, metabolism determines how one feels daily — energy, hunger, mood, cognition, recovery. At the population level, metabolic disease shapes the healthcare burden of aging societies. Understanding the fuel cycle, the hormonal regulation, and the interaction with diet, exercise, sleep, and stress is foundational for personal health and for health policy.
