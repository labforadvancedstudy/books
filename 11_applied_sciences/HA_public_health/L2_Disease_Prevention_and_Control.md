*Vaccines, sanitation, screening, and the infrastructure that keeps populations healthy*

## Prevention vs. Treatment

Clinical medicine treats sick individuals. Public health aims to keep populations from getting sick in the first place — or to catch disease early when outcomes are better. The distinction is more than philosophical: the interventions, costs, metrics, and institutions differ.

The classic framework:

- **Primary prevention**: stop disease from happening. Vaccination, sanitation, tobacco control, seat belts, workplace safety regulations.
- **Secondary prevention**: detect disease early and intervene. Cancer screening (Pap smears, mammography, colonoscopy), hypertension screening, newborn metabolic screening.
- **Tertiary prevention**: reduce complications of existing disease. Cardiac rehabilitation, diabetes self-management, fall prevention in the elderly.

Primary prevention historically delivered the largest population-level gains: clean water, sanitation, vaccines, and tobacco control each contributed more to life expectancy in the 20th century than any individual clinical treatment. But primary prevention is politically difficult — its benefits are diffuse and long-term, its costs are immediate and concentrated on specific industries or populations.

## Vaccination

Vaccination is arguably the single most consequential public health intervention in human history. It has eliminated smallpox globally, nearly eliminated polio, drastically reduced measles, rubella, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, hepatitis B, and many others.

### How Vaccines Work

A vaccine exposes the immune system to an antigen from a pathogen — without causing the disease — so the immune system learns to recognize and quickly respond to the real pathogen.

Main types:
- **Live attenuated**: weakened live virus. Strong, long-lasting immunity. Examples: MMR, varicella, yellow fever, oral polio, rotavirus. Contraindicated in immunocompromised patients.
- **Inactivated / killed**: whole pathogen, killed. Good safety profile; may require boosters. Examples: inactivated polio, hepatitis A, rabies, influenza (some formulations).
- **Subunit / protein**: pieces of the pathogen. Safe, often requires adjuvant. Examples: hepatitis B, HPV, acellular pertussis.
- **Toxoid**: inactivated toxin of a pathogen. Used for tetanus, diphtheria.
- **Conjugate**: protein + polysaccharide. Used for Haemophilus influenzae type b, pneumococcal, meningococcal.
- **mRNA**: lipid-nanoparticle-encapsulated mRNA encoding the antigen. Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines pioneered the class at mass scale.
- **Viral vector**: harmless virus engineered to express antigen. Used in some COVID-19 vaccines (Johnson & Johnson, AstraZeneca), Ebola.

### Herd Immunity

When a sufficient fraction of a population is immune (through vaccination or prior infection), the pathogen cannot sustain transmission. Unvaccinated individuals — those too young, immunocompromised, or who did not respond — are protected indirectly.

The **herd immunity threshold** depends on the pathogen's transmissibility (R₀):
- Measles (R₀ ~15): needs ~95% immunity.
- Mumps (R₀ ~10): needs ~90%.
- Polio, diphtheria, rubella (R₀ ~5–7): needs ~80–85%.
- COVID-19 (R₀ 2–6 depending on variant): 50–85%.

Falling vaccination rates drop populations below thresholds. Measles outbreaks in multiple Western countries in the late 2010s traced to vaccine hesitancy reducing coverage below the 95% needed. Polio outbreaks in Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Africa reflect gaps in vaccination programs from conflict and disinformation.

### Vaccination Programs

Childhood immunization schedules are now standard in most countries, with variations. WHO's Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) has delivered billions of vaccine doses to the world's children since 1974. Global coverage of 3-dose DTP (diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis, a bellwether) rose from ~20% in the early 1980s to ~85% by the 2010s — among the largest public health gains ever. COVID-19 disruptions temporarily reversed some progress.

Adult vaccination: annual influenza, pneumococcal for older adults, shingles, tetanus boosters, COVID-19, HPV (adolescents and young adults). Travel vaccinations (yellow fever, typhoid, Japanese encephalitis, rabies) for specific destinations.

### Vaccine Hesitancy

Despite overwhelming evidence of safety and effectiveness, vaccine hesitancy has grown in many countries. Drivers: distrust of institutions, misinformation (especially online), perceived low risk of some diseases (because vaccination reduced them), religious or philosophical objections.

WHO declared vaccine hesitancy one of the top 10 threats to global health in 2019. Responses include school immunization requirements, provider training in motivational communication, factual correction campaigns, and engagement with community leaders. The COVID-19 pandemic both increased vaccination acceptance in some populations and hardened hesitancy in others.

## Sanitation and Water

Clean water and sanitation had larger impacts on 19th and 20th century life expectancy than all drugs combined. Waterborne diseases — cholera, typhoid, dysentery, hepatitis A, countless diarrheal illnesses — killed hundreds of thousands annually before sanitation infrastructure was built.

John Snow's 1854 removal of the Broad Street pump handle — demonstrating that cholera in Soho was from a contaminated well — is the founding act of modern epidemiology. Sewer construction in London, Paris, and major US cities in the late 19th century dropped urban death rates dramatically.

Today, ~2 billion people still lack safely managed drinking water; ~3.5 billion lack safely managed sanitation. Waterborne diseases kill ~800,000 children a year, mostly under age 5, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. The remaining infrastructure buildout is one of the highest-leverage public health goals globally.

## Disease Surveillance

Public health agencies monitor disease occurrence to detect outbreaks, track trends, and evaluate interventions. Categories:

- **Notifiable disease reporting**: doctors and labs report specific diseases (tuberculosis, syphilis, measles, meningitis, many others) to health authorities.
- **Syndromic surveillance**: monitoring emergency department visits, pharmacy sales, absenteeism for patterns suggestive of outbreaks.
- **Laboratory surveillance**: network of labs reporting pathogen identifications.
- **Seroprevalence surveys**: periodic testing of blood samples for antibodies to track population exposure.
- **Wastewater surveillance**: testing sewage for pathogens — used increasingly after COVID-19 demonstrated its utility.
- **Digital surveillance**: internet search data, social media, mobile phone data have been used in some emergencies.

National agencies (CDC in the US, ECDC in Europe, China CDC, KDCA in Korea, etc.) coordinate with WHO through the International Health Regulations, which require notification of events that may constitute public health emergencies of international concern.

## Outbreak Investigation

When an outbreak is detected, the playbook is standard:

1. **Confirm**: verify the outbreak (increase above expected, not just increased reporting).
2. **Case definition**: specify what counts as a case.
3. **Case finding**: actively search for cases.
4. **Describe**: person, place, time distributions.
5. **Hypothesis**: source and transmission route.
6. **Test hypothesis**: case-control or cohort study as appropriate.
7. **Control**: interventions to interrupt transmission.
8. **Communicate**: to public, other agencies, affected communities.
9. **Follow up**: confirm control, evaluate response.

This framework has been applied to thousands of outbreaks — from local foodborne illness to global pandemics. COVID-19 revealed strengths and weaknesses: fast genomic surveillance, rapid vaccine development, but uneven testing, inconsistent public communication, and politicization of interventions.

## Infection Control

Hospitals and clinics are disease-transmission environments. **Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs)** affect ~5–10% of hospitalized patients in high-income countries, much higher in many lower-income countries.

Infection control measures:

- **Hand hygiene**: the single most important intervention. Compliance remains stubbornly under 50% in many facilities.
- **Personal protective equipment (PPE)**: gloves, gowns, masks, eye protection, based on likely exposure.
- **Isolation precautions**: contact, droplet, airborne precautions for specific pathogens.
- **Sterilization and disinfection**: of instruments and surfaces.
- **Antibiotic stewardship**: limiting unnecessary antibiotic use to reduce resistance.
- **Vaccination of healthcare workers**: influenza, COVID-19, hepatitis B, others.
- **Surveillance and feedback**: tracking HAI rates by unit and provider.

Central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI) dropped 50% in US ICUs from 2008 to 2014 after systematic implementation of bundled interventions. The techniques work when applied consistently.

## Screening

**Screening** tests asymptomatic populations for early disease. Classic criteria (Wilson and Jungner, 1968) for a good screening program:

- Disease is an important public health problem.
- There is an effective treatment.
- Facilities for diagnosis and treatment are available.
- There is a latent or early-symptomatic stage.
- There is a suitable test (sensitive, specific, acceptable, affordable).
- The natural history is understood.
- There is an agreed policy on who to treat.
- Screening costs are reasonable relative to benefits.
- Screening is a continuous process, not a one-off.

Effective screening programs:
- **Newborn metabolic screening**: dried blood spots detect dozens of treatable disorders (PKU, sickle cell, cystic fibrosis, others).
- **Cervical cancer**: Pap smears and HPV testing dramatically reduced mortality.
- **Colorectal cancer**: colonoscopy and stool-based tests reduce incidence and mortality.
- **Breast cancer**: mammography reduces mortality modestly.
- **Hypertension and hyperlipidemia**: screening in primary care drives treatment of cardiovascular risk.

Controversial or net-harmful screenings: PSA screening for prostate cancer (risks of overtreatment rival benefits), lung cancer CT screening (helpful in heavy smokers, uncertain in others), thyroid cancer screening (primarily detects indolent disease). Overdiagnosis — finding abnormalities that would never cause harm — is a major cost.

## Tobacco, Diet, Exercise, Alcohol

The leading preventable causes of death in rich countries are behavioral:
- **Tobacco**: ~20% of US deaths. Cardiovascular disease, lung cancer, COPD.
- **Poor diet**: leading risk factor globally when combined. Salt intake, low fruit/vegetable intake, processed food.
- **Physical inactivity**: cardiovascular, diabetes, cancer, mental health.
- **Alcohol**: liver disease, cancer, injury, cardiovascular; net harms exceed benefits at most intake levels.

Tobacco control demonstrates what works: taxation, indoor smoking bans, graphic package warnings, advertising restrictions, cessation support. US adult smoking fell from ~42% in 1965 to ~12% in 2022. Similar trajectories in most high-income countries. Low- and middle-income countries are earlier in this transition; tobacco companies have shifted marketing accordingly.

Diet, exercise, and alcohol interventions are harder. Behavior change is difficult at the individual level; environments matter more — what's available, what's marketed, what's affordable. Sugary drink taxes, menu labeling, active transportation infrastructure, trans fat bans show that environmental/policy interventions can shift population behaviors.

## Global Health

Major global health priorities as of 2025:

- **Undernutrition and child health**: still the leading cause of death under age 5 globally.
- **Maternal mortality**: ~295,000 women died in childbirth or from pregnancy complications globally in 2017 — 95% in low/middle-income countries.
- **Infectious diseases**: HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, malaria remain major killers in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
- **Non-communicable diseases**: cardiovascular, cancer, diabetes, COPD rising rapidly in middle-income countries.
- **Mental health**: long under-addressed; depression alone costs ~$1 trillion/year globally.
- **Antimicrobial resistance**: 1.27 million deaths attributed in 2019. Rising.
- **Climate-related health impacts**: heat, infectious disease range shifts, air pollution, food system disruption.

Major agencies: WHO coordinates; national health ministries fund and implement; NGOs (Gates Foundation, MSF, Gavi, Global Fund) supplement; partnerships (PEPFAR for HIV, Roll Back Malaria) target specific diseases. Global health financing is a fraction of what economic and moral analysis would suggest optimal.

## Why This Level Matters

Most health improvement at population scale comes from prevention, not treatment. The gains from 20th-century public health — clean water, vaccines, tobacco control — exceed gains from any clinical intervention. The biggest preventable disease burdens today — cardiovascular disease, cancer, infectious disease in low-income countries — all have well-understood prevention tools that are underused.

Public health also provides the infrastructure that clinical medicine relies on: surveillance, outbreak response, safety net programs, health protection regulations. When this infrastructure works, it's invisible; when it fails (Flint water crisis, Ebola slow response, COVID-19 testing failure), the costs are immediate and severe.
