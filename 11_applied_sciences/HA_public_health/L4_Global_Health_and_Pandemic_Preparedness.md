# L4 — Global Health and Pandemic Preparedness

Level 3 surveyed public health — epidemiology, disease surveillance, health promotion, environmental health, and health systems. Level 4 steps up to the global scale and to the specific challenge of pandemic preparedness. Infectious disease does not respect borders, and neither do noncommunicable disease risk factors, health workforce shortages, or climate-driven health impacts. Global health organizes the world's response to shared challenges, while pandemic preparedness addresses the subset that could cascade into catastrophic outbreaks. Both have been reshaped by COVID-19, for better and worse.

## Global Burden of Disease

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study, maintained by IHME and published through Lancet, provides the most comprehensive estimates of mortality, morbidity, risk factors, and disability across geographies. Its metrics — disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years of life lost (YLL), years lived with disability (YLD) — structure much of global health policy analysis.

Key patterns as of the most recent GBD analyses: noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) — cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, chronic respiratory disease — dominate global burden, including in most low- and middle-income countries. Infectious diseases remain significant, particularly among children in low-income countries. Road injuries, interpersonal violence, and suicide contribute substantially. Mental health conditions are large and growing, with depression particularly prominent.

Life expectancy has risen globally over decades but trajectories differ. Sub-Saharan Africa's recovery from HIV/AIDS was remarkable; extension has continued, though at slower rates. High-income countries have seen stalling or declining life expectancy in some populations (U.S. opioids, Russia's cardiovascular and alcohol crises, COVID-19 impacts). China has seen rapid life expectancy increases alongside rising NCD burden.

The epidemiologic transition from infectious to noncommunicable disease dominance is well-documented but not uniform. Climate change, zoonotic risk, AMR, and mental health all complicate simple forward-march narratives.

## Communicable Diseases: Successes and Persistent Challenges

HIV/AIDS has been transformed by antiretroviral therapy availability. Global programs (PEPFAR, Global Fund) have provided treatment to tens of millions and reduced mother-to-child transmission dramatically. Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) has entered scale-up, including long-acting formulations. UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets (95 percent diagnosed, 95 percent on treatment, 95 percent virally suppressed) drive measurement.

Tuberculosis remains a leading infectious killer globally with 1.3 million deaths in 2022 per WHO. Drug-resistant TB is a major concern. New drugs (bedaquiline, pretomanid, delamanid) have improved treatment of resistant TB. Shorter all-oral regimens are being validated. The WHO End TB Strategy aims for 80 percent incidence reduction and 90 percent mortality reduction by 2030 — substantially off track.

Malaria prevention and treatment have advanced. WHO approved RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine (Mosquirix) in 2021; R21/Matrix-M vaccine (Serum Institute of India) received WHO prequalification in 2023 with higher efficacy. Vector control with insecticide-treated bednets and indoor residual spraying remains foundational. Artemisinin-based combination therapy treats most cases. Insecticide and drug resistance emerge, requiring continuous adaptation.

Neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) affect over a billion people. Mass drug administration programs have eliminated or near-eliminated onchocerciasis, lymphatic filariasis, and dracunculiasis in many countries. Soil-transmitted helminths, schistosomiasis, trachoma, leishmaniasis, and others receive coordinated but underfunded attention.

Vaccine-preventable disease programs have massive impact. Routine childhood immunization prevents millions of deaths annually; DTP3 coverage globally is around 85 percent with substantial geographic variation. Polio eradication has faced resurgence from vaccine-derived poliovirus and conflict-related coverage gaps; wild poliovirus remains in Afghanistan and Pakistan. Measles resurgence reflects immunization gaps exacerbated by COVID-19 disruption.

## Pandemic Risk Landscape

Pandemic risk has three principal sources. Zoonotic spillover — pathogens crossing from animals to humans — has been responsible for most historical pandemics. SARS-CoV-2 origins remain debated; likely spillover from horseshoe bat reservoir via intermediate host, though laboratory origin has not been definitively ruled out. Influenza A pandemics have recurrent zoonotic origins (swine, avian). MERS-CoV originates in camels. Ebola spills over from fruit bats.

Risk drivers include land use change that increases human-wildlife contact, wildlife trade (including wet markets and high-density operations), intensive livestock production, climate-driven range expansion of reservoir species and vectors, and urbanization with dense informal settlements.

Laboratory-origin risk has risen in prominence. Gain-of-function research, dual-use research concerns, and biosafety lapses create possible pathways to catastrophic release. Regulatory frameworks vary internationally; the U.S. Potential Pandemic Pathogen Care and Oversight framework has evolved; NIH guidelines on recombinant DNA research have evolved.

Deliberate release — bioterrorism or biological warfare — is a long-standing but historically limited concern. Advances in synthetic biology lower barriers to creating novel pathogens. The Biological Weapons Convention lacks verification mechanisms; national programs of concern remain.

Priority pathogen lists (WHO R&D Blueprint, U.S. Priority List, HHS BARDA) identify pathogens requiring sustained research. Coronaviruses, filoviruses (Ebola, Marburg), flaviviruses (Zika, dengue), influenza, hemorrhagic fever viruses, arenaviruses, and novel "Disease X" placeholders all feature.

## COVID-19: What Worked and What Didn't

COVID-19 was a historic test of global health systems. Approximately 7 million officially reported deaths through 2024, with excess mortality estimates substantially higher (15-25+ million). Major impacts included acute illness, long COVID, health system disruptions, educational losses, economic effects, and erosion of public trust in health authorities.

Successes included rapid pathogen characterization and genome sharing (weeks not years), development of effective vaccines at unprecedented speed (roughly 11 months from pathogen identification to first authorizations), identification of effective treatments (dexamethasone, tocilizumab, remdesivir, nirmatrelvir-ritonavir), and deployment of mass vaccination programs.

Failures included disease surveillance gaps that missed early spread in many regions, misinformation and politicization, highly uneven global vaccine access (COVAX undersupplied relative to bilateral deals), uneven testing capacity, supply chain disruptions of PPE and medical supplies, and erosion of trust in institutions that pursued hasty or inconsistent messaging.

Long COVID — post-acute sequelae — emerged as a major public health concern affecting millions. Research infrastructure (NIH RECOVER initiative) has substantial funding but has been criticized for slow pace. Clinical management remains largely supportive.

Health system recovery from COVID-19 is ongoing. Childhood immunization coverage, cancer screening, chronic disease management, and mental health services all experienced disruption. Workforce burnout and retention crises particularly affect nursing and primary care.

## The WHO and International Health Governance

The World Health Organization coordinates international health under its constitution and the 2005 International Health Regulations (IHR). COVID-19 exposed IHR limitations — timely notification, travel restrictions, equitable access to countermeasures, and enforcement.

Pandemic treaty negotiations at WHO have progressed slowly since 2021. Key contested issues include pathogen sample sharing and benefit sharing (including access to resulting vaccines and treatments); technology transfer; financing; surveillance standards; and roles of non-state actors. The World Health Assembly adopted IHR amendments in 2024 addressing some concerns; a broader pandemic agreement remains under negotiation.

WHO is funded by assessed contributions from member states and voluntary contributions. The 2022 reform shifting toward more assessed (predictable) funding addresses long-standing concern about donor-driven agenda. U.S. contributions, historically large, have varied with administration policy.

Other global health institutions — Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance; the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis, and Malaria; the Pandemic Fund at the World Bank; CEPI (Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations); GAVI and CEPI collaborations — provide specific financing and technical leverage. Coordination among them remains imperfect.

## Surveillance and Early Warning

Disease surveillance requires timely detection, reporting, and response. Traditional notifiable disease systems capture conditions with mandatory reporting; data quality varies widely. Event-based surveillance monitors media, social networks, and other signals for unusual events. Genomic surveillance — pathogen sequencing to detect new variants — became mainstream during COVID-19 but coverage remains uneven.

One Health integrates human, animal, and environmental health surveillance. FAO-WHO-WOAH (World Organisation for Animal Health)-UNEP Quadripartite coordinates work on zoonotic risks, AMR, and environmental health. Implementation at country level varies; many health and veterinary systems remain siloed.

Wastewater surveillance gained prominence during COVID-19 as an anonymized community-level signal. Subsequent expansion to polio, influenza, RSV, and emerging pathogens provides a complementary surveillance approach with varying sensitivity. Integration with clinical surveillance remains in progress.

Anomaly detection using AI is emerging. ProMED-mail, HealthMap, BlueDot, and other systems monitor signals; the earliest warning of COVID-19 in some systems preceded official notification. False positive management and signal-to-noise ratio matter; no system replaces human epidemiologic judgment.

Early warning for non-infectious threats (chemical incidents, environmental contamination, extreme heat) uses parallel systems. Climate-sensitive disease (vector-borne disease in new regions, heat-associated mortality) requires enhanced surveillance with climate integration.

## Medical Countermeasure Development

Medical countermeasures — vaccines, therapeutics, diagnostics, personal protective equipment — are the tools deployed during pandemics. Sustainable capacity requires baseline demand and research infrastructure. COVID-19 exposed how thin capacity had been before 2020.

The 100 Days Mission — developing safe and effective vaccines for a novel pathogen within 100 days — guides CEPI investment and partner research. Prototype pathogen approaches (studying representative viruses from each viral family) enable faster development when new pathogens emerge. Platform technologies (mRNA, adenoviral vector, protein subunit, whole-virion) reduce development time.

Therapeutic stockpiling (Strategic National Stockpile in the U.S., similar programs elsewhere) holds antivirals, antibodies, and supplies for emergency response. Stockpiles age; rotation management and readiness exercises matter.

Diagnostic capacity for novel pathogens depends on reference laboratories, PCR capacity, and increasingly sequence-based approaches. Point-of-care testing scale-up during COVID-19 demonstrated feasibility; sustaining testing infrastructure between crises is difficult.

Manufacturing capacity and distributed production have received attention. Africa CDC's goal of 60 percent African-made vaccines by 2040 addresses supply security concerns exposed during COVID-19. Regional manufacturing hubs in Senegal, South Africa, Rwanda, and elsewhere are advancing but require sustained investment and market support.

## Antimicrobial Resistance

AMR is a slow pandemic. WHO estimates 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to bacterial AMR in 2019, with 4.95 million associated. Without action, AMR deaths could reach 10 million per year by 2050 (O'Neill Review estimate, methodologically disputed but directionally important).

Drivers include antibiotic overuse and misuse in medicine, antibiotic use in agriculture (growth promotion, metaphylaxis, treatment), poor infection control, environmental contamination, and inadequate diagnostics leading to empirical treatment. Global consumption varies widely and tracks income and regulatory quality.

Responses include antimicrobial stewardship programs in hospitals (improving outcomes while reducing inappropriate use), agricultural restrictions (EU ban on growth promotion, global WHO guidance on medically important antimicrobials), rapid diagnostics to avoid empirical treatment, infection prevention and control, and surveillance (GLASS — Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System).

New antibiotic development has struggled economically. Limited returns on investment, the paradox that stewardship reduces use of new drugs, and scientific difficulty have thinned pipelines. Push incentives (grants, public-private partnerships like CARB-X, GARDP) fund research. Pull incentives (subscription models like UK's NICE-NHS pilot, U.S. PASTEUR Act proposal) aim to restore market viability. Progress is slow.

Phages (bacteriophages), antimicrobial peptides, and vaccines represent alternative approaches to reduce antibiotic dependence. Bacteriophage therapy is regulated as personalized medicine in some jurisdictions (Georgia, France, Belgium, U.S. compassionate use) with documented individual successes but limited randomized evidence.

## Climate Change and Health

Climate change affects health through many pathways. Heat exposure increases mortality, particularly among elderly, children, and outdoor workers. Air quality changes from wildfire smoke, dust, and ozone increase respiratory and cardiovascular disease. Vector-borne disease range expansion affects malaria, dengue, tick-borne diseases, and others. Food and water security stresses nutritional health. Mental health impacts of disasters, displacement, and climate anxiety are documented.

Adaptation efforts include heat action plans (beginning with European responses after 2003 heat wave), cooling center networks, building codes requiring climate resilience, health system resilience planning, and surveillance integration of climate data. WHO's Quality Criteria for Integrating Health into Climate Adaptation guides national planning.

Health system emissions — healthcare accounts for roughly 4-5 percent of global GHG emissions — are a growing focus. NHS England's Delivering a Net Zero Health Service, Kaiser Permanente's carbon-neutral status (claimed through offsets), and similar efforts target hospital energy use, anesthetic gases, pharmaceutical supply chains, and other high-emission activities.

Extreme weather events — floods, hurricanes, droughts, wildfires — disrupt health services and produce acute mortality. Post-disaster epidemiology (waterborne disease after floods, mental health after fires, displacement impacts) requires integrated response. The U.S. Pulse of the Storms data set and similar tools inform planning.

## Noncommunicable Disease Response

NCDs — cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, chronic respiratory disease — account for approximately 74 percent of global deaths (WHO). Shared risk factors include tobacco use, harmful alcohol use, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, and air pollution. Each addresses multiple conditions.

Tobacco control has been a major global health success. WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control has driven taxation, advertising restrictions, packaging changes, smoke-free environments, and cessation programs. Global adult tobacco use prevalence has declined. Continued challenges include e-cigarettes, youth targeting, and continued industry opposition in many markets.

Salt reduction efforts aim for WHO recommended intake below 5 grams/day. Reformulation (UK Food Standards Agency initial successes, spreading to other countries), clear labeling, and public education are standard approaches. Progress has been substantial in some countries (UK, Finland, Japan after historical reductions) and limited in others.

Diet-related NCD prevention addresses ultra-processed foods, sugar-sweetened beverages, trans fats (WHO target elimination achieved in some countries but not globally), and affordability of healthy foods. Taxation on sugar-sweetened beverages has spread (Mexico, UK, France, Berkeley pioneering examples) with documented reductions in consumption.

Physical activity promotion requires built environment changes (walkable cities, bicycle infrastructure), school physical education, workplace programs, and community interventions. WHO global guidelines emphasize 150-300 minutes moderate or 75-150 minutes vigorous activity per week.

Cancer control — prevention, screening, treatment, palliation — requires coordinated national programs. HPV vaccination for cervical cancer prevention is transformative; WHO's cervical cancer elimination initiative targets 90-70-90 (vaccination, screening, treatment). Screening implementation varies; low- and middle-income country cancer outcomes depend on early detection infrastructure.

Cardiovascular disease management globally involves hypertension detection and treatment (HEARTS technical package, WHO), cholesterol management, antithrombotic therapy where indicated, and reperfusion infrastructure for acute coronary syndrome. Coverage gaps are substantial.

Diabetes prevention and management scales with global prevalence. Type 2 diabetes is increasing in nearly all regions. Lifestyle interventions, metformin, and structured care programs prevent progression and complications. Global access to insulin for type 1 diabetes remains uneven.

## Health System Strengthening

Universal health coverage (UHC) — all people access quality health services without financial hardship — is a core global health goal. Progress varies; out-of-pocket payments cause financial catastrophe for over a hundred million households annually.

Primary health care as the foundation of universal coverage was articulated in the 1978 Alma-Ata Declaration and reinvigorated in the 2018 Astana Declaration. Integrated primary care with community health workers, essential medicines access, diagnostics, and referral systems delivers cost-effective care for most common conditions.

Health workforce is a critical constraint. Global shortages projected at tens of millions of health workers by 2030, concentrated in low-income countries. Education pipelines, retention (addressing migration to higher-income countries), task shifting (community health workers doing tasks traditionally done by higher-trained professionals with supervision), and working conditions all matter.

Essential medicines access lists by WHO guide national formularies. Patent and pricing structures shape availability; voluntary licensing, compulsory licensing, and generic competition provide access routes. Pooled procurement (Gavi, PAHO, Africa CDC) achieves volume discounts.

Health financing approaches vary. Single-payer national health services, social health insurance, mixed systems, and primarily private systems each have strengths and weaknesses. Strategic purchasing, provider payment design, and protection of vulnerable populations are common policy challenges across approaches.

## Global Health Ethics

Global health raises persistent ethical questions. Who benefits from research conducted in low-income countries? How should global resources be prioritized between infectious disease and NCDs, between treatment and prevention, between countries with similar needs?

Fair benefit sharing in pathogen samples (underlying decades of debate including the Nagoya Protocol, Pandemic Influenza Preparedness Framework, and pandemic treaty negotiations) reflects deeper questions about global equity.

Clinical trials in low-income countries must meet ethical standards without exploiting vulnerable populations. Standards from Declaration of Helsinki, ICH guidelines, and increasingly national research ethics committees shape practice. Persistent concerns about standard-of-care in trial arms, access to treatment after trial, and community engagement continue.

Decolonization of global health — questioning institutions, leadership structures, and practices dominated by historical colonial powers and high-income countries — has accelerated since 2020. Locally led research, equitable partnerships, and representation in governance are ongoing changes.

## Why This Level Matters

Global health and pandemic preparedness shape outcomes for all humans, and acutely for those in precarious circumstances. The stakes are:

- **Billions of lives depend on health system quality**: Small variations in child mortality, maternal care, and treatment access mean enormous cumulative impact
- **The next pandemic is certain**: The question is when and how bad, not whether. Preparedness investment today determines response capacity tomorrow
- **Climate and biodiversity crises intersect with health**: Global health is not separable from planetary health
- **Geopolitical fragmentation threatens cooperation**: Great power tensions, vaccine nationalism, and trust erosion make coordinated response harder than it was a decade ago
- **Inequities persist and can deepen**: Medical breakthroughs without parallel access and delivery become instruments of division

Understanding global health requires combining epidemiology, health systems analysis, political economy, and ethics. No single discipline suffices.

## The Transition to Level 5

Level 5 will examine specific programs and responses in depth. How does WHO actually function during a crisis (COVID-19 lessons)? What are the operational realities of a vaccination campaign in remote regions? How do partner-country ministries of health coordinate with donors? What is the engineering of global cold chain and supply logistics? How have Ebola outbreaks in West Africa (2014-2016) and DRC (2018-2020) been responded to, and what lessons apply forward?

Level 5 will also cover the politics of pandemic response. Why did some countries succeed and others fail with similar tools? What role did trust, governance, and communication play? What should be different next time?
