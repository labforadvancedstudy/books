## Why Equity Is a Health Issue

L2 covered disease prevention and control — the interventions public health deploys. L3 asks why disease burden is distributed so unequally, and what economics can tell us about which interventions are worth doing. Health disparities by income, race, gender, geography, and education are persistent and substantial in every country. In the US, life expectancy varies by 20+ years across census tracts, sometimes within a few miles. In many countries the gap between richest and poorest in child survival is 5-10×. These disparities reflect more than access to medical care — they reflect conditions of daily life.

This level combines two related threads: the social determinants of health (why health is unequal) and health economics (how to think about resources, interventions, and tradeoffs). Both push beyond medicine as it's delivered to individuals, toward the upstream factors that shape health for populations.

## The Social Determinants of Health

**Social determinants of health (SDH)** are the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age. WHO Commission on Social Determinants (2008) crystallized the framing:

- **Socioeconomic position**: income, wealth, education, occupation, social class.
- **Structural determinants**: governance, macroeconomic, social policies, cultural and societal values.
- **Intermediary determinants**: material circumstances, psychosocial factors, behavioral and biological factors, health system access.

Key patterns:
- **Income and wealth**: across every country, higher income correlates with longer life, better health. Gradient is continuous — not just poor vs. rich.
- **Education**: probably the strongest single predictor. Life expectancy gap ~5-15 years between those with less-than-high-school vs. college education in US.
- **Occupation**: hazardous vs. safe jobs, psychosocial stress, control over work.
- **Neighborhood**: pollution, violence, food access, walkability, housing quality.
- **Discrimination**: chronic stress from racism, sexism, homophobia correlates with hypertension, cardiovascular disease, preterm birth, and mental health outcomes.
- **Early childhood**: nutrition, stress, attachment, education in the first 5 years shape lifelong health.

Rose (1985, 2001): "The sick individuals and sick populations" — individual risk factors operate within a distribution determined by societal factors. Shifting the population distribution often has larger effects than treating high-risk individuals.

## Health Disparities

Specific disparities documented across countries:

**By income**: in the US, men in the top 1% live ~15 years longer than men in the bottom 1%. Similar gradients in most countries, with smaller magnitudes in those with stronger social safety nets.

**By race and ethnicity** (US data):
- **Black Americans**: life expectancy ~4-6 years shorter than white. Maternal mortality 3-4× higher. Infant mortality 2× higher. Higher rates of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, some cancers.
- **Hispanic**: complex picture. Overall life expectancy similar or higher than white (Hispanic paradox), but high rates of diabetes, obesity.
- **Native American / Alaska Native**: life expectancy 7-10 years shorter. Worst outcomes across most categories.
- **Asian American**: overall favorable but internal heterogeneity (Filipino, Vietnamese vs. Japanese, Korean).

**By gender**: women live longer globally (~4-7 years). Men have higher rates of accidents, suicide, cardiovascular mortality at younger ages. Maternal mortality affects women specifically; gender-based violence has major health impacts.

**By geography**: rural-urban differentials; neighborhood-level variation within cities. Life expectancy in Washington DC varies 30 years between wealthiest and poorest neighborhoods.

**By country**: global life expectancy ranges from ~50 years (Central African Republic, Chad, Lesotho) to ~85 years (Japan, Switzerland, Monaco). ~30-year gap at national level.

## Causes of Disparities

Multiple pathways link social position to health:

**Material pathway**: poverty directly produces exposures — worse housing, neighborhoods with more pollution, less access to healthy food, unsafe working conditions, less ability to afford care.

**Psychosocial pathway**: chronic stress from discrimination, economic insecurity, social hierarchy activates stress response systems repeatedly. Long-term effects on cardiovascular, metabolic, immune systems. Whitehall studies of British civil servants (Marmot) showed health gradient even within professional class, correlated with autonomy and control.

**Behavioral pathway**: risk behaviors (smoking, diet, physical inactivity, substance use) cluster by social position. But these behaviors are themselves shaped by environment, stress, marketing, and availability.

**Access to care**: unequal access exists even in systems with universal coverage (geographic, cultural, linguistic barriers; provider implicit bias).

**Life course**: disadvantages accumulate across life and across generations. Childhood adversity affects adult disease risk decades later. Disadvantage in one generation predicts disadvantage in next.

Causes are typically not monocausal. Addressing health disparities requires action on multiple pathways simultaneously.

## The Grossman Model and Health as Human Capital

**Grossman (1972)** model: health is a durable capital stock that produces "healthy time" useful for work and consumption. Individuals invest in health via time and money; health depreciates with age.

Implications:
- Health investments have return — lower medical expenses, more working years, better quality of life.
- Prevention is investment in capital stock; treatment maintains or repairs.
- Labor productivity, education attainment, lifetime earnings all correlate with health.
- Health investment pays off most when life expectancy is long enough for payoff period.

**Health and economic growth**: at macro level, healthier populations have higher GDP growth. Malaria elimination, vaccine programs, clean water have been engines of development historically. Reverse also true: growth generates resources for health investment.

Debates about causation direction are legitimate, but consensus that health and wealth are mutually reinforcing is strong.

## Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

How do public health officials decide where to spend limited resources?

**Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA)** quantifies cost per health gain. Key unit: **cost per QALY** (quality-adjusted life year).

**QALY** = years of life × quality adjustment (0 = dead, 1 = perfect health). A year in wheelchair-bound, pain-free state might weight 0.8; a year in dialysis 0.6; etc.

Interventions ranked by **incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER)**: extra cost / extra QALY vs. alternative.

**Thresholds**: NICE (UK) uses £20,000-30,000/QALY as cost-effectiveness threshold (higher for some exceptional cases). Many countries use 1-3× GDP per capita. US lacks explicit threshold but implicit around $50,000-150,000/QALY.

**Highly cost-effective interventions** (<$100/QALY globally):
- Childhood vaccines.
- Oral rehydration therapy for diarrhea.
- Salt iodization.
- Insecticide-treated bednets for malaria.
- Mass deworming (contested but low-cost).

**Moderately cost-effective**:
- Tobacco taxation.
- Hypertension treatment.
- Many cancer screenings in high-risk populations.

**Expensive interventions** requiring careful evaluation:
- Many cancer therapies ($100,000-500,000+/QALY).
- Gene therapies ($2M+ per treatment).
- ICU for multiple failing organs.

Ethical critiques of CEA: Does it devalue older people (fewer remaining years)? Does it embed disability discrimination (lower QALY weight for disabled)? Does it ignore equity? Methods exist to address these but are contested.

## The Economics of Prevention

Prevention is attractive in principle: avoid disease rather than treat it. But prevention requires upfront investment with delayed benefits, and most evaluation requires long time horizons.

**Types of prevention**:
- **Primary**: preventing onset of disease (vaccination, tobacco control, healthy eating).
- **Secondary**: early detection and treatment (screening).
- **Tertiary**: reducing complications of established disease (diabetes management, rehabilitation).

**When prevention saves money**:
- Childhood vaccinations: huge savings from avoided hospitalizations.
- Smoking cessation programs: negative ICER (saves money).
- Hypertension control: typically cost-saving over decades.

**When prevention adds cost for health benefit**:
- Many screening programs: find many false positives and indolent disease.
- Breast cancer screening: cost per QALY $20,000-80,000 typically.
- Prostate cancer screening: contested, uncertain net benefit.
- Colon cancer screening: fairly cost-effective.

**When prevention costs more than it saves** but may still be worth doing:
- Exercise promotion programs.
- Weight loss interventions.
- Mental health prevention.

Important: "prevention saves money" is sometimes true, sometimes false. Each intervention needs evaluation.

## Upstream vs. Downstream Investment

**Upstream interventions** address structural causes: education, housing, income, urban design, policy. **Downstream** target individuals with disease or risk.

Upstream evidence:
- **Housing first** (providing housing without prerequisites) reduces emergency care costs for homeless populations.
- **Income supplements / EITC**: associated with lower mortality, better child health.
- **Smoking bans in public places**: reduced heart attacks, cleaner air.
- **Sugar taxes**: reduce consumption, especially among youth and low-income.
- **Alcohol policy**: taxation, availability limits reduce harm.
- **Car safety regulations**: seatbelts, airbags, drunk driving laws save many lives.

Political economy: upstream interventions are harder politically because costs and benefits fall on different groups, benefits are diffuse and delayed, industries resist.

**Health in All Policies (HiAP)** framework: considering health impacts of policies in transport, housing, labor, agriculture, education, not just health department. Adopted by WHO, some governments (Finland historically, California, Wales); implementation variable.

## Behavioral Economics and Health

Classical economics assumes rational agents maximizing utility. Health behavior often looks anything but.

Behavioral economics findings:
- **Present bias**: heavily discounting future consequences. Smoking, eating, exercise decisions skewed.
- **Status quo bias**: default options heavily affect choices. Organ donation rates differ 5-10× between opt-in vs. opt-out systems.
- **Loss aversion**: losses hurt more than equivalent gains. Deposit contracts help with behavior change.
- **Social influence**: what peers do matters. Smoking spreads through social networks; so does quitting.

Applications:
- **Nudges**: default choices, reminders, framing. Effective for immunization rates, medication adherence, savings for medical care.
- **Commitment devices**: tools to bind future self to current goals.
- **Social norms messaging**: telling people what peers do.

Nudge approaches are modest-effect but often cheap. They complement, not replace, regulation and direct service provision.

## Healthcare's Share of Economic Activity

Healthcare has grown as share of GDP in nearly every country. US grew from ~5% GDP in 1960 to ~18% today. OECD average ~9-10%. Drivers:
- **Baumol's cost disease**: labor-intensive services get more expensive relative to goods as productivity rises elsewhere.
- **Technology**: new drugs, devices, procedures add cost (and often benefit).
- **Demographics**: aging populations consume more care.
- **Income elasticity**: richer societies spend more on health.

Concerns: Is growth sustainable? Where are returns diminishing? Is it crowding out other investments?

Answers: growth likely to moderate but not stop. Returns have been high for life expectancy and quality of life, though slowing. Prevention, social determinants investment, efficient care delivery are where more value seems available.

## Health and Inequality Measurement

**Concentration index**: Gini-like measure of how health outcome is distributed by socioeconomic rank.

**Slope index of inequality**: regression slope of outcome against socioeconomic position.

**Relative index of inequality**: ratio of outcomes at top vs. bottom of socioeconomic distribution.

**Decomposition**: attributing inequality to contributing factors (income, education, geography, etc.).

These tools enable quantitative comparison across countries and over time. Most countries saw declining health inequality through mid-20th century, some see increases in recent decades (US especially, UK more recently).

## Why This Level Matters

Health equity and health economics together frame the biggest questions in health policy: what should a society spend on health, how should it be distributed, and what are the upstream investments that actually produce health?

Every health system makes these decisions, explicitly or not. Systems that grapple with them systematically — UK NICE, Scandinavian systems with robust welfare states, some low-income countries with strong public health focus — tend to get more health per dollar than systems that don't. The US system, despite extraordinary spending, achieves moderate outcomes with severe inequality. Poland, Portugal, Costa Rica achieve near-US life expectancy at fraction of cost.

The frameworks and evidence are available. Applying them requires political will to confront distributional tradeoffs, industry interests, and ideological preferences. That's the harder part.
