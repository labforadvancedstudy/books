*Where everyone meets education: by being sent there*

## The Decade (Plus) You Spent

If you grew up in a rich country, you spent roughly 13 years of your life in school before age 18. If you went to college, add 4 more. If you went to graduate school, add 2-8 more. Before first grade, you were probably in preschool for 1-2 years.

At a rough average, a modern educated adult has spent 15-20 years inside educational institutions. That is a quarter to a third of an 80-year life. Nothing else, except sleep, occupies that fraction of a life.

This is a very recent fact about humans. In 1900, most people in most countries did 4-8 years of school at most. In 1800, most people did zero. Universal schooling is a 20th century invention.

## The Building You Remember

A modern school is a building with rooms, desks, and a schedule. A teacher is in front. Students face the teacher. There are bells. There are hallways. There are grades, report cards, standardized tests, parent-teacher conferences, a principal, maybe a cafeteria, maybe a gym.

This shape is surprisingly uniform across countries. It is also a 19th-century Prussian design, later imitated by most countries because it solved the problem of educating a lot of kids at low cost. Whether it's the best design for learning is a separate question from why it won.

## The Thing That's Actually Happening

Strip away the building and the ceremony, and education is trying to do several different things at once:

1. **Transmit knowledge** — facts, skills, methods that took humanity millennia to accumulate.
2. **Develop thinking** — how to reason, argue, test claims, compute, write, read critically.
3. **Socialize** — cooperation, conflict, hierarchy, friendship, romantic relationships at close quarters for years.
4. **Sort** — signal to employers and society who can do what.
5. **Babysit** — keep kids in a defined space while parents work.
6. **Instill values** — citizenship, ethics, national identity.

These goals conflict. Pure knowledge transmission might prefer MOOCs. Pure socialization might prefer summer camps. Pure sorting might prefer standardized testing. Actual schools compromise among all six, usually imperfectly.

## The Thing Learning Science Knows

The research on how humans actually learn has converged on a few robust findings:

- **Spacing**: Spreading study over time beats cramming, substantially.
- **Testing effect**: Retrieving information is stronger learning than rereading it.
- **Interleaving**: Mixing problem types beats blocking them.
- **Feedback**: Immediate, specific feedback > delayed or vague.
- **Elaboration**: Connecting new material to what you already know.
- **Expertise reversal**: What helps novices often hurts experts, and vice versa.
- **Motivation and meaning**: Students who see why something matters learn more.

Most of these have been known for decades. Most schools use them inconsistently. This is not because teachers don't know; it is because systems optimized for "cover curriculum and produce test scores" don't give teachers the slack to redesign practice around learning science.

## The Test You Took

You took standardized tests. Your score on one of them may have meaningfully affected what university you attended, what job you got, how others saw you.

Standardized tests measure something. They correlate with subsequent academic and professional success — not perfectly, not deterministically, but consistently. They also correlate with family income, test preparation access, and other variables that complicate what they "really" mean. Good educators neither dismiss standardized tests nor treat their scores as the full measure of a person.

The test industry is huge (SAT, ACT, GRE, TOEFL, PISA, TIMSS at international scale). Whether this scale is proportionate to the signal quality is a genuinely contested question. The US has been inching away from SAT requirements at top universities over the past decade; this may prove wise or unwise, and we won't know for 20 years.

## The Credential You Hold

A degree is a credential. It signals to employers that you were able to complete a multi-year structured program, which is a real signal about conscientiousness, cognitive ability, and domain knowledge. It also signals socioeconomic access — you had the resources and runway to finish.

Credentials have been getting more expensive, especially in the US, where college costs have risen roughly 3x inflation since 1980. Student debt is over $1.7 trillion. Whether the credential is worth the debt is increasingly dependent on field and institution. Some fields (engineering, computer science, medicine, nursing) show clear wage returns. Others do not, or do so much more weakly.

This is part of the L8 conversation. It is also why alternative credentials (certificates, bootcamps, direct apprenticeship) are growing.

## The Teacher Who Shaped You

Somewhere in your 15-20 years of education there was probably at least one teacher who mattered to you more than the others. Who saw something in you, pushed you, believed in you at a hard moment. That person's effect on your trajectory was probably larger than any particular curriculum.

This is another robust finding: teacher quality matters more than almost anything else in a student's experience. Good teachers measurably shift later outcomes (earnings, college completion) even when curriculum is held constant. Identifying, training, retaining, and paying good teachers is the single most leveraged investment in any school system. Most systems do this poorly.

## The Question That Keeps Getting Asked

Is our education system preparing the next generation for the world they'll inhabit? The honest answer is "probably not well enough." Climate change, AI automation, shifting geopolitics, longer lifespans, changing family structures — these are the defining challenges of people now in elementary school, and curricula were mostly designed for a stabler world.

Education reform is chronic and mostly incremental. Dramatic successes (Finland, Singapore, Korea in different ways) exist. So do dramatic failures. The field is less a science of "here's the right way" and more a craft of "here's what seems to work in this context."

## The First Lesson

Education is the civilization's attempt to transmit itself to the next generation. It is imperfect, expensive, slow, full of compromises, and nonetheless one of the most consequential institutional activities humans undertake.

You are both a product of education and — if you teach, parent, mentor, write, or explain anything to anyone — a participant in it. Everyone teaches someone, eventually.
