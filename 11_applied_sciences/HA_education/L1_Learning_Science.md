# Level 1: Learning Science
*What we actually know about how people learn*

## The Gap Between Intuition and Evidence

Most people's beliefs about how learning works are wrong. Not maliciously wrong — just contradicted by the evidence, usually because the real mechanisms operate below conscious awareness and because the things that feel like effective learning are often not.

Some examples of intuitions that lose to evidence:

- "I learn best by rereading." The evidence: rereading is among the least effective study strategies. It feels productive because the text becomes familiar, but familiarity isn't the same as mastery.
- "Learning should feel smooth — if I'm struggling, I'm not doing it right." The evidence: productive struggle is a signature of learning. Easy learning often means shallow learning.
- "Everyone has their own learning style (visual, auditory, kinesthetic) and should be taught in that style." The evidence: studies have repeatedly failed to find that matching instruction to preferred style improves outcomes. The "learning styles" framework is mostly a myth.
- "Massed practice (cramming) is efficient for exams." The evidence: cramming can pass the exam but fails weeks later. Spaced practice retains the material.
- "Teaching the same thing multiple times helps." The evidence: yes, if the teachings are spaced out and mix related but different examples. No, if they are massed or identical.

Learning science is the discipline that measures what actually works and why. It has made substantial progress in the last 50 years.

## The Well-Established Principles

A handful of principles hold up consistently across many experiments and population subgroups.

**Retrieval practice** (also called active recall or the testing effect): pulling information from memory, with effort, strengthens future retrieval. Flashcards, closed-book practice problems, and low-stakes quizzes outperform rereading and passive review by large margins. The effect is robust across age groups, subject matter, and skill levels.

**Spacing**: distributed practice over time outperforms massed practice in the same total time. A student who studies 1 hour per day for 10 days will remember more than a student who studies 10 hours in a single session. The effect persists over weeks, months, and years — the forgetting curve is flatter for spaced study.

**Interleaving**: mixing different but related problem types within a study session outperforms blocking one type at a time. A student doing mixed practice problems (algebra + geometry + statistics) will show better transfer than one doing 20 algebra problems, then 20 geometry, then 20 statistics. This feels harder and performance on individual problems may be worse during practice — and yet long-term learning and transfer are better.

**Elaboration**: connecting new information to existing knowledge and to itself. Asking "why is this so?" and "how does this connect to what I already know?" produces deeper encoding than rote memorization.

**Worked examples**: especially for novices, studying fully worked examples before attempting problems is more efficient than pure problem-solving practice. This is the **worked-example effect**, and it reverses at higher expertise levels where problem-solving transfer dominates (the **expertise reversal effect**).

**Dual coding**: combining verbal and visual representations of the same concept improves learning. Text plus a well-designed diagram beats text alone for most concepts.

**Feedback**: specific, corrective feedback (what's wrong and why) outperforms mere correctness feedback. Timing matters less than quality; immediate and delayed feedback both work if the content is useful.

These principles are summarized in works like Brown, Roediger, and McDaniel's *Make It Stick* and the How People Learn series of National Academies reports. They are well-replicated in both lab and classroom studies.

## Memory

Cognitive psychology distinguishes several memory systems, each with distinct properties:

- **Sensory memory**: milliseconds. Raw perceptual buffer (the "iconic memory" for vision, echoic for sound).
- **Working memory**: seconds to roughly a minute. Limited capacity — traditionally 7±2 items; more recent research suggests 3–4 chunks of information. This is the bottleneck of active thinking.
- **Long-term memory**: minutes to a lifetime. Two major subdivisions:
  - **Declarative memory**: facts and events, further split into semantic (general knowledge) and episodic (personal experience).
  - **Procedural memory**: skills, habits, motor sequences. Separately stored; often retained even when declarative memory fails (e.g., amnesic patients can still ride bikes).

The transition from working to long-term memory requires **encoding effort**. Information that's merely maintained in working memory (e.g., a phone number held long enough to dial) often doesn't transfer. Information actively processed, related to existing knowledge, and retrieved with effort does transfer.

**Forgetting** is not the memory being erased but access becoming harder. Cues that reliably retrieved the memory once may fail later. Well-consolidated memories can last a lifetime; fragile new encodings decay quickly.

## Expertise and Transfer

Expert performance differs from novice performance in ways that are now well-characterized:

- **Domain-specific**: a chess master is a chess master, not a generic genius.
- **Pattern recognition**: experts see chunks and configurations where novices see isolated details. The classic chess studies (de Groot, 1946; Chase and Simon, 1973) showed masters could reconstruct game positions from brief glimpses because they recognized meaningful patterns.
- **Schema-driven**: experts organize knowledge hierarchically, which frees working memory for higher-level reasoning.
- **Automated sub-skills**: routine operations are unconscious, releasing capacity for novel problems.
- **Deliberate practice**: experts continue to improve through effortful, feedback-rich practice targeting specific weaknesses — not just total hours. Ericsson's research on violinists, chess players, and athletes formalized this.

**Transfer** — applying what you learned in one context to a different context — is famously difficult. Learning algebra in a math class does not automatically transfer to applying algebra in physics. Teaching for transfer requires varied practice contexts, explicit discussion of abstract principles, and bridging between examples.

The corollary: generic "critical thinking skills" courses have modest effects, because transfer is weak. Critical thinking in one domain uses domain-specific knowledge. Teaching it general-purpose is less effective than embedding it in specific content.

## Motivation and Engagement

Learning requires attention and effort. Motivation is the reason learners bring those things.

Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan) identifies three psychological needs whose satisfaction produces intrinsic motivation: autonomy (feeling of choice), competence (feeling of mastery), and relatedness (feeling connected). Learning environments that support these produce deeper engagement than those that don't.

Extrinsic motivators (grades, rewards, punishments) work to some extent but have well-documented limits:
- They can crowd out intrinsic motivation for already-interesting tasks.
- They drive surface compliance rather than deep learning (studying to pass the test, not to understand).
- They lose effectiveness when removed.

In practice, effective learning environments combine enough structure and accountability (to produce effortful practice) with enough autonomy, challenge, and social connection (to sustain engagement). Pure self-directed learning is rare. Pure compliance-based learning is shallow.

## What Developmental Age Does

Children's cognitive systems mature on a timeline. Some abilities develop with time and experience regardless of explicit teaching:

- **Early childhood** (0–5): massive language acquisition, motor skills, basic social cognition. These don't need formal schooling; they emerge with rich environment.
- **Primary school age** (6–11): concrete operational thinking, literacy, numeracy. Explicit instruction is highly effective for these.
- **Adolescence** (12–18): abstract reasoning, metacognition, identity formation. Executive function and emotional regulation are still maturing — the prefrontal cortex doesn't reach maturity until the mid-20s.
- **Adult learning**: strong prior knowledge and metacognitive strategies; motivation and time are the limiting factors, not cognitive capacity.

This means instruction has to match developmental capability. You can't productively teach abstract calculus to a 7-year-old. You can't productively teach arithmetic from pictorial primers to a 17-year-old. What worked for Piaget's stages is mostly still right in broad strokes, with revisions on timing and on the role of social context (Vygotsky).

## What Schools Can and Cannot Do

Schools are not the only place learning happens. Children learn language at home, motor skills from play, social skills from peers. Schooling adds:

- Explicit instruction in abstract topics (reading, math beyond basic counting, history, science).
- Practice time with feedback.
- Credentialing that signals to employers and further education.
- Social sorting (which is sometimes the main effect).

The evidence on school effects: well-taught schools matter, but family background matters more in statistical aggregate. This is not a counsel of despair about schools — intensive, high-quality tutoring and effective small-group instruction can produce measurable gains equivalent to many months of schooling. But it tempers expectations about what schools alone can fix in societies with large underlying inequalities.

**Cross-national comparisons** (PISA, TIMSS) show that some systems consistently outperform others. High performers (Finland, Japan, Korea, Singapore, Poland) have varied pedagogies but share: respected teachers, coherent curricula, adequate resources, and relatively low socioeconomic stratification within schools. There is no single model.

## Why This Level Matters

Education consumes about 5–6% of global GDP — a trillion dollars in the US alone. It shapes every downstream outcome: employment, health, civic life, economic growth. Yet most instructional practice is built on intuition, tradition, and marketing, not on evidence.

Learning science doesn't replace teachers or eliminate the art of teaching, but it does narrow the space of plausibly effective approaches. Knowing that retrieval, spacing, and interleaving work — and that rereading and learning-style matching don't — is a small number of facts that can substantially change study habits and instructional design.
