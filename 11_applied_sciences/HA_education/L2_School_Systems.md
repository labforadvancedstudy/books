# Level 2: School Systems
*How education is organized — governance, funding, curriculum, and assessment*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## Beyond Learning

Level 1 covered **how individuals learn**. This level covers **how societies organize learning** at scale — the institutional structures that take millions of children and ensure (or fail to ensure) they acquire literacy, numeracy, knowledge, and skills over a dozen-plus years.

School systems differ radically across countries. A Finnish kindergartner, a Japanese middle schooler, a Kenyan high school student, and an Indian university applicant all inhabit very different institutional realities. Some of the differences reflect culture and history; some reflect policy choices still in play. Understanding school systems — their structures, trade-offs, and patterns of success and failure — is essential for anyone who wants to reform, choose among, or understand education.

## Levels of Education

Most countries organize education in bands:

- **Early childhood / preschool** (ages 3–5 or 3–6): varies from informal care to structured programs. Universal preschool is standard in France (maternelle), Scandinavia; emerging elsewhere.
- **Primary / elementary** (ages 6–11 or 6–13): basic literacy, numeracy, introductory social and natural science. Near-universal attendance globally now.
- **Lower secondary** (ages 11–14 or 12–15): academic subjects plus broadening. Universal in most countries.
- **Upper secondary** (ages 15–18): academic track, vocational track, or both. Completion rates vary widely.
- **Tertiary / higher education** (ages 18+): universities, colleges, vocational institutions, technical schools.
- **Adult / continuing education**: lifelong learning of various kinds.

Tracking — separating students into academic and vocational streams — happens at different ages. Germany tracks early (age ~10, though reforms have softened this); Japan and the US track implicitly later through course choice and selective entrance. Early tracking cements social class; late tracking allows later-developing students to change course but may push students through without appropriate specialization.

## Governance Structures

Who runs schools varies by country:

- **Centralized systems**: national curriculum, national assessments, heavy central funding and oversight. France, Japan, Korea, China, most of Latin America. Uniformity is the strength; responsiveness to local needs the weakness.
- **Decentralized systems**: local or regional authority over schools. US (states and local districts), Germany (Länder), Australia (states), Switzerland (cantons). Local responsiveness is the strength; inequality is the weakness.
- **Hybrid**: national standards, local implementation. UK (since devolution to Scotland/Wales/NI), Canada (provincial authority with federal influence).

The US is particularly fragmented: ~13,000 school districts, heavy local property-tax funding, state-by-state variation in standards and assessments, limited federal role. The result is huge quality variation — among the best and the worst public schools in the developed world exist within 50 km of each other.

## Funding

How much a society spends on education, and how that money is distributed, shapes everything else.

OECD averages (~2023): ~5% of GDP on public education. High spenders (Norway, Korea, Israel) ~6–7%; low spenders (Japan, Italy, Ireland) ~4%. As a share of total public spending, education typically runs 10–15%.

Within countries, funding inequality matters more than aggregate spending. In the US, per-pupil spending ranges from ~$8,000 to ~$25,000+ across districts, driven mainly by local property taxes. State funding formulas partially equalize but substantial disparities remain. New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut spend heavily; Utah, Mississippi, and Idaho spend much less.

In Germany, France, Scandinavia, and most other rich countries, schools are funded nationally or regionally with less intra-country variation. Equalization is a policy choice; the US has chosen not to fully make it.

Within schools, specific factors matter:
- **Teacher salaries**: the largest cost. High-paying systems (Switzerland, Germany, Korea) attract stronger candidates.
- **Class size**: smaller classes marginally help, especially in early grades.
- **Infrastructure**: functional schools matter for morale and learning.
- **Instructional materials**: textbooks, technology, libraries.

Spending more doesn't automatically produce better outcomes (Luxembourg has the highest per-pupil spending but mid-tier PISA scores), but systematically underfunded schools systematically underperform.

## Curriculum

What students learn is determined by curriculum decisions. Who makes those decisions varies:

- **National curriculum**: France, UK (England), Japan, South Korea, most European countries. Detailed standards, often with prescribed textbooks.
- **State / regional curriculum**: US (mostly state-driven through standards), Australia, Canada (provincial), Germany (Länder), Mexico (federal with state variation).
- **Local curriculum**: traditionally common in US but reduced by state standards and testing; some private schools remain local.

Recent curriculum trends:
- **Common Core State Standards** (US): mostly adopted but implementation uneven; politically contested.
- **STEM emphasis**: more math, science, computing in most developed-country curricula.
- **Reading instruction debates**: phonics vs. whole language; "science of reading" movement pushing back toward systematic phonics after decades of other approaches.
- **Computational thinking and computer science**: increasingly in primary as well as secondary curricula.
- **Financial literacy**: slowly spreading in secondary.
- **Mental health and social-emotional learning**: expanded post-pandemic, contested in some places.
- **Civics and history**: chronically neglected in some systems; actively contested in others.

Curriculum fights often reflect deeper political conflicts. What's in the history textbook, how sex education is handled, whether creationism is taught, which literature is required — these surface in state legislatures and school boards precisely because they are proxies for larger debates.

## Assessment

Testing serves multiple functions:

- **Formative assessment**: teacher checks understanding to adjust teaching. Low-stakes, continuous, most important for learning.
- **Summative assessment**: evaluates student learning at end of unit, course, or grade.
- **High-stakes testing**: determines promotion, graduation, college admission, or school accountability.
- **International assessments**: PISA (age 15), TIMSS (grades 4 and 8), PIRLS (grade 4) enable cross-national comparison.

The US has been a testing-heavy system since the 2001 No Child Left Behind Act, with mixed effects. Benefits: focused attention on struggling students, forced disaggregation of data by subgroup revealing achievement gaps. Costs: teaching to the test, curriculum narrowing, cheating scandals, teacher demoralization.

High-stakes college admissions testing (SAT, ACT in the US; Gaokao in China; CSAT in Korea; Centre tests in Japan; A-levels in UK; Baccalauréat in France) shape secondary education heavily. Gaokao in particular — a single two- or three-day exam that largely determines university placement for ~12 million Chinese students a year — produces extreme preparation culture that starts in primary school.

International comparisons (PISA, TIMSS) have become influential. Finland's early PISA success in the 2000s made it a policy tourism destination. East Asian dominance (Singapore, Korea, Japan, China) has made their systems reference points. These comparisons have limits — PISA measures what PISA measures, not all of education's purposes — but the rankings have genuine influence.

## Teachers

The most important variable in student learning (after family background) is teacher quality. Teacher-related policy issues:

- **Preparation**: how much university + clinical training. Finland requires a master's degree; many US states require only a bachelor's plus certification. Most European and East Asian systems have substantial initial training.
- **Selection**: how competitively are teaching candidates drawn from the applicant pool? Finland, Korea, Singapore draw teachers from the top fraction of graduates; many systems draw from broader ranges.
- **Compensation**: absolute pay, relative to other professions, and career-long salary progression. Teachers are well-paid in Germany, Switzerland, Korea; middling in the US; underpaid in many developing countries.
- **Working conditions**: class size, prep time, administrative burden, respect, autonomy. Japanese teachers spend much less time directly teaching than US teachers; their day is split across preparation, coaching, and collaboration.
- **Professional development**: ongoing training, peer observation, coaching. Best systems build it into the structure; worst systems provide token PD days.

Teacher shortages are common in many systems, especially in math, science, special education, and in high-poverty or rural areas. Retention is often as much a problem as recruitment — US new-teacher attrition in the first five years is ~50%.

Teacher unions are strong in most of the developed world. They affect salaries, benefits, working conditions, and tenure systems. They've been constructive partners in some reform efforts and obstacles in others. Blanket positions for or against unions miss the more substantive questions about what specifically they bargain for.

## Inequality and Achievement Gaps

Educational systems reproduce social inequality, often dramatically. Achievement gaps by family income, race, language background, immigration status, and rural/urban location are documented across systems and persistent over time.

US data: the gap between low-income and high-income students on PISA is among the largest in the OECD, though not the widest. Within-country gaps are often larger than between-country differences.

Causes are complex:
- **Pre-school differences**: vocabulary, early literacy, executive function differ at kindergarten entry.
- **Summer learning loss**: affluent kids have enriching summers; low-income kids regress.
- **School quality**: low-income kids concentrated in lower-resource, higher-turnover schools.
- **Out-of-school time**: tutoring, extracurriculars, travel, exposure.
- **Family stability and parental involvement**.
- **Health and nutrition**.

Interventions that work: high-quality preschool (Perry Preschool, Abecedarian Project), reduced class size in early grades, structured literacy, high-dosage tutoring, extended learning time for disadvantaged students. Effect sizes are modest and cumulative — no single intervention closes large gaps.

The US and UK have large within-country inequality. Scandinavian countries, Japan, and Canada have smaller gaps, partly reflecting smaller income inequality and stronger universal services.

## Higher Education

Tertiary education has expanded dramatically — from ~10% of young people globally in 1970 to ~40% today. Structures vary:

- **Universal, tuition-free**: Germany, Nordic countries, some others. Access is broad, credentials are less stratified.
- **Tuition-based with aid**: US, UK, Australia, Korea, Japan. Diverse institutions; expensive; stratified by selectivity.
- **Mixed public-private**: most countries, with public universities dominant in some and private expanding in others.

Major issues:
- **Cost**: US tuition has risen faster than inflation for decades. Student debt ~$1.75 trillion.
- **Graduation rates**: highly variable. Top-tier universities ~90%+; some institutions ~30%.
- **Returns**: a college degree raises lifetime earnings substantially on average, but variation is large by field, institution, and individual.
- **Signaling vs. human capital**: some economists argue much of college's payoff is signaling rather than skills learned. The answer is probably "both, in varying proportions."
- **Vocational/technical alternatives**: strong in Germany (apprenticeships), Switzerland, Austria; weak in the US; varied elsewhere.
- **For-profit higher education**: often predatory, with poor outcomes for students. Major regulatory concern in US.

## Reform Dynamics

School systems are notoriously hard to reform. Reasons:

- **Many stakeholders**: teachers, parents, students, administrators, unions, elected officials, advocacy groups, academics.
- **Outcomes lag**: interventions today may not show results for years.
- **Measurement challenges**: what you measure shapes what gets taught.
- **Cultural embedding**: schools reflect and reinforce cultural patterns.
- **Career-long commitments**: teachers trained decades ago still teach; curriculum changes take a generation to fully work through.

Reform successes in various countries: Finland's teacher professionalization (1970s–90s), Korea's universal secondary expansion, Poland's post-transition reforms (substantial PISA gains), Estonia's digital education transformation. Failures are also common — high-visibility reforms that produced little or reversed.

## Why This Level Matters

A country's long-term economic prospects, social mobility, and cultural vitality depend on its schools. Most individual adult life outcomes — earnings, health, civic engagement — correlate strongly with educational attainment. Education is the single largest discretionary public investment in most developed countries.

Understanding how school systems are structured, funded, and held accountable is essential for any serious engagement with public policy, parenting, or community life. It's also a prerequisite for reform: most failed reforms failed from not understanding the system they were trying to change.
