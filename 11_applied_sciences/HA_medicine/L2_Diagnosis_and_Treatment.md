# Level 2: Diagnosis and Treatment
*From symptoms to decisions — how modern medicine actually works*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Clinical Encounter

Medicine happens in clinical encounters. A patient walks in with a complaint. A clinician listens, examines, orders tests, interprets results, forms a diagnosis, recommends treatment, and follows up. That sequence — repeated billions of times a year globally — is where scientific knowledge meets individual suffering.

Every step has its own methodology, its characteristic errors, and its accumulated experience. L1 covered the underlying anatomy and physiology; L2 is about the decision process itself.

## History and Physical

The oldest diagnostic tools remain the most powerful: **history** (what the patient tells you) and **physical examination** (what you observe directly).

A thorough history covers:

- **Chief complaint**: the main reason for the visit, in the patient's own words.
- **History of present illness**: onset, character, timing, aggravating/alleviating factors, associated symptoms.
- **Past medical history**: prior illnesses, surgeries, hospitalizations.
- **Medications and allergies**: including over-the-counter, supplements, and alcohol/tobacco/substance use.
- **Family history**: conditions in close relatives.
- **Social history**: occupation, living situation, travel, exposures, sexual history, stress, diet, exercise.
- **Review of systems**: systematic survey for symptoms beyond the chief complaint.

Studies repeatedly find that history alone leads to the correct diagnosis in 60–80% of cases. The physical exam confirms, localizes, or narrows; tests usually refine what history and exam already suggest.

A good clinician spends more time on history than on exam or test review. A rushed history produces downstream testing cascades, misdiagnoses, and frustrated patients. Time spent here is leveraged through the rest of the encounter.

## Clinical Reasoning

Diagnosis is probabilistic reasoning under uncertainty. Formal and informal frameworks help:

**Illness scripts**: experienced clinicians recognize patterns — "a 55-year-old smoker with substernal chest pressure, diaphoresis, and left arm radiation" triggers the myocardial infarction script immediately. Pattern recognition is fast and often correct, but fails on atypical presentations.

**Differential diagnosis**: listing possible diagnoses for the given presentation, ranked by likelihood and by seriousness if missed. A common mnemonic: "what's most likely? what's most dangerous? what's most treatable? what else could it be?"

**Bayesian reasoning**: each piece of information (symptom, sign, test result) shifts the probability of each diagnosis. The clinician's prior probability (what's likely given age, sex, setting) is updated by each finding.

**Cognitive biases**: the clinician's reasoning is susceptible to anchoring (locking on to the first diagnosis), availability bias (overweighting recently seen cases), confirmation bias (seeking data that supports the current hypothesis), premature closure (accepting a diagnosis too early). Good clinicians deliberately consider alternatives, welcome disconfirming data, and know when to step back.

Teaching clinical reasoning is hard. Textbook knowledge is necessary but not sufficient. Most clinical education is apprenticeship — cases seen with supervision, feedback on reasoning, increasing independence over years.

## Diagnostic Testing

Tests refine diagnosis. They are not neutral — every test has sensitivity, specificity, and a cost.

- **Sensitivity**: probability that the test is positive if the disease is present. A sensitive test doesn't miss the disease.
- **Specificity**: probability that the test is negative if the disease is absent. A specific test doesn't falsely flag disease.
- **Positive predictive value**: probability of disease given a positive test. Depends on prevalence.
- **Negative predictive value**: probability of no disease given a negative test. Depends on prevalence.

A highly sensitive test is good for **ruling out** disease when negative. A highly specific test is good for **ruling in** disease when positive. No test is perfect; most tradeoffs sensitivity against specificity.

**Threshold** for ordering a test: if the test won't change management, don't order it. Low-value tests (routine preoperative screening in healthy patients, imaging for most back pain, annual electrocardiograms in low-risk adults) generate noise, cost, and false positives without improving outcomes.

Modern diagnostics span:

- **Laboratory tests**: blood chemistry, hematology, microbiology cultures, immunoassays, genetic tests.
- **Imaging**: X-ray, ultrasound, CT, MRI, nuclear medicine, PET. Each with indications, risks (radiation for X-ray/CT, contrast for many), and cost.
- **Electrophysiology**: ECG, EEG, EMG.
- **Endoscopy and biopsy**: direct visualization and tissue sampling.
- **Genetic and molecular tests**: increasingly central in oncology, rare disease, infectious disease.

Test ordering has grown steadily — too much in many systems. Over-testing raises cost, produces false positives requiring workup, and occasionally causes direct harm. Under-testing misses diagnoses or delays them. The balance is a clinical judgment, not an algorithm.

## Imaging

Modern imaging changed diagnosis beyond recognition.

**X-ray**: 1895. Still the workhorse for chest (pneumonia, heart failure, pneumothorax) and orthopedic imaging (fractures). Cheap, fast, moderate radiation.

**Ultrasound**: 1940s onward. Real-time, no radiation, portable, moderate cost. Dominant for obstetrics (fetal imaging), cardiology (echocardiography), abdominal organs, vascular imaging, and point-of-care (trauma, procedure guidance). Operator-dependent.

**CT (computed tomography)**: 1970s onward. Cross-sectional X-ray with computational reconstruction. Fast, detailed, widely available. Workhorse for trauma, acute abdominal pain, pulmonary embolism, stroke (modern CT with perfusion and angiography), and oncologic staging. Radiation exposure a concern for young patients and repeated studies.

**MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)**: 1980s onward. Uses magnetic fields and radio waves; no ionizing radiation. Excellent soft-tissue contrast. Workhorse for neurological, musculoskeletal, pelvic, and cardiac imaging. Slower, noisier, more expensive than CT. Contraindicated in some patients with metallic implants or claustrophobia.

**Nuclear medicine** (radiotracers): functional imaging. Bone scans, cardiac stress tests, thyroid imaging. PET (positron emission tomography) combines with CT or MRI for metabolic/functional imaging — major role in oncology staging.

**Interventional radiology**: image-guided procedures that were previously surgical. Drainage of abscesses, biopsies, stent placement, embolization, tumor ablation. A major shift in minimally invasive care over the past three decades.

## Pharmacotherapy

Most medical treatment involves **drugs**. The modern pharmacopoeia includes thousands of compounds across classes:

- **Antibiotics**: penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, and many more. Resistance spreading; stewardship essential.
- **Cardiovascular**: antihypertensives (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, diuretics), antiplatelets, anticoagulants, lipid-lowering drugs (statins, etc.), antiarrhythmics.
- **Diabetes**: insulin (multiple types), metformin, sulfonylureas, GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors.
- **Pain and inflammation**: NSAIDs, acetaminophen, opioids, corticosteroids.
- **Psychiatric**: antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, others), antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, anxiolytics, stimulants.
- **Oncology**: traditional chemotherapy, targeted therapies, immunotherapies — a rapidly changing field.
- **Biologics**: monoclonal antibodies, fusion proteins. Major impact in autoimmune disease, oncology, rare genetic disease.

Drug effects are rarely binary. Each has dose-response relationships, side effects, interactions with other drugs, and individual variability (some of it genetic — **pharmacogenomics**). A sensible prescription accounts for the patient's other conditions, other medications, preferences, and ability to adhere.

Adverse drug events are common — one of the most frequent causes of preventable harm in healthcare. Elderly patients on many medications (polypharmacy) are especially vulnerable. Medication reconciliation at every care transition is a major patient-safety practice.

## Procedures and Surgery

When medication isn't enough, **procedures** and **surgery** are the options.

Minor procedures done in outpatient settings: skin biopsies, joint injections, lumbar punctures, chest tube insertion, central line placement. Most require local anesthesia; many can be done in clinic.

Major surgery falls into categories:

- **Emergency surgery**: trauma, ruptured abdomen, acute vascular, obstetric emergencies. Done in hours.
- **Urgent surgery**: oncologic resections, cardiac interventions, symptomatic conditions. Done in days to weeks.
- **Elective surgery**: joint replacements, hernia repairs, bariatric, plastic/reconstructive. Scheduled.

**Minimally invasive approaches**: laparoscopy (small incisions with camera and instruments), endoscopy (through natural orifices), robotics (surgeon-controlled robotic arms). Over the past 30 years, many procedures have shifted from open to minimally invasive with faster recovery and equivalent or better outcomes.

**Surgical quality**: determined by surgeon skill, hospital volume, team function, and patient factors. Major quality indicators include mortality, morbidity, reoperation rate, infection rate, and length of stay. Better-performing centers have 2–4× lower complication rates than worst-performing centers for complex procedures — a huge, often invisible variation.

## Evidence-Based Medicine

Medicine has moved toward decisions grounded in formal evidence — **evidence-based medicine (EBM)**.

The hierarchy:
1. **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** of randomized trials.
2. **Randomized controlled trials** (RCTs).
3. **Cohort studies**.
4. **Case-control studies**.
5. **Case series and case reports**.
6. **Expert opinion** and mechanistic reasoning.

RCTs are the gold standard for causal inference: randomization averages out known and unknown confounders between treatment and control groups. But RCTs have limitations — expensive, short-duration, narrow inclusion criteria, may not generalize to real-world patients.

**Clinical guidelines** synthesize evidence into recommendations. Professional societies publish guidelines regularly — for heart disease, diabetes, cancer screening, immunization, and thousands of other topics. Good guidelines distinguish strong recommendations from weak ones and grade the underlying evidence.

EBM has limitations: many questions have limited evidence; individual patients may differ from study populations; clinical judgment remains necessary; overreliance on guidelines can become defensive medicine rather than personalized care. The ideal is well-informed clinical judgment — not algorithmic rote application, and not clinical gestalt untethered from data.

## Shared Decision-Making

The paternalistic model — "doctor knows best, patient follows" — has largely given way to **shared decision-making**, especially for decisions with significant tradeoffs.

Core elements: the clinician conveys what is known and uncertain; the patient shares values and preferences; together they decide. For procedures with different options (e.g., treatment choices in early prostate cancer, screening decisions), or with significant side effects, shared decision-making is now standard practice.

**Decision aids** — pamphlets, videos, interactive tools — help patients understand options and quantify tradeoffs. Evidence shows decision aids increase knowledge and reduce decisional conflict without systematically changing care choices.

The limit: emergency situations and patients who prefer paternalism. The clinician's role adapts to context.

## Chronic Disease Management

Acute care — diagnosing and treating discrete illnesses — is the classic model. Most of modern medicine is now **chronic disease management**: long-term care for hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, COPD, mental illness, cancer, autoimmune disease.

Chronic disease management involves:
- **Monitoring**: regular measurements (blood pressure, HbA1c, symptoms, function).
- **Medication adjustment**: titrating to targets.
- **Behavior change**: diet, exercise, medication adherence, sometimes difficult life changes.
- **Care coordination**: across specialists, pharmacists, home health, caregivers.
- **Exacerbation management**: handling flare-ups without hospitalization where possible.

Effective chronic disease management depends on continuity — a clinician who knows the patient over time. Systems built around one-time encounters fail chronic disease care. The shift to **primary care**, **team-based care**, and **population health** reflects this reality.

## Why This Level Matters

Understanding how medicine actually makes diagnostic and therapeutic decisions clarifies what medicine can and cannot do, where it excels and where it fails. Every patient encounter is an applied exercise in probability, communication, and evidence interpretation — not the clean algorithm that textbooks or TV suggest.

Patients and policymakers who understand the structure of clinical decision-making can advocate for what matters: more thinking time per encounter, better diagnostic testing at the point of care, continuity rather than fragmentation, evidence-based judgment rather than defensive over-testing.
