# L4 — Medical Specialties and Emerging Therapies

Level 3 surveyed clinical medicine as an integrated practice: diagnosis, pharmacology, surgery, pathology, and evidence-based care. Level 4 descends into the specialty landscape and examines the therapeutic revolution underway. Modern medicine is organized around specialties whose tools, evidence bases, and patient populations differ enormously. The past two decades have seen emergence of therapeutic modalities — targeted biologics, cell and gene therapies, immunotherapies, precision medicine — that operate on principles unknown to classical pharmacology. Understanding these developments matters for every dimension of health policy, practice, and equity.

## The Structure of Medical Specialization

Medical specialties emerged from 19th and 20th century scientific developments and professional organization. Core specialties include internal medicine, surgery, pediatrics, obstetrics-gynecology, psychiatry, family medicine, emergency medicine, radiology, pathology, and anesthesiology. Subspecialties within these have multiplied — cardiology, oncology, nephrology, gastroenterology, endocrinology, rheumatology, pulmonology, hematology, infectious disease, geriatrics within internal medicine alone.

Workforce implications are substantial. Primary care is chronically underresourced in most health systems; specialists cluster in urban centers; reimbursement structures favor procedural specialties over cognitive ones. The U.S. faces projected primary care shortages; U.K. and European systems have different but related challenges; low- and middle-income countries face severe specialist shortages in most fields.

Training pipelines take 7-14+ years after undergraduate education. Pressures on training include working-hour regulations (EU Working Time Directive, U.S. ACGME rules), technology adoption requirements, and tension between generalist breadth and specialist depth. Graduate medical education funding structures shape specialty distribution in ways that often misalign with population needs.

## Oncology: The Precision Revolution

Oncology has been transformed by genomics and targeted therapies. Tumor molecular profiling now guides treatment for most common cancers. Actionable targets include HER2 in breast and gastric cancers, EGFR and ALK in lung cancer, BRAF in melanoma and colorectal cancer, BRCA1/2 in ovarian and breast cancer (and poly-ADP ribose polymerase inhibitor eligibility), microsatellite instability-high tumors (checkpoint inhibitor responsiveness), and increasingly fusion genes detected across tumor types.

Immunotherapy — particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4) — has transformed outcomes in melanoma, lung, kidney, and several other cancers. Drugs including pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab, and ipilimumab generate durable responses in subsets of patients, with combination regimens expanding applicability.

CAR-T cell therapy engineers patient T cells to target cancer antigens, delivering dramatic responses in certain B-cell malignancies (acute lymphoblastic leukemia, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, multiple myeloma). Commercial products include tisagenlecleucel, axicabtagene ciloleucel, and others. Manufacturing complexity, high costs ($400,000-$500,000+ per treatment), and severe toxicities (cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity) constrain broader deployment. Solid tumor CAR-T remains largely investigational.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) — antibodies linked to cytotoxic payloads — deliver chemotherapy selectively to tumor cells. Trastuzumab deruxtecan has transformed HER2-positive and HER2-low breast cancer. Enfortumab vedotin, sacituzumab govitecan, and others expand the class. ADC development is among the most active areas of oncology.

Tumor-agnostic therapies treat cancers based on shared molecular features regardless of organ site. Larotrectinib for NTRK fusion tumors, pembrolizumab for microsatellite instability-high tumors, and dabrafenib-trametinib for BRAF V600E tumors exemplify the approach. It upends traditional organ-based oncology organization.

Early detection through circulating tumor DNA (liquid biopsy) and multi-cancer early detection (e.g., Galleri, Grail) is advancing commercially. Evidence for mortality benefit remains preliminary; trials (UK NHS Galleri trial) will inform adoption.

## Cardiology: Devices and Interventions

Cardiology has been transformed by interventional procedures, implantable devices, and new pharmacotherapies. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with drug-eluting stents dominates treatment of obstructive coronary disease. Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI/TAVR) has replaced surgical aortic valve replacement for many patients. Transcatheter mitral valve and tricuspid valve therapies are expanding rapidly.

Cardiac electrophysiology manages arrhythmias with increasingly sophisticated ablation techniques (including pulsed field ablation for atrial fibrillation, approved in 2024), implantable loop recorders, and leadless pacemakers. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) prevent sudden cardiac death; subcutaneous variants avoid transvenous lead complications.

Heart failure treatment has added sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and GLP-1 agonists to established therapies (beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, sacubitril-valsartan). Mortality reductions from modern heart failure therapy are substantial.

Lipid management has moved beyond statins to PCSK9 inhibitors (evolocumab, alirocumab) and small-interfering RNA (inclisiran) for persistent LDL elevation. Bempedoic acid offers a statin-intolerant alternative. New targets (lipoprotein(a), ANGPTL3) show promise.

GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide) produce unprecedented weight loss in obesity treatment and expanding cardiovascular benefits (SELECT trial showed CV mortality and morbidity reductions). The cardiovascular and metabolic implications of effective obesity pharmacotherapy are profound but still unfolding.

## Neurology and Neuroscience

Neurology has been slower to therapeutic breakthrough than oncology or cardiology but is now accelerating. Monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid-beta — lecanemab and donanemab — produce modest cognitive benefits in early Alzheimer's disease, with substantial debate over clinical significance, safety (brain edema and microhemorrhage), and cost. Their approvals (FDA 2023 and 2024) have not resolved controversies about amyloid as a primary target.

Multiple sclerosis treatment has transformed with high-efficacy disease-modifying therapies (ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, natalizumab, cladribine). Progressive MS has seen first FDA-approved treatments, though overall efficacy remains limited. Remyelination drugs are in development.

Migraine prevention has advanced with CGRP pathway drugs (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab, atogepant, rimegepant). For many patients with chronic or difficult-to-treat migraine, these represent transformative improvement.

Parkinson's disease treatment remains primarily symptomatic. Deep brain stimulation provides durable benefit for motor symptoms in selected patients. Disease-modifying approaches — alpha-synuclein antibodies, GBA activators, LRRK2 inhibitors — are in active trials with uncertain outcomes.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis saw FDA approval of tofersen (antisense oligonucleotide) for SOD1 mutations — an early precision medicine intervention in neurodegenerative disease. Gene therapy approaches for other rare ALS variants are entering trials.

Stroke treatment has advanced dramatically with endovascular thrombectomy for large vessel occlusions. Time-to-treatment windows have extended with imaging-based selection. Mobile stroke units and telestroke networks expand access.

Epilepsy management has added newer antiseizure medications and broadened surgical options including laser interstitial thermal therapy and responsive neurostimulation (NeuroPace RNS, Medtronic DBS).

## Psychiatry: New Modalities

Psychiatry has struggled with limited pharmacotherapy progress for decades but now shows renewed activity. Ketamine and esketamine (Spravato) offer rapid-onset depression treatment, particularly for treatment-resistant depression. Psychedelic-assisted therapy with psilocybin and MDMA has progressed through Phase 3 trials; MDMA for PTSD failed FDA approval in 2024 despite Phase 3 data, reflecting regulatory concerns about trial design and bias.

Psilocybin therapy for depression showed promise in trials by Compass Pathways, Usona Institute, and academic groups; regulatory approvals remain pending. Breakthrough designations signal regulatory openness, but standard clinical practice integration poses challenges (trained therapists, prolonged sessions, post-trip integration).

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is established for treatment-resistant depression; accelerated protocols (SAINT/Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy) show higher response rates in shorter timeframes. Electroconvulsive therapy remains highly effective for severe depression and catatonia despite stigma.

Digital mental health interventions — app-based CBT, AI chatbots, digital therapeutics — have proliferated. Evidence for effectiveness varies; FDA-cleared prescription digital therapeutics remain a small segment; many direct-to-consumer apps lack rigorous evaluation. Rapid expansion during COVID-19 has partially retrenched as distinct from promising structured offerings.

Psychiatric drug discovery broadly remains difficult. Biological heterogeneity of diagnostic categories, lack of validated biomarkers, and difficulty translating animal models all slow progress. Genome-wide association studies have identified thousands of loci contributing to psychiatric conditions but few therapeutic leads.

## Infectious Disease and the Pandemic Era

COVID-19 reshaped infectious disease practice and public visibility. mRNA vaccine platforms (Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna) transitioned from experimental to deployed-at-scale, enabling future rapid response to pathogens. Antiviral development (remdesivir, nirmatrelvir-ritonavir / Paxlovid, molnupiravir) occurred at unprecedented speed.

HIV treatment and prevention advanced further. Long-acting injectable antiretrovirals (cabotegravir-rilpivirine) enable less-frequent dosing. Long-acting cabotegravir for pre-exposure prophylaxis offers alternatives to daily oral PrEP. The lenacapavir PrEP trial results (2024) showing near-100 percent efficacy represented major advance. Cure approaches — stem cell transplants, broadly neutralizing antibodies, gene editing — remain early.

Hepatitis C cure with direct-acting antivirals transformed patient outcomes over 2013-2014. Global elimination targets (WHO 2030) face access and screening gaps but meaningful progress. Hepatitis B management approaches are advancing, though functional cure remains difficult.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a slow-moving crisis. New antibiotic pipelines remain thin; many promising compounds fail in development. Reserve-and-pay models and "pull incentives" aim to restore development economics. Diagnostic stewardship and vaccine deployment supplement traditional approaches.

Vaccine development has broadened beyond infectious disease. Therapeutic vaccines for cancer, platform technologies for rapid response, and combination vaccines for multiple pathogens are advancing. Global vaccination gaps remain stark — routine immunization coverage declined during COVID-19 and has not fully recovered.

## Surgery: Minimally Invasive and Robotic

Surgical practice has shifted toward minimally invasive approaches — laparoscopic, endoscopic, percutaneous, and robotic procedures replace open surgery for many indications. Robotic surgery platforms (Intuitive Surgical's da Vinci dominant, competitors including CMR Surgical Versius, Medtronic Hugo, Stryker Mako) expand capabilities and access.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols combine preoperative optimization, minimally invasive techniques, multimodal analgesia, early mobilization, and nutrition to reduce length of stay and complications. ERAS has spread across specialties with documented benefits.

Transplant medicine continues evolving. Ex-vivo organ perfusion (machine preservation) extends donor organ viability. Normothermic regional perfusion expands controlled-donation-after-circulatory-death (DCD) procurement. Xenotransplantation with gene-edited pig organs (eGenesis, Revivicor) advanced to first patient trials in 2022-2024; outcomes remain early with important but incremental information being collected.

Bariatric surgery remains highly effective for severe obesity and associated comorbidities. Expanded access and sleeve gastrectomy's replacement of gastric bypass as common procedure have shaped the field. Competition with GLP-1 pharmacotherapy is reshaping practice patterns.

## Pediatrics and Rare Diseases

Pediatrics has been transformed for many rare diseases by gene and cell therapies. Spinal muscular atrophy treatments — onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma, AAV9 gene therapy), nusinersen (antisense oligonucleotide), risdiplam (small molecule splicing modifier) — have transformed prognoses for infants who previously faced rapid decline.

Duchenne muscular dystrophy treatments (exon-skipping antisense oligonucleotides, gene therapy) have reached approval despite ongoing controversy about surrogate endpoints and clinical benefit. Cystic fibrosis treatment with CFTR modulators (ivacaftor, lumacaftor-ivacaftor, elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor/Trikafta) transformed outcomes for most patients with sensitive mutations.

Sickle cell disease saw first gene therapy approvals in 2023 — exagamglogene autotemcel (CRISPR-Cas9 editing) and lovotibeglogene autotemcel (lentiviral vector). Complex and expensive (hospitalization, conditioning chemotherapy, $2M+ price), these therapies offer potentially curative treatment but with access challenges.

Pediatric cancer outcomes continue improving with targeted therapies and immunotherapies adapted for children. Survivorship care for childhood cancer survivors addresses long-term complications from treatments.

Congenital heart disease surgery outcomes have improved so substantially that adult survivors now outnumber pediatric patients with congenital heart disease. Management of adult congenital heart disease has emerged as a subspecialty.

Neonatal care has extended viability to earlier prematurity with associated outcomes challenges. Therapeutic hypothermia for neonatal encephalopathy has improved outcomes. Nutrition, bronchopulmonary dysplasia management, and parental engagement have all advanced.

## Women's Health and Obstetrics

Reproductive health includes contraception, fertility treatment, pregnancy care, and menopause management. Long-acting reversible contraception (IUDs, implants) has expanded. Fertility treatment advances include improved IVF outcomes, preimplantation genetic testing, and emerging approaches (uterine transplantation, mitochondrial replacement, in vitro oogenesis in research).

Maternal mortality varies starkly by geography and demographics. The U.S. has worse maternal outcomes than peer countries; racial disparities in maternal mortality are substantial. Interventions include pregnancy-related Medicaid extension, perinatal quality collaboratives, doula support, and clinical protocols for hemorrhage, hypertension, and sepsis.

Menopause management has evolved since WHI findings complicated hormone therapy. Targeted hormone therapy in appropriate candidates, non-hormonal options for vasomotor symptoms (fezolinetant, NK3 receptor antagonist, approved 2023), and increased attention to menopausal transition management are reshaping practice.

Gynecologic oncology benefits from targeted therapies (BRCA-directed PARP inhibitors, HER2-directed therapy for certain uterine cancers) and immunotherapy (cervical cancer, endometrial cancer).

## Geriatrics and Aging

Geriatric medicine emphasizes integrated care for complex older patients with multiple conditions. Comprehensive geriatric assessment improves outcomes. Avoiding inappropriate medications (Beers Criteria, STOPP/START), managing polypharmacy, and addressing fall risk, frailty, and cognitive impairment are central.

Geriatric oncology tailors treatment intensity to fitness rather than chronological age. Geriatric assessment integrated into oncology practice improves treatment decisions and outcomes.

Frailty measurement tools guide decisions about surgery, cancer treatment, and long-term care. Sarcopenia, osteoporosis, and fall prevention receive structured approaches.

Dementia care has seen prolonged stagnation in disease-modifying therapy; care models emphasize supportive care, caregiver support, and safety. Modest evidence for cognitive and exercise interventions, hearing and vision optimization, and cardiovascular risk management.

## Dermatology: Biologics Transform Inflammation

Dermatology has been transformed by biologics for chronic inflammatory skin diseases. Psoriasis treatment with TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab, brodalumab), IL-23 inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab) produces clear or near-clear skin for most patients. Atopic dermatitis treatment has advanced with dupilumab (IL-4/IL-13), JAK inhibitors, and others.

Alopecia areata has FDA-approved treatments (baricitinib, ritlecitinib) for severe cases. Vitiligo treatment with topical ruxolitinib demonstrates the spread of targeted therapy to conditions previously difficult to treat. Hidradenitis suppurativa has expanding options.

Melanoma treatment has transformed with immunotherapies and targeted therapies; many advanced-stage patients now see durable responses.

Skin cancer detection is advancing with dermoscopy, reflectance confocal microscopy, and AI-assisted analysis. Total body photography with serial comparison is increasingly standard in high-risk patients.

## Cell and Gene Therapies Beyond Cancer

Gene therapy extends beyond rare pediatric conditions and cancer to ophthalmology, neurology, hematology, and metabolism. Voretigene neparvovec for RPE65-related retinal dystrophy (first approved AAV gene therapy for inherited disease) enabled pathway for others. Hemophilia gene therapies (valoctocogene roxaparvovec for hemophilia A, etranacogene dezaparvovec and fidanacogene elaparvovec for hemophilia B) offer potentially durable factor correction.

Base editing and prime editing — CRISPR-Cas variants enabling precise single-base changes without double-strand breaks — are entering clinical trials for various conditions. Exa-cel (CRISPR approach to sickle cell) represents the first approved CRISPR-based medicine.

In vivo gene editing (CRISPR administered systemically rather than ex vivo) has entered trials for transthyretin amyloidosis (NTLA-2001), hereditary angioedema, and other conditions. Safety and durability of effects are under evaluation.

Cell therapies beyond CAR-T include mesenchymal stem cell products (various indications with mixed evidence), dendritic cell vaccines, tissue-engineered products (autologous cartilage, limbal stem cells), and emerging allogeneic off-the-shelf products.

Pricing and access are major challenges. Multi-million-dollar one-time therapies strain reimbursement systems. Payment models — outcomes-based contracts, annuity payments, dedicated funding pools — are being developed but implementation lags innovation.

## Precision Medicine and Diagnostics

Precision medicine extends beyond oncology. Pharmacogenomics guides dosing and drug selection for anticoagulants (CYP2C9, VKORC1), antiplatelets (CYP2C19 for clopidogrel), and many others. Preemptive pharmacogenomic testing (Mayo Clinic, Vanderbilt, academic health systems) is implemented variably.

Whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing in clinical practice accelerate rare disease diagnosis. Undiagnosed Disease Networks and academic rare disease programs have substantial track records. Newborn genomic screening is expanding beyond traditional metabolic panels.

Polygenic risk scores aggregate many common variants into disease risk estimates. Clinical utility remains contested for many conditions; implementation depends on actionable interventions. Cardiovascular disease polygenic scores are entering clinical use in select health systems.

Multi-omic diagnostics combine genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and sometimes microbiome analysis. These approaches are reshaping research and starting to enter clinical practice; standardization and interpretation remain challenges.

## Digital Medicine and AI

AI applications in medicine have expanded rapidly. Radiology has the largest footprint — FDA-cleared AI for chest X-ray, mammography, CT, and MRI interpretation. Pathology AI for prostate, breast, and other cancers is advancing. Dermatology AI for skin lesion analysis. Cardiology AI for ECG interpretation, echocardiography, and arrhythmia detection.

Large language models in medicine generate debate. Potential applications include documentation (ambient AI scribes like Abridge, Nuance DAX), summarization, patient-facing triage, clinical decision support, and literature synthesis. Evaluation frameworks, safety guardrails, and integration workflows are all under active development. Liability, privacy, and clinical validation remain open questions.

Telemedicine expanded dramatically during COVID-19 and partially retrenched. It remains a permanent option for many encounters; reimbursement frameworks (U.S. Medicare, commercial payers) are still evolving. Specialty telehealth (telestroke, teleradiology, telepsychiatry) is mature; broader practice integration varies.

Wearables and remote monitoring enable continuous physiologic data collection (heart rate, ECG, oxygen saturation, activity, sleep, glucose). Clinical integration ranges from consumer wellness to FDA-cleared diagnostic devices. Decentralized clinical trials incorporating wearable data are expanding.

## Health Disparities and Access

Medical breakthroughs diffuse unevenly. Access barriers include geography (specialist shortage in rural areas), insurance and payment, language and cultural barriers, distrust of medical systems (particularly in communities with historical mistreatment), and structural inequities.

Racial and ethnic disparities persist across many conditions — maternal mortality, cardiovascular disease, diabetes complications, cancer screening and outcomes. Interventions range from provider training (unconscious bias, cultural competency) to system restructuring (patient navigators, community health workers, targeted outreach).

International disparities are starker. CAR-T, gene therapy, and other advanced treatments reach few patients in low- and middle-income countries. Essential medicines access varies; patent and pricing structures shape availability. WHO prequalification, voluntary licensing, and generic manufacturing provide partial routes to affordability.

Global health programs — PEPFAR for HIV, Gavi for vaccines, Global Fund for tuberculosis and malaria — have transformed outcomes for billions. Sustaining political commitment and financial support remains uncertain.

## Why This Level Matters

Medical specialties and emerging therapies define what is possible for health. The pace of change has accelerated to where practice five years ago looks substantially different from practice today, and the next decade promises further transformation. Understanding this landscape matters for:

- **Patients and families**: Informed decisions require knowledge of available therapies and realistic expectations
- **Health system design**: Financing, delivery, and workforce planning must accommodate therapies that didn't exist a decade ago
- **Research priorities**: Investment allocation shapes future therapies; neglected areas (most psychiatric disorders, many chronic conditions, conditions of low-income populations) reflect choices
- **Ethics and equity**: Who benefits from advances, at what price, with what oversight, are central questions
- **Public understanding**: Medical progress is real but uneven; neither utopian nor dystopian narratives capture the reality

Medicine in 2025 is more effective than ever for many conditions, stubbornly limited for others, and vastly more expensive and complex than a generation ago. Navigating its future requires understanding specialty frameworks, therapeutic modalities, and the systems that deliver them.

## The Transition to Level 5

Level 5 will examine specific clinical workflows and therapeutic programs in depth. How does a comprehensive cancer center actually deliver precision medicine? What is the engineering of CAR-T manufacturing and delivery? How do health systems structure rare disease diagnostic programs? What are the operational realities of telemedicine at scale?

Level 5 will also examine uncomfortable truths: treatments that don't deliver promised benefits, pricing that prices many patients out, structural failures in mental health care, and the politics of medical progress. Critical evaluation of emerging therapies — which are transformative, which are incremental, which are hype — is a core competence at this tier.
