*Where everyone meets medicine: the moment something is wrong*

## The Hour You Noticed

You felt fine. Then something shifted. A pain that wasn't there before. A cough that wouldn't stop. A temperature. A lump. A strange fatigue. A moment of "this isn't right."

That hour is the start of the medical encounter. Every clinical visit, every test, every prescription, every surgery in human history began with someone noticing that their own body had changed in a way they couldn't explain.

## The Short History

For most of human existence, medicine didn't help. Sometimes it actively hurt. Bloodletting, purging, trepanning, mercury, heroic doses of opium. Doctors were educated and well-meaning and mostly wrong. Life expectancy barely changed for thousands of years.

Three things changed this, roughly in order:

1. **Germ theory** (1860-1880s): Pasteur and Koch established that specific microorganisms cause specific diseases. This is when handwashing before surgery became standard, not before.
2. **Antibiotics** (1928 onwards): Penicillin and its successors made bacterial infections routinely treatable. Before antibiotics, a cut could kill you.
3. **Evidence-based medicine** (post-1950): Randomized controlled trials replaced expert opinion as the gold standard for "does this treatment work." Cochrane reviews aggregate trials. Guidelines are updated when evidence shifts.

Before these three, doctors did their best. After these three, doctors started actually bending mortality curves. US life expectancy at birth was 47 in 1900 and 77 in 2000. Most of that gain was infant and childhood mortality, which public health and medicine together wrestled down.

## The Three Questions

Every clinical encounter at the simplest level is three questions:

1. What is wrong with you? (diagnosis)
2. What can we do about it? (treatment)
3. What is likely to happen? (prognosis)

Everything in medicine is built to answer these three. History-taking, physical exam, blood tests, imaging, genetic tests — all in service of the first question. Surgery, drugs, therapy, rehabilitation — the second. Follow-up, monitoring, survival statistics — the third.

Most errors in medicine happen in the transition between questions. A wrong diagnosis leads to a wrong treatment. A right diagnosis plus a non-existent treatment leads to a prognosis conversation that nobody wants to have.

## The Prescription

About 60% of US adults take at least one prescription drug. Roughly 20% take five or more. Every pill you take has been through a process:

- Target identification (which biology is broken)
- Molecule design or discovery
- Preclinical testing (cells, animals)
- Phase I trial (safety in a few dozen humans)
- Phase II trial (does it do anything, ~100-500 humans)
- Phase III trial (does it beat existing treatment, ~1,000-5,000 humans)
- Regulatory review
- Post-market surveillance (do we see problems we missed)

This takes 10-15 years and costs $1-2 billion per approved drug. Most candidates fail. The ones that succeed are the ones you take without thinking about it.

## The Scan You Had

You've probably had an X-ray, ultrasound, CT, or MRI at some point. Each of these is a way of seeing inside a body without opening it.

- X-ray: high-frequency electromagnetic radiation. Bones block it; soft tissue doesn't.
- Ultrasound: sound waves bouncing off tissue. Real-time, cheap, safe.
- CT: X-rays from many angles, reconstructed by computer into cross-sections.
- MRI: radio waves from atomic nuclei aligned by a strong magnet. No radiation, great soft tissue contrast.

These modalities are younger than you might think. X-ray: 1895. Ultrasound clinical: 1950s. CT: 1971. MRI clinical: 1980s. Before them, internal diagnosis was mostly guessing from external symptoms or cutting open the patient (alive or dead). Medical imaging is one of the largest leaps in human perception we have ever made.

## The Doctor Who Is Tired

Your clinician is a human who:

- Trained 8-15 years after high school
- Owes hundreds of thousands in educational debt (in the US)
- Works long hours in high-cognitive-load environments
- Is at elevated risk of burnout, depression, and suicide
- Is held to high standards of accuracy with limited time per patient

The medical profession is itself under systemic strain that affects the quality of care. A rushed 12-minute appointment is not the doctor's fault and not the patient's fault. It is the system design. This is the L7 book's subject.

## The System You Navigate

When you get sick in a rich country, you enter a system: primary care, specialists, pharmacies, insurance, hospitals, urgent care, telemedicine. The system is unevenly designed. Different countries have radically different outcomes at radically different costs.

The US spends about 17% of GDP on healthcare and gets life expectancy slightly below other rich countries. France spends about 11% and gets better outcomes. Japan spends 10% and gets even better. This is not about doctors being better or worse. It is about system design — how care is paid for, how it is delivered, how equitably it is distributed.

Medicine and the health system are separate questions. A great doctor in a bad system is limited. A mediocre doctor in a great system still saves lives.

## The First Lesson

When something is wrong, you go to medicine. Medicine is the applied science of making bodies work better, using everything we know from biology, chemistry, physics, psychology, and engineering. It is one of humanity's oldest endeavors and has only recently, in the span of history, started consistently succeeding.

You will be a patient. Everyone will. Understanding what medicine can and cannot do is not a luxury. It is a life skill.
