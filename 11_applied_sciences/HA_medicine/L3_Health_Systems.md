# Level 3: Health Systems
*Financing, delivery, coverage, and the architecture of how countries organize medical care*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## From Doctors to Systems

L2 covered diagnosis and treatment — what clinicians do with individual patients. L3 steps back to the system that puts those clinicians, facilities, medicines, and technologies within reach of populations. Medicine as practiced depends critically on how it's financed, organized, regulated, and coordinated. The same disease with the same treatment options produces very different outcomes across health systems — reflecting differences in access, quality, coordination, and incentives rather than differences in what's medically possible.

This level asks how different countries arrange their health systems, what each arrangement does well and poorly, and why reforming health systems is among the hardest policy challenges. The US spends ~18% of GDP on health; Germany ~12%; UK ~10%; Japan ~11%; most of Africa <5%. Some high-spenders have excellent outcomes; some don't. Costs, outcomes, and equity depend on system design, not just resources.

## Core Functions

All health systems, regardless of organization, must perform several functions (WHO framework):
- **Service delivery**: providing preventive, curative, rehabilitative care.
- **Health workforce**: doctors, nurses, pharmacists, community health workers.
- **Health information systems**: medical records, surveillance, registries.
- **Access to essential medicines and technologies**: procurement, distribution, regulation.
- **Financing**: collecting and pooling funds, paying for services.
- **Leadership and governance**: stewardship, regulation, oversight.

These functions can be organized in many ways; the design choices are what distinguish systems.

## Financing Models

**Tax-funded (Beveridge model)**: government collects taxes, provides care directly through public facilities. UK NHS, Scandinavian systems, Spain, Italy.

**Social health insurance (Bismarck model)**: mandatory insurance via payroll contributions, funds pool risk and pay providers. Germany, France, Netherlands, Japan, Taiwan.

**National health insurance**: single-payer insurance funded by government, provision mostly private. Canada, South Korea.

**Out-of-pocket / private dominant**: patients pay directly or via private insurance. US (mixed), many lower-income countries.

**Mixed**: most real systems combine elements. US has Medicare (public elderly), Medicaid (public poor), employer-sponsored private insurance, ACA exchanges, VA (integrated), IHS — a patchwork.

Implications for coverage:
- **Universal health coverage (UHC)**: goal that everyone has access without financial hardship. WHO and SDG target. Achieved variably.
- **Catastrophic health spending**: costs >10-40% of income. Affects hundreds of millions annually. Major driver of poverty globally.
- **Financial protection**: insurance and subsidies that prevent medical bankruptcy.

## Provision Models

Independent of financing, providers can be:
- **Public**: government-owned hospitals and clinics, salaried staff. NHS, much of France, Italy, Nordic.
- **Private for-profit**: investor-owned hospitals, physician practices, insurer-owned. Much of US, parts of other systems.
- **Private non-profit**: religious hospitals, charitable systems. Large in US, Germany.
- **Mixed**: most systems combine. Germany has private-practice physicians and mixed public/nonprofit/private hospitals, all funded through social insurance.

**Integration**: whether hospitals, physicians, and primary care are under unified management affects coordination. HMOs (Kaiser, Group Health) vs. fee-for-service fragmented care.

## Payment Mechanisms

How providers are paid shapes what they do:
- **Fee-for-service (FFS)**: paid per service delivered. Incentivizes volume. Dominant historically in US, under pressure for reform.
- **Capitation**: fixed payment per enrolled patient per period. Incentivizes keeping patients well and cost-conscious.
- **Salary**: employees paid regardless of volume. Public systems, HMOs.
- **Case-based (DRG)**: payment per admission based on diagnosis. US Medicare since 1983, many systems adopted. Incentivizes efficient inpatient care.
- **Pay-for-performance (P4P)**: bonuses for quality measures. Limited effectiveness empirically.
- **Bundled payments / episode-based**: single payment for all care around a condition. Complex to implement.
- **Value-based / accountable care**: providers share savings (and risk) for managing populations. US ACOs, various experiments.

Real systems use mixes. Most US providers now receive a blend of FFS, capitation, DRG, bundled payments depending on payer.

## Primary Care

**Primary care** — first-contact, comprehensive, continuous, coordinated care — is a critical system element. Countries with strong primary care have better outcomes at lower cost (Starfield, Macinko research).

Models:
- **GP gatekeeper**: specialists accessed via referral. UK, Netherlands, Nordic.
- **Open access**: patients can self-refer to specialists. US, France, Germany (with financial incentives to use GP first).
- **Community health workers**: paraprofessionals providing outreach. Essential in low-resource settings; expanding in US Medicaid, community health centers.
- **Polyclinics / health centers**: multispecialty outpatient hubs. Common in social-insurance and some public systems.

**Primary care supply**: most advanced economies have physician workforce skewed toward specialists. US has ~1:2 primary:specialist; most European countries 1:1 or better. Shortages of primary care in many rural areas globally.

## Hospital Care

Hospitals are the most expensive, most visible element:
- **Tertiary / academic medical centers**: complex care, specialty services, training, research.
- **Community hospitals**: general care.
- **Specialty hospitals**: children's, cardiac, cancer, rehabilitation.
- **Public vs. private**: ownership affects access, pricing, mission.

**Hospital volumes**: higher volume predicts better outcomes for complex procedures (cardiac surgery, cancer surgery). Concentration in regional centers of excellence tension with local access.

**Length of stay** has fallen dramatically. US average hospital LOS ~4.5 days today vs. 7+ days decades ago. Shift to outpatient, day surgery, home-based care.

**Consolidation**: hospital mergers have concentrated markets in US, raising prices. Evidence of quality improvement from consolidation is mixed. Antitrust concerns growing.

## Pharmaceutical Systems

Drugs are ~10-20% of health spending in most systems. Pricing, access, and innovation involve distinct tradeoffs.

**Pricing mechanisms**:
- **Reference pricing**: pay based on price of therapeutic alternatives or international prices. Germany, Australia, Canada.
- **HTA / cost-effectiveness**: threshold per QALY (£20-30,000 NICE UK, higher in US when used). Coverage decisions based on evidence.
- **Negotiation**: single-payer or coordinated negotiation extracts better prices. Most OECD countries.
- **Free pricing**: manufacturers set; insurance negotiates. US historically.

US pays 2-4× more for same drugs as European countries with negotiation. Inflation Reduction Act 2022 introduced Medicare negotiation for first time; implementation unfolding.

**Essential medicines list (WHO)**: priority drugs that should be available everywhere. Basis for many national drug policies.

**Generics**: ~90% of US prescriptions are generic but only ~20% of spending. Generic competition drives prices down dramatically after patent expiry (10-90% reductions typical).

**Biologics and biosimilars**: expensive biological drugs (monoclonal antibodies, protein therapies). Biosimilars are copy versions; uptake slower than small-molecule generics.

**Specialty drugs**: for chronic or serious conditions, often very expensive. Growing share of spending.

**Access gaps**: many low-income countries lack access to expensive drugs (cancer therapies, hepatitis C cures) despite generic availability. Patent structures, licensing, donor programs all factor in.

## Quality and Safety

Healthcare quality and safety are persistent challenges:

- **Medical errors**: ~250,000 US deaths annually attributable, per estimates. Wrong medications, wrong-site surgery, missed diagnoses, hospital-acquired infections.
- **Variation in practice**: similar patients receive very different care in different regions (Dartmouth Atlas). Some variation reflects appropriate individualization; much reflects provider preferences and supply-induced demand.
- **Overuse**: too many imaging tests, surgeries of questionable benefit. Choosing Wisely campaign identifies overused services.
- **Underuse**: missed preventive care, undertreated chronic disease.
- **Misuse**: inappropriate therapy, medication errors.

Quality improvement approaches:
- **Checklists**: surgical safety, central line insertion, sepsis. Demonstrably effective.
- **Clinical pathways**: standardized care protocols.
- **Patient safety culture**: reporting, learning from errors.
- **Measurement and reporting**: quality metrics, hospital compare data.
- **Accreditation**: Joint Commission (US), CQC (UK), equivalent elsewhere.

Quality varies enormously across hospitals, providers, and regions — a reminder that organizational factors, not just medical knowledge, determine outcomes.

## Workforce

Health workforce shortages and maldistribution are common:
- **Physicians**: ~3.6 per 1000 in OECD average. Germany 4.4, US 2.6, lower in many middle-income countries.
- **Nurses**: 4-20 per 1000, wide variation.
- **Rural/urban**: rural areas under-staffed almost everywhere.
- **Specialty imbalance**: shortages of primary care, geriatrics, psychiatry in many systems.

Migration: many health workers move from lower- to higher-income countries. Ethics of worker migration (brain drain) vs. individual right to mobility vs. destination country needs remain contested.

Training pipelines: 7-15 years from high school to practicing physician. Changes take decades to work through system. Increasing mid-level providers (nurse practitioners, physician assistants) one response to primary care shortage.

## Health Technology

Diffusion and evaluation of new technologies:
- **Medical devices**: imaging, implants, surgical tools. $400B+ global market. FDA, CE mark regulation; varying evidence requirements.
- **Digital health**: EHRs, telemedicine, remote monitoring, AI diagnostics. Accelerated by COVID; still finding sustainable models.
- **Precision medicine**: genomic, biomarker-guided therapy. Expensive but increasingly standard for some cancers.
- **Artificial intelligence**: diagnostic imaging, clinical decision support, administrative. Multiple FDA-cleared tools; workflow integration slow.

**Health technology assessment (HTA)**: evaluating clinical and cost effectiveness. NICE (UK), IQWiG (Germany), CADTH (Canada). US has nothing comparable — historically politically blocked.

## International Comparisons

Cross-country comparisons illuminate design choices:
- **Cost**: US ~$12,000 per capita; Germany, France ~$6,000; UK ~$5,000; Canada ~$5,000; Japan ~$4,600.
- **Outcomes**: life expectancy, infant mortality, amenable mortality. US lags despite higher spending. Lifestyle, social determinants, healthcare system all factor.
- **Access**: universal coverage achieved in virtually all OECD except US (mostly).
- **Satisfaction**: highest in systems with universal coverage, strong primary care, financial protection.

The US health system is outlier in cost, complexity, and lack of universal coverage. Within OECD the fundamental debates are about how to structure UHC, not whether — the US is the exception.

## Global Health

Low- and middle-income countries face distinct challenges:
- **Dual disease burden**: infectious and non-communicable diseases simultaneously.
- **Weak health systems**: shortages of everything — workers, facilities, drugs, data.
- **External donor dependence**: for specific disease programs (HIV, TB, malaria, immunization) vs. general system strengthening.
- **Maternal and child health**: largest gaps where investment has high return.
- **Pandemic preparedness**: COVID exposed weaknesses even in rich systems.

Institutions: WHO, Global Fund, GAVI, World Bank health investments, bilateral donors (PEPFAR, UK FCDO), philanthropies (Gates Foundation).

Progress over last decades significant: child mortality halved since 1990, HIV deaths halved from peak, new vaccines (rotavirus, HPV, pneumococcal, malaria) deployed. But 2030 SDG targets unlikely to be met without acceleration.

## Why This Level Matters

Health system design determines whether a population gets adequate care at affordable cost. It's one of the largest public finance and private spending domains. It affects life expectancy, economic productivity, financial security, and political legitimacy.

Reform is persistently difficult. Interest groups (providers, insurers, drug companies, unions, employers), federalism, ideology, and inertia all slow change. Successful reforms often require extended political leadership. Failed reforms (US multiple attempts, some European backlashes) highlight the difficulty.

Yet evidence about what works is surprisingly clear. Universal coverage, strong primary care, negotiated drug prices, integrated care, measurement and quality improvement — these produce better outcomes at lower cost. Acting on the evidence remains harder than producing it.
