# Level 1: Anatomy & Physiology for Practice
*What clinicians need to know about the body*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Organ Systems

The clinical view of the body divides it into about a dozen organ systems, each with specific normal function, common failure modes, and associated subspecialty:

- **Cardiovascular**: heart, arteries, veins, capillaries. Pumps blood; distributes oxygen, nutrients, hormones; removes CO₂ and metabolic waste. Failures: heart attack, heart failure, arrhythmia, hypertension, stroke. Subspecialty: cardiology; cardiac surgery.
- **Respiratory**: nose, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, lungs. Gas exchange — O₂ in, CO₂ out. Failures: asthma, COPD, pneumonia, lung cancer, pulmonary embolism. Subspecialty: pulmonology.
- **Gastrointestinal**: mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, liver, pancreas, gallbladder. Breaks down food, absorbs nutrients, excretes waste. Failures: ulcers, inflammatory bowel disease, hepatitis, cirrhosis, cancers. Subspecialty: gastroenterology; hepatology.
- **Renal/urinary**: kidneys, ureters, bladder, urethra. Filters blood, regulates water, electrolytes, acid-base balance, blood pressure. Failures: kidney disease, kidney stones, UTIs, bladder cancer. Subspecialty: nephrology; urology.
- **Endocrine**: thyroid, adrenals, pituitary, pancreas (endocrine function), gonads. Hormonal signaling. Failures: diabetes, thyroid disease, adrenal disorders. Subspecialty: endocrinology.
- **Nervous**: brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves. Sensation, cognition, motor control, autonomic regulation. Failures: stroke, dementia, epilepsy, Parkinson's, neuropathies. Subspecialty: neurology; neurosurgery; psychiatry.
- **Musculoskeletal**: bones, joints, muscles, tendons, ligaments. Structure and movement. Failures: fractures, arthritis, back pain. Subspecialty: orthopedics; rheumatology.
- **Integumentary (skin)**: skin, hair, nails. Barrier, temperature regulation, sensation. Failures: infections, cancers, eczema, psoriasis. Subspecialty: dermatology.
- **Reproductive**: gonads, uterus, penis, breast, related structures. Sex-specific organ systems. Failures: infertility, cancers, pregnancy complications. Subspecialty: obstetrics/gynecology; urology.
- **Hematologic/immunologic**: blood cells, bone marrow, lymphatic system. Oxygen transport, clotting, immune defense. Failures: anemia, bleeding/clotting disorders, leukemia/lymphoma, immune deficiency, autoimmunity. Subspecialty: hematology; oncology; immunology.

A generalist (family physician, internist, pediatrician) handles problems across all these systems in their common forms. Specialists handle the complex cases in one system.

## Homeostasis

The body maintains an internal environment — temperature, pH, blood glucose, electrolytes, blood pressure, oxygen saturation — within tight ranges, despite wide variation in external conditions and demands. This is **homeostasis**, and almost every pathological condition is a failure of some homeostatic system.

- **Core body temperature**: normally 36.5–37.5°C. Hypothalamus regulates via sweating, shivering, vasoconstriction, vasodilation, behavior.
- **Blood pH**: 7.35–7.45, a very narrow range. Maintained by bicarbonate buffering, renal regulation, respiratory rate.
- **Blood glucose**: fasting 70–100 mg/dL; postprandial up to 140. Maintained by insulin (lowers) and glucagon (raises).
- **Blood pressure**: systolic 90–130, diastolic 60–80 in healthy adults. Maintained by cardiac output, vascular tone, kidney function, hormonal signaling (renin-angiotensin system, antidiuretic hormone).
- **Oxygen saturation**: 95–100% in healthy adults at sea level. Maintained by respiratory rate, cardiac output, hemoglobin level.

Clinicians routinely measure these parameters (vital signs, basic metabolic panel, arterial blood gas) because they give a rapid read on whether homeostasis is holding. When it fails — a patient in septic shock with low BP and acidosis, or diabetic ketoacidosis with high glucose and acidosis, or respiratory failure with low O₂ and high CO₂ — rapid intervention is often life-saving.

## The Cardiovascular System in Detail

The heart is two pumps in parallel and in series. The right side receives deoxygenated blood from the body, pumps it to the lungs. The left side receives oxygenated blood from the lungs, pumps it to the body. Each side has an atrium (upper chamber, receives blood) and ventricle (lower chamber, pumps blood).

- **Heart rate**: resting 60–100 bpm in adults. Lower in athletes; higher in infants.
- **Blood pressure**: systolic (peak, during ventricular contraction) and diastolic (valley, between contractions).
- **Cardiac output**: 4–8 L/min at rest; rises to 20+ L/min in exercise.

Coronary arteries feed the heart muscle itself. If a coronary artery is blocked (typically by atherosclerotic plaque rupture and clot formation), the heart muscle downstream dies — a **myocardial infarction** (heart attack). This is the leading cause of death globally.

Treatment has progressed enormously. In 1950, a heart attack had a 30%+ hospital mortality. Now it's under 10% in developed countries with timely percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI, stenting) and modern drug therapy. Each improvement — beta blockers, aspirin, statins, stents, thrombolytics, troponin-based diagnosis — saved lives at population scale.

## The Respiratory System in Detail

Breathing moves roughly 6 liters of air per minute at rest, 100+ L/min during heavy exercise. In the alveoli — 300 million tiny air sacs with combined surface area of a tennis court — oxygen diffuses into blood and CO₂ diffuses out.

The respiratory system's homeostatic drive is CO₂ level, not oxygen. When blood CO₂ rises, chemoreceptors in the brain trigger faster breathing. You can hold your breath until CO₂ builds up painfully, long before oxygen runs out. That's why drowning victims breathe water in even when it's visibly bad for them — the CO₂ reflex overrides everything.

Asthma is bronchial inflammation and spasm; airways narrow, exhalation becomes slow and labored. COPD is mostly from smoking — chronic bronchitis and emphysema destroying alveolar architecture. Pneumonia is infection filling alveoli with fluid or pus. Each presents differently on exam and imaging.

## The Immune System

Innate immunity — physical barriers (skin, mucus), neutrophils, macrophages, complement — provides immediate, non-specific defense. Adaptive immunity — T cells and B cells with antibodies — provides specific, memory-based defense that takes days to mobilize for a new pathogen but weeks to years to decay.

Vaccines exploit adaptive immunity by exposing the body to antigen without the pathogen, priming memory cells to respond fast if the real pathogen arrives. Most major infectious disease mortality has been reduced by vaccines, antibiotics, sanitation, or all three.

Autoimmune diseases — rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis, celiac, many others — are failures of self-recognition where the immune system attacks the body itself. Treatment is immunosuppression, which trades autoimmunity risk for infection risk.

Immunodeficiency can be primary (genetic — severe combined immunodeficiency, for example) or acquired (HIV, chemotherapy, transplant immunosuppression). Patients with compromised immune systems die of infections that would be trivial in healthy people.

## The Nervous System

The brain has roughly 86 billion neurons and trillions of synapses. It consumes about 20% of the body's energy at about 2% of body mass. It runs the show for everything from breathing (autonomic) to consciousness (cortical).

For clinical practice, key distinctions:
- **Central nervous system** (brain + spinal cord): higher functions, coordination, major sensory and motor processing.
- **Peripheral nervous system** (nerves from spinal cord to body): sensation, motor control, autonomic regulation.
- **Autonomic nervous system** (sympathetic + parasympathetic): involuntary functions — heart rate, digestion, sweating, pupil size.

Stroke is loss of blood flow to part of the brain. The classic symptoms depend on the region: motor deficits (face, arm, leg on one side), speech difficulty, visual loss. "Time is brain" — every hour without blood flow kills an estimated 120 million neurons. Thrombolytics or mechanical thrombectomy can rescue tissue if given within hours.

Dementia is chronic, progressive cognitive loss. Alzheimer's disease is the most common form. No disease-modifying treatments exist that dramatically change the course (as of mid-2020s; new amyloid-targeting drugs have modest effects at best). Prevention is the realistic hope: controlling hypertension, diabetes, and inactivity cuts dementia risk measurably.

## The Clinical Reasoning

A physician sees a patient and does something like:

1. **History**: what symptoms, when, how severe, what makes them better or worse, past medical history, medications, family history, social history.
2. **Examination**: inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation — the traditional sequence, now augmented with vital signs and bedside ultrasound.
3. **Differential diagnosis**: list of plausible diagnoses, ranked by probability given the evidence.
4. **Testing**: targeted lab tests, imaging, procedures to confirm or exclude specific diagnoses.
5. **Treatment**: medications, surgery, lifestyle modification, referral, continued observation.

This pattern — history, physical, differential, workup, treat — is the backbone of clinical practice. Modern medicine has vastly more technology (imaging, molecular diagnostics, biomarker panels) but the reasoning structure is the same as it was a century ago.

## Why This Level Matters

A clinician's job is pattern recognition under time pressure. The 20 most common conditions in primary care account for perhaps 70% of visits. The occasional rare disease tests whether you can see the less-common pattern. Every patient gets some version of the same reasoning process: what is likely, what is dangerous, what fits the evidence.

Knowing the basic anatomy and physiology is prerequisite. You cannot read a chest X-ray if you don't know what the normal anatomy looks like. You cannot interpret blood tests if you don't know what homeostasis they reflect. You cannot prescribe if you don't know how drugs are absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted.
