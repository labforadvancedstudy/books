# Level 2: Legal Systems
*Courts, procedures, actors — how law actually operates*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## From Sources to Systems

L1 answered *what is law made of*. L2 answers *how does law actually work*. Rules on paper do nothing. The legal system is the machinery that interprets, applies, enforces, and modifies law in practice — through courts, tribunals, agencies, lawyers, and the interaction of millions of daily decisions.

Two legal families dominate the world. **Common law** systems — derived from English law — are the norm in the US, UK, Canada, Australia, India, Malaysia, Kenya, and many others. **Civil law** systems — derived from Roman law and codified in the Napoleonic and German traditions — dominate continental Europe, Latin America, much of Asia and Africa, and Québec within Canada. Several countries combine features; **Islamic law** and **customary law** systems operate alongside state law in many countries.

The differences matter: in common law, judges make law through precedent; in civil law, judges apply codes; in Islamic law, jurists derive rules from religious sources. Each produces a different feel — not because the underlying social needs differ, but because the machinery of rule-making and rule-applying differs.

## Court Systems

Most countries organize courts in a hierarchy:

- **Trial courts**: hear evidence, make factual findings, issue judgments. The workhorses. Most cases end here.
- **Appellate courts**: review trial court rulings for legal error. Don't retry facts. Often sit in panels of 3–5 judges.
- **Supreme courts**: final arbiter. Typically handle far fewer cases, focused on legal issues of broad importance.

In the US, the system is more complex: parallel federal and state systems, specialized courts (bankruptcy, tax, immigration, military, administrative tribunals). Most daily legal life happens in state courts.

In civil law countries, specialized administrative courts handle disputes between citizens and the state. Constitutional courts (separate from ordinary supreme courts) review laws for constitutionality. Germany's Bundesverfassungsgericht is the template.

International courts exist for specific domains: the International Court of Justice (state-state disputes), International Criminal Court (individual criminal liability for genocide, war crimes, etc.), World Trade Organization dispute settlement, regional courts (European Court of Human Rights, European Court of Justice, Inter-American Court of Human Rights).

Judicial independence — courts deciding based on law, not political pressure — is a defining feature of rule-of-law systems. Its strength varies. Independent judiciaries in the US, UK, Germany, Japan, and many others deliver predictable rulings even in politically sensitive cases. Captive judiciaries in authoritarian systems deliver the rulings the regime wants. Between those poles are legal systems with partial independence — independent in routine matters, compromised on politically sensitive ones.

## Civil Litigation

**Civil litigation** resolves disputes between private parties. Typical stages:

1. **Complaint**: the plaintiff files a pleading stating the claim.
2. **Answer**: the defendant responds, admitting, denying, or asserting defenses.
3. **Discovery**: parties exchange documents, take depositions, serve interrogatories. The most expensive stage.
4. **Motion practice**: parties file motions — to dismiss, for summary judgment, to compel discovery, etc.
5. **Trial**: if the case survives motions and doesn't settle. In common law, a jury often decides factual questions.
6. **Judgment**: the court rules.
7. **Appeal**: the losing party may appeal to a higher court.
8. **Enforcement**: winning the judgment is one thing; collecting is another. Judgment liens, garnishment, seizure of assets.

In the US, civil cases overwhelmingly settle — over 95% never reach trial. Settlement reflects the cost and uncertainty of litigation, plus discovery revealing strengths and weaknesses on both sides.

Civil procedure rules govern each step. In the US, federal courts follow the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure; state courts have their own rules. In civil law systems, procedure is typically judge-led with less adversarial drama: the judge investigates, questions witnesses, and decides.

## Criminal Justice

**Criminal law** punishes conduct offensive to society — murder, theft, fraud, assault, regulatory violations. The state prosecutes; the accused may be imprisoned, fined, executed (where legal), or subject to other restrictions.

Typical stages in a common-law system:

1. **Arrest** (or summons): based on probable cause.
2. **Initial appearance**: charges read, bail addressed.
3. **Preliminary hearing / grand jury**: establish probable cause to proceed.
4. **Arraignment**: formal plea (guilty, not guilty, no contest).
5. **Pretrial motions and discovery**: suppress evidence, dismiss charges.
6. **Plea negotiations**: the vast majority of cases resolve by plea bargain, not trial.
7. **Trial**: by jury unless waived. Prosecution must prove beyond reasonable doubt.
8. **Sentencing**: by judge, within statutory ranges, often with sentencing guidelines.
9. **Appeal**: often limited but important for legal error correction.
10. **Post-conviction relief**: limited review after appeals exhausted.

Plea bargaining dominates US criminal practice — ~95% of convictions result from pleas. The system would collapse without it, but plea bargaining also produces false guilty pleas (innocent defendants accepting pleas to avoid trial risk), sentencing disparities, and dependency on prosecutorial discretion.

Civil law systems rely more on investigation by magistrates or prosecutors and less on plea-bargaining, though practices vary.

### Burden and Standard of Proof

Civil cases usually use **preponderance of the evidence** (more likely than not, >50%). Some civil matters (fraud, punitive damages) use **clear and convincing evidence** (higher, somewhere ~75%). Criminal cases require **beyond a reasonable doubt** (very high — not quantified but much higher than civil).

The asymmetry is intentional: depriving someone of liberty or life is so serious that we accept letting some guilty go free to avoid convicting the innocent. Blackstone's ratio ("better that ten guilty escape than that one innocent suffer") is ancient.

## Constitutional Review

Many countries have **constitutional review** — courts can strike down laws that violate the constitution.

**Judicial review** (US model): ordinary courts can address constitutional questions; the Supreme Court is final arbiter. Established in the US by *Marbury v. Madison* (1803).

**Concentrated review** (German/European model): a special constitutional court handles constitutional questions; ordinary courts can't strike down laws.

**No review** (UK, traditionally): parliamentary sovereignty means no court can strike down an Act of Parliament. The UK now has limited review under the Human Rights Act, but parliament remains supreme in theory.

Constitutional review is contested terrain. Supporters argue it protects minority rights against majorities; critics argue it transfers political decisions from elected branches to unelected judges. Most democratic systems have found some balance — usually involving constitutional review for some issues but not all.

## Administrative Law

Most modern legal regulation happens through **administrative law** — agencies issuing regulations, adjudicating cases, enforcing standards. The alphabet soup of US agencies (EPA, FDA, SEC, FTC, FCC, OSHA, etc.) and their equivalents elsewhere produce vastly more legal rules and decisions than legislatures or courts.

Administrative law issues:

- **Delegation**: how much rulemaking authority can legislatures hand to agencies?
- **Procedure**: when must agencies allow public comment, conduct hearings, give reasons?
- **Judicial review**: when and how can courts review agency action?
- **Deference**: how much should courts defer to agency interpretations of their own statutes?

The US Supreme Court's 2024 *Loper Bright* decision overruled the longstanding Chevron doctrine — under which courts had deferred to reasonable agency interpretations — shifting more interpretation authority to courts. This is a major institutional shift whose full effects will unfold over years.

## Lawyers and Judges

The legal profession is organized around licensing and professional ethics.

**Lawyers** (attorneys, solicitors, barristers — terminology varies) are licensed by states or nations, often after law school and examinations. They owe duties to clients (competence, confidentiality, loyalty) and to the legal system (candor, avoiding frivolous claims).

Specialization has intensified. Big firms have dozens of practice areas; solo practitioners still handle general cases but most modern legal work is specialty-specific. Major specialties: corporate, litigation, tax, intellectual property, employment, family, criminal, real estate, immigration, bankruptcy.

**Judges** are selected by various methods: election (most US state judges), appointment by executive (federal judges in the US, most European judiciaries), or career civil service (many civil law countries, where judges are professional civil servants from the start). Each method has trade-offs for independence, accountability, and quality.

Judicial pay, status, and workload vary dramatically. US federal judges have lifetime tenure, moderate pay, and heavy workload. Many European judges have career tracks and pensions but rotate through specializations. Judicial corruption exists at various levels in many countries; independence is a matter of institutional strength, not a binary.

## Alternative Dispute Resolution

Much legal dispute resolution happens outside courts:

- **Negotiation**: direct discussion between parties. The baseline.
- **Mediation**: a neutral facilitator helps parties reach agreement. Nonbinding; parties can walk away.
- **Arbitration**: parties agree to submit the dispute to an arbitrator who issues a binding decision. Common in commercial, labor, and consumer disputes. Enforceable under national laws and international treaties (New York Convention, 1958).
- **Restorative justice**: especially for criminal matters, bringing together offenders and victims for dialogue and resolution. Limited use but growing.

Arbitration has become controversial in consumer and employment contexts — mandatory arbitration clauses can deprive ordinary people of court access and class actions. Commercial arbitration between sophisticated parties is less controversial.

## Legal Aid and Access

Legal representation matters. Parties with good lawyers systematically do better than those without. In the US, criminal defendants have a constitutional right to counsel (*Gideon v. Wainwright*, 1963), but public defender systems are chronically underfunded.

Civil legal aid is even more constrained. Low-income people face evictions, debt collection, family law issues, immigration, and benefits denials largely without counsel. Legal aid organizations handle a small fraction of need.

**Access to justice** is a major issue. Most other rich democracies do better than the US in legal aid provision, though few do extraordinarily well. Technology-based solutions — self-help tools, online dispute resolution, AI assistance — are emerging but don't yet close the gap.

## International Law

**International law** governs relations between states, and increasingly individuals operating across borders.

Sources:
- **Treaties**: bilateral and multilateral agreements.
- **Customary international law**: practices that states accept as legally binding.
- **General principles of law** common to major legal systems.
- **Judicial decisions** and **scholarly writings** as subsidiary sources.

Key bodies:
- **UN Charter system**: General Assembly, Security Council, International Court of Justice, specialized agencies.
- **International Criminal Court**: individual liability for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, aggression.
- **WTO**: international trade law and dispute resolution.
- **International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea**: maritime disputes.
- **Regional systems**: European Convention on Human Rights, Inter-American Court, African Court.

International law's chronic weakness is enforcement. There's no international police force. Compliance depends on state interest, diplomatic pressure, reciprocity, and domestic political will. The system works for most routine matters (aviation, shipping, intellectual property, extradition) but falters on geopolitically sensitive issues — Russia's invasion of Ukraine, Myanmar's persecution of the Rohingya, the Israel-Gaza conflict, and others all illustrate the limits.

## Why This Level Matters

Law on paper and law in action are different. The same statute produces different outcomes depending on which court interprets it, which prosecutor prioritizes it, which agency enforces it, and whether the parties have competent counsel. Understanding the system is understanding what law actually does.

Every major social issue — civil rights, economic regulation, criminal justice reform, immigration, family policy, corporate conduct — runs through the legal system. Reform efforts often target statutes or constitutions but fail because the implementation machinery remains unchanged. Reform that ignores the system rarely succeeds.
