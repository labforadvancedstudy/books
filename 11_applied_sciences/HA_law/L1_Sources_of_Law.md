# Level 1: Sources of Law
*Constitution, statutes, regulations, case law, custom — and how they stack*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Question Every Law Student Learns First

Where does a legal rule come from? In any given jurisdiction, an answer to that question picks out one of several **sources of law**:

1. **Constitution** (or its functional equivalent)
2. **Statutes** (written laws passed by legislatures)
3. **Regulations** (rules issued by executive agencies under statutory authority)
4. **Case law** (rules established by judicial decisions)
5. **Custom and usage** (unwritten norms with legal force in specific contexts)
6. **International law and treaties** (binding at the interstate level, sometimes incorporated domestically)

Different legal systems weight these sources differently. Common-law jurisdictions (UK, US, Canada, Australia, India) rely heavily on case law. Civil-law jurisdictions (most of continental Europe, Latin America, Japan, Korea, China) rely more heavily on codified statutes. Islamic-law jurisdictions incorporate religious sources. Mixed systems (Scotland, Louisiana, South Africa) combine multiple traditions.

But almost every modern legal system has a hierarchy: constitution beats statute, statute beats regulation, regulation beats ordinary administrative practice. When rules conflict, higher sources prevail.

## The Constitution

A constitution is the foundational legal document of a polity. It does some combination of:

- Defines the state's structure (branches of government, levels of jurisdiction).
- Allocates power (who can do what; the doctrine of separation of powers in many systems).
- Enumerates rights (speech, religion, property, due process, equality; substantive and procedural).
- Specifies the amendment process (usually harder than passing an ordinary law).

The US Constitution (1789) is the oldest functioning written constitution. Most modern constitutions have been written or substantially rewritten since 1945. The UK and a handful of others have unwritten or partially unwritten constitutions — constitutional norms are scattered across key statutes, case law, and conventions.

A constitution's rules are harder to change, which is why politically important questions are lifted into constitutional text. In the US, the Bill of Rights protects speech, religion, assembly, and due process against ordinary legislative change. In Germany, the Basic Law's human dignity clause is explicitly unamendable.

**Constitutional review**: in many systems, courts can strike down statutes as unconstitutional. This is judicial review (US: Marbury v. Madison, 1803) and constitutional court review (Germany, Korea, Italy, many others). Countries vary on whether review is centralized in a constitutional court or dispersed among ordinary courts.

## Statutes

Statutes are written laws enacted by legislatures. The process in most democracies:

- A bill is introduced (by a legislator, a government, or rarely by popular initiative).
- Committees review, amend, and report.
- Floor debate and vote in one chamber.
- Possibly a second chamber (bicameral systems).
- Executive signs or vetoes.
- Possible veto override.
- Publication in an official gazette; typically an effective date.

Statutes are organized into **codes** in most jurisdictions — the US Code, the French Civil Code, the German BGB, the Korean Civil Code. A code groups statutes by subject matter. In common-law countries, large areas of law (contracts, torts, property) were historically uncodified and developed through case law, though statutes have progressively covered more ground.

Statutes are interpreted by courts, which creates its own body of interpretive rules. The main schools:

- **Textualism**: the statute means what the words say, as understood at enactment.
- **Purposivism**: the statute should be read to advance its legislative purpose.
- **Intentionalism**: the statute means what the legislators intended.

These approaches can produce different outcomes for the same text. Modern US Supreme Court interpretation has shifted more textualist over the last generation. Other systems mix approaches.

## Regulations (Administrative Law)

Legislatures pass statutes at a level of generality. The detailed work — how to calculate a tax deduction, what counts as a safe drug, what concentration of lead in drinking water is permitted — falls to executive agencies.

In the US, agencies (EPA, FDA, SEC, IRS, OSHA, etc.) issue regulations under authority delegated by statute. The process is typically:

- **Notice of Proposed Rulemaking** (NPRM) with draft text in the Federal Register.
- **Public comment period** (usually 30–90 days).
- Agency review of comments.
- **Final rule** publication, often with preamble responding to major comments.
- Possible legal challenge (regulations can be struck down if beyond statutory authority or procedurally defective).

Regulatory law has exploded in modern states. The Code of Federal Regulations runs to over 200,000 pages. Most of the specific rules a business or individual complies with day-to-day are regulatory, not statutory.

Regulatory oversight is the fight over what agencies can do. Recent US Supreme Court decisions (West Virginia v. EPA, 2022; Loper Bright, 2024) have narrowed agency discretion. Europe and other advanced democracies have their own versions of the administrative state and their own ongoing debates about its scope.

## Case Law (Common-Law Systems)

In common-law jurisdictions, court decisions create binding precedent — **stare decisis**. When a higher court interprets the law in a given way, lower courts must follow that interpretation in future cases on the same question.

This has two important properties:

- **Horizontal stare decisis**: a court generally follows its own prior decisions (with some flexibility for higher courts to reverse themselves).
- **Vertical stare decisis**: lower courts must follow higher courts.

Common-law development is incremental. A new case with a new fact pattern allows a judge to apply the existing rule, extend it, distinguish it, or (at the highest court level) overrule it. Over centuries, this builds up a massive body of accumulated case law covering every domain where statutes haven't superseded it.

Civil-law systems also produce court decisions, and these decisions influence subsequent cases, but the formal theory is that the code is the source and courts apply it. In practice, lines of case law build up in civil-law systems too, especially at constitutional and supreme court levels.

Important legal databases: Westlaw, LexisNexis (US); BAILII (UK); similar national databases everywhere. A modern lawyer spends much of their research time finding on-point case law.

## Custom and Usage

Some legal rules are enforced as custom — unwritten, but widely recognized and historically followed. In international law, customary rules are rules derived from consistent state practice plus a sense that the practice is legally required (opinio juris).

Within domestic systems, custom plays smaller but real roles: industry practice in commercial law, professional norms in some fields, tribal law in indigenous contexts, and religious-law systems for personal status questions in many countries.

The significance of custom varies hugely. In some places, it is subsumed into statute or rendered obsolete. In others, it is a primary source.

## International Law

International law governs relations between states (and sometimes between states and their citizens). Main sources:

- **Treaties**: agreements between states. Binding on signatories.
- **Customary international law**: widely observed state practices.
- **General principles of law recognized by civilized nations**.
- **Judicial decisions and scholarly writings** (subsidiary sources).

International law's enforcement is weak compared to domestic law. There is no world police force. Compliance depends on reciprocity, reputation, and coercion (economic sanctions, military force). Nonetheless, international law shapes trade (WTO), human rights (UN treaty bodies), the law of the sea (UNCLOS), international criminal law (ICC), and much else.

Some treaties incorporate directly into domestic law. Others require implementing legislation. The US generally requires implementation for treaties other than narrowly self-executing ones; monist systems like the Netherlands incorporate treaties directly.

## The Hierarchy in Practice

When rules conflict, the resolution depends on the specific system. In the US:

1. The US Constitution overrides everything else.
2. Federal statutes override state law in areas of federal authority.
3. Federal regulations override state law where preempted by federal statute.
4. State constitutions override state statutes.
5. State statutes override state regulations and local ordinances.
6. Later statutes override earlier ones on the same subject.
7. Specific statutes override general ones covering the same conduct.

This hierarchy is itself contested at the margins — how much does federal law preempt state law, and in what areas? These are ongoing constitutional fights.

## Why This Level Matters

Law operates in everyone's life continuously: contracts you sign, traffic you drive, taxes you pay, food you eat, drugs you're prescribed. All of it is governed by rules from some combination of these sources.

Understanding the sources helps you:
- Read a legal citation and know what kind of authority it is.
- Predict how a legal question will be resolved (what rule applies, which court decides).
- Understand why legal change is slow in some areas and fast in others — constitutional amendments take supermajorities; regulations can be changed by a new administration.

Law is less mystical than non-lawyers sometimes assume. It is a system of rules produced by identifiable institutions, enforced by identifiable actors, interpreted by courts with reasonably consistent methods. The vocabulary is specialized; the logic is legible.
