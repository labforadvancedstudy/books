# Level 3: Substantive Law Areas
*Contract, tort, property, criminal, constitutional — the major substantive categories of law*

## From Systems to Substance

L2 covered legal systems — the institutions, procedures, and families of law that determine how legal disputes are resolved. L3 turns to substance. What are the main subject matter areas of law, what rules they apply, and what puzzles they try to solve? The substantive divisions aren't arbitrary — they correspond to distinct kinds of human conduct and distinct puzzles about how law should regulate them.

This level surveys the main substantive fields: contract, tort, property, criminal, constitutional law, plus newer areas like environmental, antitrust, administrative, and international. Each has its own distinctive concepts, remedies, and ongoing debates. The goal here is literacy, not expertise — enough to read a legal document, understand a court decision in the news, or identify which area of law is in play in a given dispute.

## Contract Law

**Contract law** enforces agreements. Its fundamental question: when will courts make parties perform their promises or pay for breach?

Elements of an enforceable contract (common law):
- **Offer and acceptance**: parties reach agreement on specific terms.
- **Consideration**: each side gives something of value (not just mere promise without exchange).
- **Mutual assent** ("meeting of the minds").
- **Capacity**: parties competent to contract (not minors, not mentally incapacitated).
- **Legal purpose**: contract for illegal activity unenforceable.

**Civil law systems** reach similar results with different doctrinal language (consideration not required as such; good faith plays larger role).

**Statute of Frauds** (inherited from English 1677 statute): certain contracts must be in writing — land sales, contracts taking more than a year to perform, guaranties, sales over $500 (UCC).

**Breach and remedies**:
- **Damages** (the default): put the non-breaching party where they would have been had contract been performed. Can include lost profits, consequential damages, sometimes pain and suffering.
- **Specific performance**: court orders actual performance. Rare; reserved for unique goods (real estate, heirlooms) or where damages inadequate.
- **Rescission**: unwinding the contract, restoring parties to pre-contract position.

**Commercial law / UCC (US)**: Uniform Commercial Code, adopted by every US state with variations, governs sales of goods, secured transactions, commercial paper. Departs from common law in some respects (firm offers, acceptance variations).

**Common contract areas**: employment, real estate, consumer transactions, commercial sales, construction, licensing, franchising, mergers and acquisitions.

## Tort Law

**Tort law** governs civil wrongs — injuries one person causes another outside of contractual relationships. Enables injured parties to seek compensation.

Major tort categories:
- **Intentional torts**: battery, assault, false imprisonment, defamation, intentional infliction of emotional distress, trespass, conversion.
- **Negligence**: the dominant category. Failure to exercise reasonable care, causing harm.
- **Strict liability**: liability without fault for abnormally dangerous activities, defective products.
- **Nuisance**: interference with use and enjoyment of property or public rights.

**Negligence elements** (all required):
- **Duty**: defendant owed plaintiff a duty of care.
- **Breach**: defendant failed to meet the standard of care.
- **Causation**: breach caused the harm (both factual and proximate/legal).
- **Damages**: actual harm suffered.

**Standard of care**: "reasonable person" test usually, with modifications for professionals (medical malpractice uses professional standard), children, emergency conditions.

**Damages**:
- **Compensatory**: medical expenses, lost wages, pain and suffering, property damage.
- **Punitive**: to punish egregious conduct, deter similar acts. Controversial; limited in many jurisdictions.

**Product liability**: manufacturers liable for defective products. Three theories: manufacturing defect, design defect, failure to warn. Strict liability applied in most US states for design/manufacturing defects.

**Medical malpractice**: special rules in many jurisdictions (pre-suit review, damage caps, expert witness requirements). Highly contentious.

**Tort reform** debates: damage caps, frivolous lawsuits, insurance costs. Evidence mixed on whether caps reduce premiums or improve care.

## Property Law

**Property law** governs relationships among people with respect to things. Not primarily about things themselves, but about rights, duties, privileges, and immunities between persons regarding them.

**Types of property**:
- **Real property**: land and structures attached.
- **Personal property**: tangible goods (chattels) and intangibles (stocks, bonds, contract rights).
- **Intellectual property**: patents, copyrights, trademarks, trade secrets.

**Types of ownership**:
- **Fee simple**: full ownership, transferable, inheritable.
- **Life estate**: ownership for duration of a life.
- **Leasehold**: temporary possession for specified term.
- **Concurrent ownership**: joint tenancy, tenancy in common, tenancy by the entirety.
- **Easement**: right to use another's land for specific purpose.
- **Mortgage / security interest**: conditional interest securing a debt.

**Real estate transactions**: deed (written document transferring title), recording (public registry), title insurance, closing processes. Varies considerably by jurisdiction.

**Landlord-tenant law**: combines contract and property. Statutes often override common law with tenant protections (habitability warranties, notice requirements, eviction procedures).

**Zoning and land use**: local government authority over land use. Constitutional limits (takings clause in US; proportionality in EU/UK). Major urban policy tool.

**Adverse possession**: open, notorious, continuous use of another's land for statutory period (usually 7-20 years) can transfer title. Ancient doctrine; rare in practice.

**Intellectual property** is its own major field:
- **Patents**: 20-year monopoly on inventions meeting novelty, utility, non-obviousness.
- **Copyrights**: automatic protection for original creative works. Life + 70 years (US, most).
- **Trademarks**: marks identifying source of goods/services. Renewable indefinitely.
- **Trade secrets**: confidential business information, protected while kept secret.

## Criminal Law

**Criminal law** prohibits conduct harmful to society, enforced by the state.

Categories:
- **Felonies**: serious crimes, typically punishable by >1 year imprisonment.
- **Misdemeanors**: less serious, typically ≤1 year jail or fines.
- **Infractions**: minor violations, typically fines only.

**Elements of crime**:
- **Actus reus**: the prohibited conduct.
- **Mens rea**: the required mental state (intentionally, knowingly, recklessly, negligently).
- **Causation**: conduct caused the prohibited harm.
- **Concurrence**: actus reus and mens rea occurred together.

**Major crime categories**:
- **Against persons**: homicide (murder, manslaughter), assault, battery, kidnapping, sexual assault, robbery.
- **Against property**: theft, burglary, arson, vandalism.
- **Drug offenses**: possession, distribution, manufacturing.
- **White-collar**: fraud, embezzlement, insider trading, bribery.
- **Public order**: DUI, disorderly conduct, trespass.
- **Cybercrime**: hacking, identity theft, online fraud.

**Defenses**:
- **Self-defense**: force proportional to threat.
- **Insanity**: recognized in various forms (M'Naghten, irresistible impulse, ALI test). Rarely successful.
- **Duress, necessity**: compulsion by threat or circumstance.
- **Mistake of fact, mistake of law**: varying application.
- **Entrapment**: government induced the crime.
- **Statute of limitations**: time limits on prosecution for most crimes (exception: murder usually).

**Punishments**: incarceration, probation, fines, restitution, community service, in some jurisdictions capital punishment.

**Sentencing**: mandatory minimums, sentencing guidelines, judicial discretion vary enormously. US sentencing is far harsher than most peer countries.

## Constitutional Law

**Constitutional law** governs the structure of government and relationship between government and individuals.

Major areas:

**Structural**:
- **Separation of powers**: legislative, executive, judicial. Checks and balances.
- **Federalism** (in federal systems): allocation between central and regional governments.
- **Judicial review**: courts' authority to invalidate laws inconsistent with constitution. Established in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) for US; varies elsewhere.

**Rights** (US as example, broadly applicable in many):
- **Freedom of speech, religion, press, assembly**: First Amendment. Extensive case law.
- **Due process**: procedural (fair procedures) and substantive (certain rights against government intrusion).
- **Equal protection**: government cannot treat similarly situated people differently without adequate justification.
- **Search and seizure**: Fourth Amendment; warrant requirements, exceptions.
- **Self-incrimination**: Fifth Amendment Miranda warnings.
- **Right to counsel**: Sixth Amendment; extended to misdemeanors in *Argersinger*.
- **Cruel and unusual punishment**: Eighth Amendment.
- **Voting rights**: various amendments, Voting Rights Act of 1965.

**Scrutiny levels** (US):
- **Rational basis**: default for most government action; defer to legislature.
- **Intermediate scrutiny**: some classifications (gender); law must be substantially related to important state interest.
- **Strict scrutiny**: fundamental rights, racial classifications; law must be narrowly tailored to compelling state interest.

Constitutional interpretation approaches: textualism, originalism, living constitutionalism, structuralism, purposivism. Contentious and politically charged.

## Administrative Law

**Administrative law** governs actions of government agencies. Matters because much of modern regulation is agency-made rather than legislated directly.

Key features:
- **Rulemaking**: notice, comment, publication in public registers. Formal and informal.
- **Adjudication**: agency hearings on specific cases (disability, immigration, licensing).
- **Judicial review**: courts review for arbitrary and capricious, substantial evidence, ultra vires action.
- **Standing**: who may challenge agency action.

**Administrative Procedure Act (US, 1946)** sets framework. EU, Japan, UK have parallel structures.

Major US agencies: EPA, FDA, FCC, FTC, SEC, OSHA, NLRB, many more. Each with its own statute, rules, procedures.

Tension: democratic accountability (agencies delegated by legislature, executive-led) vs. expertise (technical decisions benefit from specialized knowledge) vs. due process (individuals affected deserve fair procedures).

## Environmental Law

Emerged as distinct field mid-20th century. Mixture of statutory (Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, NEPA, CERCLA in US; similar elsewhere), regulatory (EPA rules), common law (nuisance, trespass), international (UN Framework Convention on Climate Change, Paris Agreement, Convention on Biological Diversity, Montreal Protocol).

Instruments: permit systems, emission standards, environmental impact assessments, liability for contamination (Superfund), market-based (cap-and-trade, carbon taxes).

Recent emphasis: climate change litigation, environmental justice, nature rights (legal personhood for rivers in some jurisdictions), biodiversity.

## Antitrust / Competition Law

Regulates anti-competitive conduct. Prohibits:
- **Agreements restricting competition**: price-fixing, market allocation, bid-rigging. Per se illegal.
- **Monopolization**: acquiring or maintaining monopoly through anticompetitive conduct.
- **Anticompetitive mergers**: blocked or conditioned.
- **Abuse of dominance**: varying definitions EU/US.

US: Sherman Act (1890), Clayton Act (1914), FTC Act (1914). EU: Articles 101 and 102 TFEU. China: 2008 Anti-Monopoly Law. Convergence on basic principles; differences in application.

Recent focus: Big Tech (Google, Amazon, Meta, Apple). US and EU both pursuing major cases. Network effects, data, platform dynamics challenge traditional antitrust frameworks.

## International Law

Governs relations among states and increasingly actions by non-state actors.

Sources (Article 38 of ICJ Statute):
- **Treaties**: binding agreements between states.
- **Custom**: consistent state practice out of sense of legal obligation.
- **General principles** of law.
- **Judicial decisions** and **scholarly writings** as subsidiary sources.

Major areas:
- **Law of war / international humanitarian law**: Geneva Conventions, Hague Conventions.
- **Human rights**: Universal Declaration, ICCPR, ICESCR, regional instruments.
- **International criminal law**: ICC, tribunals for Yugoslavia, Rwanda.
- **Trade**: WTO agreements.
- **Investment**: bilateral investment treaties, arbitration.
- **Law of the sea**: UNCLOS.
- **Environmental**: climate, biodiversity conventions.

Enforcement remains weak at international level — no global police. Depends on state cooperation, reputation, reciprocity, economic leverage.

## Why This Level Matters

Substantive law areas define what lawyers do in practice and what matters in everyday life. Understanding which area of law applies is the first step in analyzing any legal situation. Understanding core concepts (consideration, negligence, fee simple, mens rea, strict scrutiny) makes news, contracts, and disputes legible rather than mysterious.

Substantive law also reveals choices societies make about responsibility, fairness, rights, and community. US tort law reflects a risk-allocation philosophy different from French; US criminal law is more punitive than European; property law in different jurisdictions reflects different balances between owner and community. These aren't just technical differences; they're embedded values.
