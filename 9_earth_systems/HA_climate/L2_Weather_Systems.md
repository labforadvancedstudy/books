## Weather vs. Climate

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a place and time — today's temperature, humidity, wind, clouds, precipitation. **Climate** is the statistics of weather over decades — average temperatures, rainfall patterns, the frequency of storms.

A weather forecast is usually useful for 3–7 days. Beyond that, the chaotic nature of the atmosphere overwhelms initial-condition precision, and the forecast converges to climatology (what's typical for this time of year). Climate projections, paradoxically, become *more* reliable at longer time scales — the noise averages out while the underlying drivers (solar input, greenhouse gases, ocean heat) dominate.

This level focuses on weather: how the atmosphere actually moves, what produces storms and fair days, why your local forecast depends on features thousands of kilometers away.

## Pressure and Wind

The atmosphere is a fluid in a gravitational field on a rotating planet. Pressure is the weight of the atmosphere above a given point. Spatial differences in pressure drive air from high to low pressure — that's wind, fundamentally.

If Earth did not rotate, air would flow directly from high-pressure regions to low-pressure regions along the pressure gradient. But Earth rotates once per day, and the **Coriolis effect** deflects moving air: to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern.

The result is that air spirals around pressure systems rather than flowing directly in or out:

- **Highs (anticyclones)**: air sinks at the center, spreading outward, rotating clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere (counterclockwise in the Southern). Usually associated with clear skies, stable conditions.
- **Lows (cyclones)**: air rises at the center, converging inward, rotating counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere. Associated with clouds, storms, precipitation.

At altitude, wind flows nearly parallel to lines of equal pressure (**geostrophic** flow) because Coriolis balances the pressure gradient. Near the ground, friction slows the wind and makes it cross pressure lines slightly toward low pressure — which is why wind blows inward toward cyclones at the surface.

## Fronts

A **front** is the boundary between two air masses of different origin, temperature, and humidity.

**Cold fronts**: cold air advancing, wedging under warmer air ahead. The lifted warm air cools adiabatically and often produces a narrow band of intense precipitation — thunderstorms, brief heavy rain, sometimes severe weather. Cold fronts move quickly (30–60 km/h), and the weather clears rapidly behind them.

**Warm fronts**: warm air advancing, overriding colder air ahead. The warm air glides up the sloping frontal surface over a long distance, producing widespread, gentler precipitation — stratus clouds, hours of steady rain or drizzle, sometimes extensive snow. Warm fronts move more slowly.

**Occluded fronts**: where a fast-moving cold front catches up to and lifts a warm front off the ground. Common in mature storm systems; produces complex, cloudy, often wet weather.

**Stationary fronts**: boundaries that aren't moving much. Can produce prolonged rain as disturbances move along them.

Mid-latitude weather is largely organized around fronts. Weather maps show them as bold lines with triangles (cold) or half-circles (warm) pointing in the direction of motion. Reading a surface weather map means tracking how these boundaries are moving and what's on either side.

## Extratropical Cyclones

Mid-latitude weather is dominated by **extratropical cyclones** — large low-pressure systems that form along the polar front (the boundary between cold polar air and warmer mid-latitude air).

Life cycle:

1. **Wave**: a small disturbance develops along the polar front, with a warm sector and a cold sector.
2. **Open stage**: the disturbance deepens, pressure drops at the center, warm and cold fronts become distinct.
3. **Mature stage**: the system has a well-defined low center, a warm sector between the fronts, and active precipitation.
4. **Occluded stage**: the cold front catches the warm front, lifting warm air off the ground. The system weakens.
5. **Dissipation**: residual low pressure slowly fills in.

The whole cycle takes a few days to a week. Extratropical cyclones move along the **storm tracks** — preferred paths set by the jet stream. Storm tracks shift north in summer and south in winter, taking the weather with them.

## Jet Streams

**Jet streams** are narrow, fast-flowing air currents in the upper troposphere, roughly 9–12 km altitude. The **polar jet** separates cold polar air from warmer mid-latitude air; the **subtropical jet** sits at the boundary of mid-latitudes and tropics.

Jets are driven by large temperature gradients and constrained to high altitudes by Earth's rotation and the structure of the atmosphere. Wind speeds in the core of a jet commonly exceed 150 km/h and can reach 350 km/h.

Jets matter for weather because:

- **They steer surface weather**: cyclones follow the jet. A southward-bulging jet brings cold and stormy weather; a northward-bulging jet brings mild and clear weather.
- **They produce the jet itself**: the jet's wavy pattern (**Rossby waves**) organizes whole weather regimes.
- **They enable divergence aloft**: beneath certain parts of the jet, upper-level divergence drops surface pressure and intensifies cyclones.

Commercial aircraft ride the jet when flying eastward (tailwind saves fuel) and avoid it flying westward (headwind costs fuel). A transatlantic flight's route and altitude are both planned around jet positions.

## Thunderstorms

**Thunderstorms** are small-scale but intense convective systems. The recipe: warm, moist air at the surface; cool, dry air aloft (unstable atmosphere); and a trigger (a front, terrain, daytime heating).

A thunderstorm cycle:

1. **Cumulus stage**: warm, moist air rises; water vapor condenses releasing latent heat, accelerating the updraft; cumulus clouds grow vertically.
2. **Mature stage**: the cloud reaches the tropopause and spreads into an anvil. Precipitation begins. A downdraft of cool air falling through precipitation develops alongside the updraft. Lightning is most intense.
3. **Dissipating stage**: the downdraft cuts off the warm air inflow; the storm collapses.

A typical single-cell thunderstorm lasts an hour. **Multicell storms**, **squall lines**, and **supercell storms** are progressively more organized forms, with supercells producing most of the damaging tornadoes in the US Great Plains.

Lightning is electrical discharge within a thundercloud or between cloud and ground — a very rapid neutralization of charge separated by the turbulent, ice-and-water mixed-phase interior of the cloud. Thunder is the acoustic shock of air heated by the lightning bolt to tens of thousands of degrees.

## Tropical Cyclones

In tropical oceans, a different storm type dominates: the **tropical cyclone** (hurricane, typhoon, or cyclone depending on ocean basin).

Requirements:
- Warm sea surface (≥26.5°C) to depth of ~50 m.
- Pre-existing disturbance (easterly wave, monsoon trough).
- Low vertical wind shear (shear tilts and destroys organized convection).
- Sufficient Coriolis force (so latitudes 5°–30°, not on the equator).
- High mid-level humidity (so rising air doesn't dry-entrain).

Given all these, the initial disturbance develops deep convection, rotation organizes around a low-pressure center, and a warm-core storm intensifies as latent heat release lowers central pressure and enhances inflow.

A mature hurricane has:
- A nearly calm, clear **eye** at the center.
- An **eyewall** of intense convection around the eye — the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
- **Rainbands** spiraling outward.

Intensity is measured by the Saffir-Simpson scale (categories 1–5) based on sustained wind speed, but storm surge and rainfall often do more damage than wind directly. Hurricane Katrina (2005), Harvey (2017), and Ian (2022) all produced catastrophic damage primarily from flooding, not wind.

Climate warming is affecting tropical cyclones: slight increases in intensity, notable increases in rainfall (~7% more moisture per °C of warming), slower storm motion (more rainfall per storm), and possible poleward shift of tracks.

## El Niño, La Niña, and Teleconnections

The tropical Pacific has its own mode of climate variability. In normal conditions, trade winds push warm surface water westward; cold water upwells off South America.

**El Niño**: trade winds weaken, warm water sloshes back eastward, upwelling off South America diminishes. Global effects — wetter winters in the southwestern US, drought in Indonesia and Australia, weaker Asian monsoon.

**La Niña**: trade winds strengthen, the Pacific warm pool piles up in the west, upwelling intensifies. Generally opposite effects from El Niño.

These oscillations are **teleconnections** — patterns where atmospheric circulation in one region drives weather in distant regions. ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation) is the best-known; there are also the North Atlantic Oscillation, Arctic Oscillation, Madden-Julian Oscillation, and others. Skilled seasonal forecasting depends on tracking these modes.

## Numerical Weather Prediction

Modern weather forecasts are produced by **numerical weather prediction (NWP)**: solving the equations of fluid dynamics on supercomputers using observations to set initial conditions.

Key components:
- **Data assimilation**: fitting a mathematically consistent 3D atmosphere to incoming observations (satellites, radiosondes, aircraft reports, buoys, surface stations).
- **Global models**: solve the fluid equations at ~10 km resolution over the whole Earth, producing forecasts to 10–16 days out.
- **Regional models**: higher resolution (1–5 km) over limited areas, producing more detailed short-range forecasts.
- **Ensemble forecasting**: running the model many times with slight perturbations to quantify forecast uncertainty.

Forecast skill has improved dramatically — a 5-day forecast today is about as accurate as a 3-day forecast was in 1990. Improvements come from more observations (especially satellite data), better computational resources, better models, and better understanding of physical processes like cloud microphysics and land-surface interaction.

Despite this, weather beyond ~10 days is fundamentally limited by atmospheric chaos. Two nearly identical initial states diverge exponentially, setting a predictability horizon.

## Why This Level Matters

Weather systems organize the daily experience of climate for every human being. A planet's habitability depends on its weather patterns — storm tracks, monsoons, hurricanes, fronts — delivering moisture and moderating temperature extremes. Agriculture, infrastructure, transportation, energy, and disaster preparedness all depend on understanding weather.

Modern civilization made a huge leap with reliable weather forecasts. Shipping routes, flight operations, power grid loading, crop planning, and emergency response all became easier. The 5-day forecast that city dwellers take for granted is a triumph of 20th-century atmospheric science and computing.
