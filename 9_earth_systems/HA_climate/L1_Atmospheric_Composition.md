# Level 1: Atmospheric Composition
*What the air is made of, and why the recipe matters*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Air You Are Breathing, by the Numbers

Take a lungful of air. By volume, dry air is:

- **Nitrogen (N₂)**: 78.08%
- **Oxygen (O₂)**: 20.95%
- **Argon (Ar)**: 0.93%
- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)**: 0.042% (420 ppm, as of mid-2020s)
- **Neon, helium, methane, krypton, hydrogen, xenon, ozone**: trace amounts, totaling under 0.003%

Plus water vapor, which varies from near 0% (polar winter, high-altitude desert) to about 4% (humid tropics). Water vapor is the single most variable component of the atmosphere and has outsized effects on weather and the greenhouse effect.

The percentages of nitrogen, oxygen, and argon are the same whether you breathe them in Tokyo, on top of Mount Everest, or in a Himalayan valley. The mixture is well-mixed because turbulence in the lower atmosphere scrambles any local differences within days to weeks. What *does* change with altitude is the **pressure** — and because the composition stays constant, the partial pressure of each gas drops together.

## Why the Recipe Is What It Is

The atmosphere is not a random mixture. Each component is there for reasons traceable to geology and biology:

- **N₂** is the most abundant gas because nitrogen's triple bond is so strong that it barely reacts with anything. Over 4.5 billion years, volcanic and biological nitrogen leaked into the atmosphere and mostly stayed there. Lightning and bacteria fix nitrogen into forms life can use, but the atmospheric reservoir is enormous.
- **O₂** is the second most abundant because cyanobacteria invented photosynthesis roughly 2.4 billion years ago, flooded the ocean, then the atmosphere, with oxygen. Plants and algae have been running this chemistry for eons. If photosynthesis stopped, atmospheric oxygen would decline on a timescale of millions of years (weathering and respiration consume it).
- **CO₂** is low because plants and oceans pull it down, and because most of Earth's carbon is locked in carbonate rocks (limestone, chalk). The atmospheric CO₂ level is the tiny residual of a much larger carbon cycle.
- **Ar** is inert and accumulates from the radioactive decay of potassium-40 in rocks over geological time.
- **Water vapor** cycles through the atmosphere in days — it's evaporating somewhere and condensing somewhere else continuously.

This is a highly contingent composition. A slightly different geological history, or a biosphere that never invented photosynthesis, and the mixture would be radically different. Venus has a CO₂-dominated atmosphere because it lost its oceans and never fixed carbon biologically. Mars has a thin CO₂ atmosphere because it lost most of it to space. Earth sits in a narrow band where the composition supports liquid water and complex life.

## The Pressure You Do Not Feel

At sea level, the atmosphere pushes down on you at about 101,325 pascals, or 1 atmosphere, or 1.01 bar, or 14.7 psi. That's roughly 10 tons of air pressing on every square meter. You don't feel it because the pressure is equalized inside your body.

Pressure drops with altitude because there's less air pressing down from above. The relationship is approximately exponential: every ~5.5 km up, pressure halves. Specific numbers:

- Sea level: 1013 hPa
- 1,000 m (typical mountain town): ~900 hPa
- 3,000 m (Denver, or a ski resort): ~700 hPa
- 5,500 m (high-altitude base camp): ~500 hPa
- 8,848 m (Everest summit): ~330 hPa
- 10,500 m (cruise altitude for jetliners): ~230 hPa
- 30 km (stratospheric balloon): ~12 hPa
- 100 km (Kármán line, space): <0.001 hPa

This pressure profile determines how high birds fly, how high airplanes fly, how high humans can survive without pressurization, and why you need oxygen supplements above about 4,000 meters.

## The Layers

The atmosphere is not uniform. It is divided into layers based on how temperature changes with altitude:

- **Troposphere** (surface to 8–15 km, depending on latitude): temperature drops with altitude at about 6.5°C/km. This is where essentially all weather happens. It contains roughly 75% of the atmosphere's mass.
- **Stratosphere** (up to ~50 km): temperature *rises* with altitude because ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation and heats the gas around it. Commercial jets fly in the lower stratosphere because the inverted temperature structure suppresses vertical mixing — no turbulence from weather systems.
- **Mesosphere** (50–85 km): temperature drops again. This is where most meteors burn up.
- **Thermosphere** (85–600 km): temperature rises dramatically (can exceed 1,500°C) because the thin gas absorbs energetic solar radiation. But the density is so low that a thermometer would register cold — the gas molecules are fast but far apart. The ISS orbits here.
- **Exosphere** (>600 km): atoms escape to space. The atmosphere fades into interplanetary space.

The boundaries (tropopause, stratopause, mesopause) are where the temperature gradient reverses. These layers matter for practical aerospace, for how ozone protects us, and for understanding radio propagation and space weather.

## Trace Gases That Punch Above Their Weight

CO₂ is 0.04% of the atmosphere by volume. Methane is about 0.0002% (2 ppm). Nitrous oxide is about 0.00003% (0.33 ppm). Yet these trace gases set the climate.

The reason: the main atmospheric gases (N₂, O₂, Ar) are transparent to both visible light and infrared radiation. The Sun's visible light passes through and warms the surface. The surface re-radiates in infrared. N₂ and O₂ let this infrared pass back out to space.

But CO₂, CH₄, H₂O, and N₂O absorb specific infrared bands. They re-radiate some of that energy downward, keeping the surface warmer than it would otherwise be. This is the **greenhouse effect**, and without it, Earth would average about -18°C — a frozen planet. With the natural greenhouse effect, Earth averages about 15°C.

Human activity has raised atmospheric CO₂ from roughly 280 ppm (pre-industrial) to over 420 ppm (mid-2020s). Methane has roughly doubled. These are rapid changes by geological standards — we have added as much CO₂ in 150 years as the planet naturally cycles in millions of years — and the climate system is responding.

## The Ozone Layer, One Level Down

About 90% of atmospheric ozone is in the stratospheric ozone layer, between 15 and 35 km. Ozone (O₃) absorbs ultraviolet-B and ultraviolet-C radiation that would otherwise damage DNA in surface organisms.

In the 1970s, researchers discovered that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), used as refrigerants and aerosol propellants, were breaking down in the stratosphere and catalytically destroying ozone. A hole opened over Antarctica each spring. The Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFCs, and the ozone layer is slowly recovering.

This is a real case where international science-policy coordination worked. The ozone hole is expected to fully close in the mid-21st century.

## Why This Level Matters

The atmosphere is not a passive backdrop. It is a layered, chemically specific, pressure-stratified medium whose composition both supports life and shapes the climate.

To understand weather, you start with atmospheric composition. To understand climate change, you start with atmospheric composition. To understand aircraft, balloons, weather balloons, rockets, and satellite atmospheric drag, you start with atmospheric composition.

Once you know what the air is made of and how it's structured, you can start asking harder questions: how does it move? Where does its energy come from? Why is weather so chaotic in the troposphere and so calm in the stratosphere? Why do hurricanes form in some places and not others?
