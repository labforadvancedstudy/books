# Level 4: Climate Impacts and Adaptation
*From physics to people — the consequences of a warming planet and how societies respond*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## From Mechanism to Consequence

L3 covered climate dynamics — the physics of how the system works and why it's warming. L4 turns to impacts — what a warming world means for ecosystems, water, food, health, infrastructure, and economies — and to adaptation, the complement to mitigation. Mitigation (reducing emissions) bends the trajectory; adaptation handles what's already committed. By mid-century, warming of ~1.5-2 °C above preindustrial is essentially locked in absent dramatic emissions changes; warming of 2.5-3 °C is a central estimate under current policies. Impacts at these levels are not speculative — they are emerging in observational data.

This level surveys the impact domains, the evidence on attribution and projection, adaptation strategies and their limits, equity dimensions (impacts fall unevenly), and the governance architecture that shapes the response.

## Observed Impacts

Already documented:

**Temperature extremes**: heatwaves intensifying and lengthening. 2021 Pacific Northwest heatwave (>46 °C in Portland), 2022 Europe (~45 °C across UK and continent), 2023 summer globally warmest ever measured, 2024 continued trend. Attribution science (Otto, van Oldenborgh, others) now routinely attributes specific heatwaves as "made X times more likely by climate change" with quantified confidence.

**Precipitation extremes**: intense rainfall events increasing in frequency and magnitude. Clausius-Clapeyron relation (~7% more water vapor per °C warming) drives baseline; convective enhancement can be larger. 2021 German flooding, 2022 Pakistan floods (~$30B damage, 1700+ deaths), 2023 Libya Derna dam failures, 2024 central Europe.

**Drought**: Western US/Colorado basin in historic 20+ year drought; Horn of Africa drought 2020-23; Chile and southwest US; European droughts 2018, 2022. Both "flash droughts" (rapid onset) and multi-year megadroughts intensifying.

**Wildfires**: area burned increasing in many regions. US West, Canada, Australia, Mediterranean, Siberia. 2023 Canada set records (~18 Mha burned). Smoke transport creates far-field air quality events.

**Sea ice**: Arctic summer minimum has declined ~40% since 1979; multi-year ice largely gone. Antarctic sea ice anomalously low 2023-24, breaking decades of near-stability.

**Glaciers and ice sheets**: mountain glaciers retreating globally; Greenland losing ~280 Gt/yr; Antarctica accelerating ice loss from West Antarctic glaciers. Mass loss contributes to sea level rise.

**Sea level**: ~22 cm rise since 1880; current rate ~4.5 mm/yr and accelerating. Coastal flooding "sunny day" events in Miami, Norfolk, Venice much more common than 30 years ago.

**Ocean**: warming, acidifying, deoxygenating. Marine heatwaves stress coral reefs. Stratification increasing; nutrient mixing reduced.

**Ecosystems**: species ranges shifting poleward/upslope; phenology (timing of events) changing; coral bleaching mass events; permafrost thaw releasing carbon.

**Agriculture**: heat stress on grain crops at higher temperatures; shifting growing seasons; increasing yields in some cold regions, declining in hot regions.

## Attribution

**Event attribution** — quantifying how much climate change contributed to a specific event — has matured into a rigorous science. Techniques:
- **Probability ratios**: compare event frequency in current climate vs. counterfactual pre-industrial.
- **Statistical analysis of observations**.
- **Climate model ensembles** (large initial-condition ensembles now feasible).
- **Conditional attribution**: given local conditions, how much more likely was the event?

Typical findings: European 2022 heatwave "~3× more likely" due to climate change; 2017 Harvey precipitation "~15% more intense"; some regional droughts "virtually impossible" without climate change.

**Impact attribution** — how much of damage/mortality traces to climate change — is advancing but harder. Requires modeling not just weather but society's exposure and vulnerability.

## Projections

IPCC AR6 (2021-22) synthesized projections across scenarios:

**SSP1-1.9**: aggressive mitigation, meets 1.5 °C target approximately. Requires rapid, deep emissions cuts and some carbon removal. Currently not on this track.

**SSP1-2.6**: strong mitigation. Peak ~1.7-1.9 °C around mid-century, declining late century.

**SSP2-4.5**: "middle of the road." Current policies trajectory. ~2.7-3 °C by 2100.

**SSP3-7.0**: high emissions, regional conflict. 3.6-4.2 °C by 2100.

**SSP5-8.5**: very high emissions. 4-5 °C by 2100. Often used as "high warming" but current policy trajectories don't point here.

**Regional heating** generally exceeds global mean — 2× over Arctic, land more than ocean. High-latitude warming compounds impacts.

**Tipping elements**: potential nonlinear transitions (WAIS collapse, AMOC weakening, Amazon dieback, permafrost release). Uncertainty large; some evidence these thresholds could be crossed in 1.5-3 °C range, some higher.

## Sectoral Impacts

**Water**: changing precipitation, intensified extremes, snowpack loss (many regions rely on snowmelt for summer water), glacier retreat. Both too much (floods) and too little (drought) worsening.

**Agriculture**:
- Staple crops (wheat, maize, rice) show yield declines above certain temperature thresholds.
- Coffee, cocoa, wine grape zones shifting.
- Fisheries shifting poleward; some tropical fish stocks collapsing.
- Net food production can continue with adaptation and technology; regional disruption severe.

**Health**:
- **Direct heat mortality**: thousands of deaths per major heatwave in affected regions; limits of human heat tolerance (wet-bulb ~35 °C) occasionally exceeded already.
- **Vector-borne disease**: malaria, dengue ranges shifting; Lyme spreading.
- **Respiratory**: wildfire smoke, aeroallergens, ground-level ozone.
- **Water-related**: diarrheal disease from contaminated water.
- **Nutritional**: crop failures, food price spikes.
- **Mental health**: climate anxiety, displacement trauma, loss of place.

**Infrastructure**:
- Roads, rails buckling in heat; concrete and asphalt degraded.
- Electric grid stressed by extreme heat and cooling demand; wildfire risk from utility equipment.
- Ports and coastal infrastructure exposed to rising seas and storm surge.
- Aviation affected by heat (takeoff constraints), turbulence.
- Water systems (Colorado River, many others) stressed.

**Ecosystems**:
- Coral reefs: mass bleaching frequency increasing; many reefs may be functionally lost above 1.5-2 °C.
- Boreal forests: insect outbreaks, fires, gradual dieback.
- Amazon: drying trend, fire vulnerability.
- Mountain and polar ecosystems: species with nowhere to go as warming advances.

**Economic**:
- Damage estimates: $100B-1T+/yr in US alone by mid-century; trillions globally.
- Discount rate debates influence net-present-value estimates dramatically.
- Climate VaR for financial institutions emerging.
- Productivity losses from heat (outdoor labor, cognitive performance).

**Migration and displacement**:
- Sea level rise coastal displacement: Bangladesh, Pacific Islands, Vietnam, large US and European coastal populations at risk.
- Drought-driven rural-urban migration.
- Internal displacement currently larger than cross-border climate migration.
- Projections range from tens to hundreds of millions displaced by 2050, with wide uncertainty.

## Adaptation

Adaptation reduces vulnerability to impacts. Categories:

**Behavioral**: what people and firms do differently — crop choice, work timing, cooling behaviors, evacuation compliance.

**Technical**: new or modified technologies — heat-tolerant crops, cooling, water-efficient irrigation, flood-resistant buildings.

**Ecosystem-based**: mangroves, wetlands, green roofs for stormwater, reforestation.

**Infrastructure**:
- **Seawalls, dikes, storm surge barriers**: Netherlands Delta Works, Thames Barrier, proposed NYC outer harbor barriers.
- **Elevated buildings**: Florida flood elevation, Tokyo flood barriers.
- **Managed retreat**: moving communities from untenable locations. Very politically hard; happening slowly.
- **Cooling**: expanded AC (but itself climate-forcing if powered by fossil fuels), white roofs, urban trees.
- **Water supply**: desalination, reuse, new storage, demand management.

**Institutional / governance**:
- Early warning systems: Bangladesh cyclone warnings dramatically reduced fatalities; heat warning systems.
- Insurance: climate risk incorporated into premiums (or not — moral hazard of state-subsidized insurance).
- Building codes updated for climate.
- Emergency management.
- Planning regulations to limit development in risky areas.

**Financial**:
- **Parametric insurance**: payout triggered by index (e.g., wind speed) rather than damage assessment.
- **Risk pooling**: African Risk Capacity, Caribbean CCRIF.
- **Climate funds**: Green Climate Fund, Adaptation Fund, Loss and Damage Fund.

## Limits of Adaptation

Adaptation has limits. At some warming level, adaptation costs exceed benefits, or technical options become infeasible:

- **Hard limits**: wet-bulb temperatures exceeding human survival; agricultural zones not supporting any crop; land physically inundated.
- **Soft limits**: cost, equity, political will, institutional capacity.
- **Maladaptation**: adaptation that increases vulnerability elsewhere or in future (e.g., dikes that encourage development, then fail catastrophically; AC that increases emissions).
- **Tipping points**: some ecosystems and assets cannot adapt and are simply lost.

"Adaptation-plus-mitigation" is required — neither alone is adequate for high warming scenarios.

## Equity and Justice

Climate impacts fall unequally:

**By wealth**: poorer households and nations more exposed, less able to adapt. A Bangladeshi farmer has fewer options than a Dutch landowner.

**By geography**: low-latitude regions (already hot) see larger proportional impacts; small island states face existential threats.

**By generation**: emissions today commit impacts on people not yet adult.

**By sector**: outdoor workers, farmers, fishers more exposed than office workers.

**Within countries**: minorities, indigenous peoples, informal settlements, coastal communities disproportionately exposed.

**Loss and damage**: concept that some losses are beyond adaptation (lost cultures, lost species, lost coastlines). After decades of contestation, COP27 (2022) established a Loss and Damage Fund; operationalization ongoing.

**Climate justice**: framework arguing those least responsible for emissions bear disproportionate impacts and should receive support. Intersects with international finance, human rights, indigenous rights.

## Migration and Displacement

**Internal climate displacement**: already happening. World Bank Groundswell reports project 143M internal climate migrants by 2050 across three regions (Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, Latin America); other estimates higher.

**Cross-border migration**: historically smaller than internal; could grow. Legal status of "climate refugees" unresolved in international law.

**Managed retreat**: deliberate movement from untenable areas. Fiji relocating coastal villages; Louisiana's Isle de Jean Charles; some US and UK coastal buyouts. Politically and emotionally difficult.

## Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR)

To meet Paris goals under most scenarios, CDR is needed at scale:
- **Afforestation / reforestation**: planting trees. Co-benefits; permanence uncertain (fires, disease).
- **Soil carbon sequestration**: agricultural practices building soil organic matter.
- **Bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS)**: grow biomass, burn for energy, capture CO₂. Land-use concerns.
- **Direct air capture (DAC)**: mechanical systems pulling CO₂ from ambient air. Energy-intensive; currently ~$400-1000/tCO₂; targets $100-200/tCO₂.
- **Enhanced weathering**: accelerating natural rock weathering that absorbs CO₂.
- **Ocean-based**: seaweed farming, ocean alkalinization, biological pumps.
- **Carbon mineralization**: reacting CO₂ with rocks to form stable carbonates.

Scale required: gigatons per year by mid-century under most Paris-compliant scenarios. Current CDR at ~1-2 MtCO₂/yr (excluding natural sinks). Vast gap.

## Solar Geoengineering

**Solar radiation modification (SRM)**: deliberately reflecting sunlight to reduce warming. Most-studied: stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI), mimicking volcanic eruption.

Potential: effective at cooling; cheap (~$10-50B/yr for significant cooling).

Concerns:
- **Termination shock**: if stopped suddenly, rapid warming.
- **Regional effects**: monsoons, hydrology may shift unevenly.
- **Moral hazard**: undermines mitigation urgency.
- **Governance**: unilateral deployment could destabilize international order.
- **Unknown unknowns**: complex system, novel intervention.

Research is limited and politically sensitive. Currently no deployment; some research programs (Harvard SCoPEx canceled 2024).

## International Governance

**UNFCCC (1992)**: foundation treaty; annual COPs.
**Kyoto Protocol (1997)**: first binding emissions targets; limited ratification and effectiveness.
**Paris Agreement (2015)**: global agreement; all countries submit Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs); aspirational 1.5°C target; ratchet mechanism for increased ambition.

**Status**: NDCs collectively still fall short of 1.5°C; gap closing slowly. Net zero pledges by ~2050 from most major emitters; implementation variable.

**Global Stocktake (2023)**: first comprehensive assessment under Paris. Confirmed insufficient progress; called for tripling renewables, doubling efficiency, transitioning away from fossil fuels.

**Adaptation**: global goal on adaptation under Paris; operationalization emerging.

**Finance**: rich-country pledge of $100B/yr by 2020 — finally achieved ~2022 (with accounting disputes); New Collective Quantified Goal from 2025.

**Loss and damage**: fund established 2022; capitalization limited; ongoing negotiations.

## Co-Benefits

Climate action often has co-benefits:
- **Air quality**: phasing out coal reduces PM2.5 deaths.
- **Energy security**: domestic renewables vs. imported fossils.
- **Health**: active transport, healthier diets.
- **Jobs**: renewable energy, efficiency industries. Though coal, oil workers displaced.
- **Biodiversity**: nature-based solutions support ecosystems.

Framing climate action as co-benefit delivery helps build political support.

## Why This Level Matters

Climate change is now driven by impacts more than by mitigation urgency alone. Every flood, fire, heatwave, drought, and crop failure accumulates as evidence and as damage. Adaptation is not an alternative to mitigation — it is a complement, and both are necessary.

The equity dimensions make climate policy harder: those most responsible for emissions are often most protected from impacts; those most harmed have least voice. Climate governance increasingly wrestles with these realities.

For engineers, planners, farmers, investors, health professionals, and citizens, climate is no longer a future concern. Decisions about infrastructure, agriculture, water, buildings, insurance, and migration are being made now under a changing climate. Understanding impacts, adaptation options, limits, and governance is core literacy for the century ahead.
