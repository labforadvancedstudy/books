# Level 0: The Weather You Feel
*Where everyone meets climate: by being outside*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook — Phase 2B stub -->

> "Climate is what you expect; weather is what you get." — Mark Twain (attributed)

## The Air You Forgot About

Take a breath. You just moved roughly half a liter of air. That air weighs about 0.6 grams and is made of nitrogen (78%), oxygen (21%), argon (1%), and traces of everything else including the CO₂ your ancestors never breathed.

You do this 20,000 times a day without thinking. The total weight of the atmosphere sitting on your shoulders right now is about 200 kilograms. You don't notice because there's equal pressure inside you pushing out. If you flew to space without a suit, that balance would vanish and you would learn very quickly how much atmosphere weighs.

## The Wind That Means Something

Wind is not random. A breeze on your face is a slice of a much larger pattern: air flowing from a high-pressure region to a low-pressure region. The high-pressure region is usually cooler and denser. The low-pressure region is warmer or has rising air. The pressure difference drives the flow. Earth's rotation curves the flow into the patterns weather maps draw.

Most weather you experience is built from three ingredients:
- A temperature difference (equator hot, poles cold)
- A humidity difference (ocean wet, continental interior dry)
- A spinning planet that twists the flow

Every thunderstorm, every hurricane, every gentle spring afternoon comes from those three things interacting.

## The Rain You've Cursed

Water evaporates off the ocean, rises, cools, condenses into droplets, falls back as rain. This is called the hydrological cycle and it moves something like 500,000 cubic kilometers of water a year, entirely on solar power.

What you feel as "rain" is the last step of a process that started days ago, thousands of kilometers away, in tropical ocean air. The water hitting your windshield has been in the atmosphere for on average about nine days. Before that it was somewhere else. After a while it will be somewhere else again.

The atmosphere is not a thing. It is a slow river of water, heat, and gas, constantly redistributing energy from where there's too much to where there's too little. You experience a tiny slice of this river as "weather."

## The Difference Between Weather and Climate

If you run outside now, you meet weather. Today's temperature, this morning's humidity, this afternoon's wind. Weather is chaotic in the technical sense: tiny changes at one point can drastically change the forecast a week later. This is why forecasts get dramatically worse past about ten days.

Climate is the long-run average. It is what weather tends to do in your region over a decade, a century, ten thousand years. Climate is not chaotic on those timescales — it follows the energy balance of the planet, which is driven by solar input, atmospheric composition, and the reflectivity of Earth's surfaces.

"Climate change is not real because it's cold today" is like saying the ocean isn't real because this cup of water isn't heavy. The scales are different. Climate is the ocean; weather is the cup.

## The Thermometer You Trust

You check the temperature. You trust the number. That number is the product of a global network: millions of surface stations, balloons released twice a day from ~800 sites worldwide, satellites measuring radiation at dozens of wavelengths, ships, buoys, aircraft reporting during flight.

All of that data is continuously assimilated into numerical weather models running on supercomputers. Your phone's "72°F" comes out of the same pipeline that drives aviation weather, agricultural planning, hurricane forecasting, and IPCC climate reports. It is one of the most integrated global observational systems humans have ever built.

Before this system existed (roughly pre-1950), meteorology was a local art. Now it is a planetary science.

## The Fact You Already Know

Over the last 150 years, humans have released roughly 1,700 gigatons of CO₂ into the atmosphere, mostly by burning fossil fuels. The atmospheric concentration has gone from about 280 parts per million (pre-industrial) to about 425 parts per million (2024). The physics of what CO₂ does to infrared radiation is Textbook-level certain: more CO₂ → more retained heat → warmer surface.

The global mean surface temperature is about 1.1-1.2°C warmer than pre-industrial. Most of that warming has happened in your lifetime if you're over thirty.

This is not a political claim. It is a measurement. Politics begins when we argue about what to do about it. The book at L8 and L9 will engage that argument honestly, with tier markers where they belong.

## The First Lesson

The atmosphere is a fluid. It flows. It has moods. It remembers decades but forgets weeks. Weather is what you experience. Climate is what it means. Humans have entered the unique position in the planet's history of being able to measure the atmosphere precisely and also being the thing changing it most.

You feel weather every day. Climate is what determines whether your grandchildren will live in a recognizable world.
