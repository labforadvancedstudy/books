*Where people live, why they live there, and how space organizes society*

## Why Geography Still Matters

In an era of instant communication and global supply chains, it is tempting to say geography has become irrelevant. The opposite is true. Almost every major fact about the modern world — where wealth concentrates, where conflicts recur, where pandemics spread, where food comes from, where electricity is cheap, where water is scarce — has a geographical footprint. Physical geography (climate, soil, terrain, rivers, coasts) sets the stage. Human geography is about how we've arranged ourselves on that stage.

Human geography studies:

- **Population**: where people live, how their numbers change, how they move.
- **Settlement**: patterns of cities, towns, and rural areas.
- **Economic geography**: the spatial arrangement of production, trade, and employment.
- **Cultural geography**: languages, religions, foodways, and their spatial patterns.
- **Political geography**: borders, states, territories, electoral geography.
- **Urban geography**: internal structure of cities.

Each intersects with physical geography, with history, and with the other subfields. A full account of any place requires all of them.

## World Population

Global population is about 8.1 billion (2025) and still growing, though more slowly than in the late 20th century. The distribution is radically uneven:

- **Asia**: ~4.8 billion (~60% of humanity). China ~1.4B, India ~1.45B (now the most populous country), Indonesia ~280M, Pakistan ~250M, Bangladesh ~175M, Japan ~124M.
- **Africa**: ~1.5 billion, growing fastest. Nigeria ~225M, Ethiopia ~130M, Egypt ~115M.
- **Europe**: ~750 million, mostly stable to slightly declining.
- **Americas**: ~1 billion combined. US ~340M, Brazil ~215M, Mexico ~130M.
- **Oceania**: ~45 million.

Within countries, population concentrates sharply. China's population is concentrated east of the Heihe-Tengchong Line (the eastern 43% of territory holds ~94% of the population). Canada's population hugs the US border. Egypt's population is essentially along the Nile — 95% of Egyptians live on 5% of the country's land.

## The Demographic Transition

A consistent pattern unfolded as societies industrialized and urbanized:

1. **High birth rate, high death rate**: pre-industrial. Slow population growth.
2. **High birth rate, falling death rate**: early industrialization. Sanitation, medicine, nutrition reduce mortality, especially child mortality. Population explodes.
3. **Falling birth rate, low death rate**: as urbanization, education (especially women's), and contraception spread, birth rates drop. Growth slows.
4. **Low birth rate, low death rate**: fertility near or below replacement (~2.1 children per woman). Population stable or declining.
5. **Very low birth rate, rising death rate**: aging population. Deaths exceed births. Some populations shrink absolutely.

Most of Europe, East Asia, and North America are in stages 4 or 5. Much of Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East are in stages 2 or 3. By around 2080, most projections show world population peaking around 10 billion and beginning to decline.

Consequences of stage-5 demographics: smaller workforces per retiree, rising dependency ratios, pressure on pension systems, shrinking school enrollment. Some countries (Japan, South Korea, Italy, parts of Eastern Europe) are already managing this. Others will face it later.

## Urbanization

In 1950, ~30% of humans lived in cities. In 2020, ~55%. By 2050, ~68% is expected. The urbanization transition is ongoing — accelerated in Asia and Africa, largely complete in most of the developed world.

Cities grow for a few reasons:
- **Agglomeration economies**: concentrating people and firms creates larger markets, deeper talent pools, and more specialization. Productivity scales super-linearly with city size.
- **Industrial and service employment**: manufacturing, services, and knowledge work are urban activities.
- **Infrastructure efficiency**: cities deliver water, power, sanitation, and transport at lower per-person cost than dispersed settlement.
- **Rural push**: mechanized agriculture needs fewer farmers; rural-to-urban migration is part of the structural transformation.

**Megacities** — agglomerations over 10 million — have proliferated. Tokyo (~37M), Delhi (~33M), Shanghai (~30M), São Paulo, Mexico City, Cairo, Mumbai, Dhaka, Beijing, Lagos — each with unique challenges (housing, transport, pollution, governance).

**Primate cities** — cities much larger than any other in their country (Bangkok, Buenos Aires, Seoul, Paris) — concentrate wealth and influence, but can starve other regions. **Polycentric systems** (Germany, US, China) distribute activity more evenly.

## Economic Geography

The map of where things are made and traded is tightly structured:

- **Agriculture**: follows climate, soil, and water. Temperate grain belts (North American prairies, Ukraine, Argentina's Pampas, Australia), rice production in monsoon Asia, tropical export crops (coffee, cacao, bananas) near the equator.
- **Resource extraction**: wherever the resources are. Oil in the Middle East, Russia, US, Canada, Venezuela. Iron ore in Australia, Brazil. Copper in Chile, Peru. Lithium in Chile, Australia, China.
- **Heavy manufacturing**: has consolidated in East Asia (China, Japan, Korea) with growing share in South and Southeast Asia. Partial decentralization toward India, Vietnam, Mexico is underway but slow.
- **High-tech manufacturing**: concentrated in East Asia (semiconductors in Taiwan, Korea; electronics assembly in China; photolithography in the Netherlands).
- **Services and knowledge work**: distributed across advanced economies, but with sharp concentrations (New York, London, San Francisco Bay Area, Tokyo, Shanghai, Bangalore).

Global trade is organized along major maritime routes. Seven strategic chokepoints (Strait of Hormuz, Strait of Malacca, Suez Canal, Panama Canal, Bab-el-Mandeb, Bosphorus, Danish Straits) each carry a significant fraction of global seaborne trade. Disruption of any one — closure, blockade, conflict — ripples through global prices in days.

## Migration

Human migration is shaped by:

- **Economic pulls**: higher wages, more opportunity.
- **Demographic complements**: aging societies import workers; young societies export them.
- **Political pushes**: conflict, persecution, authoritarian regimes.
- **Environmental pressure**: drought, flooding, sea-level rise.
- **Family reunification**: chain migration along existing networks.

Roughly 280 million people (~3.5% of world population) live outside their country of birth. Major flows: Mexico and Central America to the US; South Asia to the Gulf, UK, and Canada; sub-Saharan Africa to Europe and the Gulf; Southeast Asia to the Gulf and Japan; Eastern Europe within the EU.

Internal migration (within-country) is larger than international migration. China's internal rural-to-urban migration over the past 40 years — ~300 million people — is the largest movement of humans in history.

Refugee flows (forced migration) account for ~40 million of the total: Syria, Afghanistan, Ukraine, Venezuela, Myanmar, South Sudan are recent major sources. International refugee law (1951 Refugee Convention) and host country policies together determine outcomes.

## Cultural Geography

Languages are geographical. About 7,000 languages are spoken, but half the population speaks one of the top ten: Mandarin, Spanish, English, Hindi, Arabic, Bengali, Portuguese, Russian, Japanese, Punjabi/Lahnda. Languages correlate with historical empires, migrations, and contact patterns.

Religions cluster geographically. Christianity in the Americas, Europe, sub-Saharan Africa. Islam in North Africa, the Middle East, Central Asia, Indonesia, Pakistan, Bangladesh. Hinduism mostly in India and diaspora. Buddhism in East and Southeast Asia. Judaism scattered but with major concentration in Israel. Secular populations concentrate in East Asia, Western Europe, and parts of the Americas.

Foodways, music, architecture, dress, and family structures all have cartographic signatures. Globalization has homogenized some of this (the Big Mac sold almost everywhere) but local particularities remain robust. Understanding a region means understanding its cultural geography alongside its physical and economic maps.

## Political Geography

The world is divided into ~195 sovereign states (depending on recognition). Borders are products of history, war, treaty, and colonial drawing — rarely natural. Most conflicts today occur in areas where borders cut across ethnic, religious, or linguistic groups: the Sahel, the Horn of Africa, the Middle East, the Caucasus, parts of South Asia.

**Exclaves, enclaves, and contested territories** litter the map: Kaliningrad (Russian exclave in Europe), Nagorno-Karabakh (long-disputed), Kashmir (divided), Western Sahara (status unresolved), Taiwan (contested status), Crimea (contested), and dozens more. Each is a point of potential friction.

**Electoral geography** — how political support is spatially distributed within a country — affects outcomes. Urban-rural divides dominate politics in the US, UK, France, Germany, Brazil, Turkey, India. Coastal-interior divides matter in China, Vietnam. Geographic concentration of an ethnic minority can make regional autonomy or secession debates possible (Catalonia, Scotland, Quebec, Kurdistan).

## Urban Structure

Cities themselves have internal geography. Classic models (concentric zones, sector models, multiple nuclei) describe older industrial cities. Modern cities often have:

- **Central business district**: high-value services, headquarters, historically dense.
- **Inner-ring residential neighborhoods**: range from gentrified to declining.
- **Industrial zones and ports**: legacy infrastructure, often transforming to residential/commercial.
- **Suburbs**: lower density, single-family housing, shopping districts.
- **Edge cities and exurbs**: office parks and housing beyond traditional suburbs.
- **Informal settlements** (in developing countries): high-density, low-infrastructure, self-built housing at the urban fringe or in overlooked parcels.

Transportation infrastructure (highways, subways, commuter rail) shapes the internal structure. Cities built for the car (Los Angeles, Phoenix, Atlanta) look different from cities built for transit (New York, Paris, Tokyo). Building patterns are remarkably path-dependent — once the road network and land use are in place, they change slowly.

## Why This Level Matters

Human geography is the bridge between natural geography and the social sciences. It's where the physical world — climate, water, minerals, terrain — meets the human world of economies, cultures, and politics. Every major policy issue — migration, trade, climate adaptation, urbanization, inequality, conflict — has a geographical dimension that becomes invisible if you think only in national aggregates.

Reading a good atlas is analytically powerful. Population density maps, economic activity maps, ethnic composition maps — each reveals patterns that are obscured in tables of numbers. The spatial view is sometimes the decisive one.
