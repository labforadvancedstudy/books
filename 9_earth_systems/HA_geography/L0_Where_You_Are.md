# Level 0: Where You Are
*Where everyone meets geography: by existing at a point on the planet*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook — Phase 2B stub -->

> "Geography is destiny." — Napoleon (attributed, likely apocryphal)

## The Coordinates You Know Without Knowing

You know roughly where you are. Not in latitude and longitude — most people can't do that in their head — but in relation: which way is north, where the ocean is, how far to the next town, which direction the sun comes up. This internal map is geography at its simplest. Every human being carries one. Most animals too.

Phones have made this weaker, oddly. A generation ago, adults could navigate cities without looking down. Now we follow blue arrows. The skill hasn't atrophied in the species — it's just been offloaded. Which is itself an interesting geographic fact: human navigation is now partly lived in satellite orbits.

## The Biome You Don't Notice

You live in a biome. Temperate forest, Mediterranean scrub, tropical rainforest, desert, boreal taiga, tundra, prairie. The trees outside your window (or the absence of trees) are not random — they are the result of how much water falls where you are, how hot it gets in summer, how cold in winter, and what soils accumulated.

If you grew up in one biome and move to another, the first week is disorienting. The light is different. The smells are different. The insects are different. You realize how much of your sense of "normal" was set by an accident of where you were born.

## The Border That Is and Isn't There

There is a line on the map that separates your country from the next one. On the ground, mostly, there's nothing there. Some grass. A road. A fence in some places. Sometimes a river that was already there before the border was drawn on top of it.

And yet that invisible line controls what language is spoken on each side, what currency works, what laws apply, whether you need a passport, what you can say about the government without being arrested, how much the healthcare costs, how old you have to be to drink, drive, marry, or die.

Geography is not just physical. Most borders are recent — under 200 years old. Many will be different in 200 years. But right now, the line on the ground matters enormously for the life inside the line.

## The City That Grew Because of a River

Almost every major old city is on water. The Thames, the Seine, the Tiber, the Yangtze, the Hudson, the Nile, the Ganges. This is not coincidence. Before trains and trucks, water was the only way to move bulk cargo economically. Cities grew at points where the water network and the land network intersected: river mouths, fall lines, confluences, harbors.

Your city may now have highways, airports, and fiber optic cables, but the reason it's where it is probably still goes back to a river that was useful in 1700 or 1200 or 1000 BCE. Geography has extraordinary inertia. Once a city is somewhere, it stays. Roads follow. Then railways. Then airports. Then people, then jobs, then the next generation.

## The Poverty That Sits on a Fault Line

Some of the poorest regions of the world are poor for reasons that include geography. Landlocked countries have harder trade. Tropical agricultural countries carry heavier disease burden. Mountainous regions struggle with infrastructure cost. Island nations have high shipping costs. Desert countries have water scarcity.

None of this is destiny — South Korea was poor in 1960 and geography didn't stop it. But geography is a strong initial condition. Policy, institutions, and culture push against geography. Sometimes they win. Sometimes they don't.

The L7-L8 chapters will take this argument apart carefully because it has been misused (to justify colonialism, to excuse bad governance) and the careful version is much more interesting than either the simplistic "geography explains everything" or the dismissive "geography explains nothing."

## The Map You Trust

Look at a world map. You are probably seeing a Mercator projection, which makes Greenland look the size of Africa (it is 14 times smaller). You are probably seeing North at the top (this is a convention, not a law; for most of history it wasn't). You are probably seeing the familiar Atlantic-centered view (from a Pacific-centered view, the Americas and Eurasia look very different).

Every map is a distortion. Cartographers have to choose what to preserve and what to warp. A Peters projection gets area right but ugly shapes. A Robinson gets shapes mostly right but distorts the poles. There is no "true" flat map of a sphere. Every map is an argument about what matters.

## The Satellites Watching You

Right now, dozens of satellites are imaging your exact location. Commercial satellites can resolve your car in a parking lot. Governments can do better. The entire Earth is photographed, classified, and indexed — roads, fields, buildings, crops — in near-real-time.

This is a historically novel situation. Before 1960, most of the Earth was not visible from above on any given day. Now almost all of it is. Agriculture, disaster response, military intelligence, climate monitoring, archaeology — all run on satellite data. Geography is no longer about exploring; it is about analyzing what is already seen.

## The First Lesson

You are at a point. That point has physical properties (climate, altitude, distance from coast), human properties (language, government, culture), and historical properties (what happened here that still echoes). All of those are geography.

Geography is not a list of capitals. It is the framework that makes all other human sciences make sense. Strip it out and economics, politics, public health, and history become unmoored.

Where you are determines, partly, who you become. Not completely. But partly.
