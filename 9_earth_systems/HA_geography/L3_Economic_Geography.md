# Level 3: Economic Geography
*Location, trade, agglomeration, and the spatial organization of economic activity*

## Why Economic Activity Isn't Evenly Distributed

L2 covered human geography broadly — population, urbanization, cultural and political space. L3 narrows to the economic dimension. Economic activity clusters wildly. Most of the world's GDP is produced on a tiny fraction of the world's surface — a few hundred metropolitan regions account for the majority. Silicon Valley, Tokyo, New York, London, Shanghai — each generates output per square kilometer thousands of times greater than global averages.

This is not an accident of history. It reflects consistent forces — transport costs, economies of scale, labor pooling, knowledge spillovers, demand externalities — that push production to cluster. Understanding these forces explains why places differ in prosperity, why globalization has the shape it does, why some regions develop and others stagnate, and why policy interventions to reshape geography often fail or succeed depending on whether they work with or against these forces.

## Classical Location Theory

Early economic geographers asked: if you could locate a factory anywhere, where would you put it?

**Von Thünen (1826)**: agricultural location theory. Concentric rings around a central market — intensive, perishable crops close in (dairy, vegetables); extensive, durable crops farther out (grain, ranching). Transport costs to market drive the pattern.

**Weber (1909)**: industrial location. Firms minimize the sum of transport costs for raw materials and output, plus labor costs. Different industries locate at different points in the material-to-market triangle depending on weight loss in production.

**Christaller (1933)**: central place theory. Settlements form a hexagonal hierarchy — small villages providing convenience goods, towns providing shopping goods, regional centers providing specialized services, and so on. Higher-order goods require larger market areas, so higher-order centers are rarer.

**Lösch (1940)**: extended Christaller with more rigorous economic foundations, recognizing that different goods generate different hexagonal networks that overlap.

These models are stylized but capture real patterns. Retail hierarchies, agricultural land use gradients around cities, and industrial location patterns often approximate these predictions. Modern extensions incorporate congestion, amenities, input-output linkages, and multiple spatial equilibria.

## Agglomeration Economies

Why do firms cluster even when land, wages, and costs are higher in clustered locations?

**Marshall (1890)** identified three agglomeration forces:
- **Input sharing**: specialized suppliers locate near clusters of customers; customers benefit from deep supplier markets.
- **Labor market pooling**: workers with specialized skills concentrate where firms using those skills are many; firms find workers more easily.
- **Knowledge spillovers**: ideas travel farther in face-to-face interaction than across distance. Clusters absorb and generate innovations faster.

Modern urban economics adds:
- **Demand externalities**: concentrated population generates demand for services that support further concentration.
- **Consumption amenities**: restaurants, theaters, specialty shops, dating markets — all function better at higher density.
- **Matching**: more workers and more firms create better matches; marriage markets work similarly.

These forces are **increasing returns to scale** at the regional level. A city twice as large isn't just twice as productive — it's maybe 2-15% more productive per worker, a scale elasticity documented across many industries and countries (Glaeser, Ciccone, others).

## Trade and Specialization

**Comparative advantage** (Ricardo, 1817): regions specialize in what they produce relatively efficiently, trade for the rest. Even if one region is more productive at everything, specialization based on relative efficiency yields mutual gains.

Extensions:
- **Heckscher-Ohlin**: regions export goods using intensively the factors they have in abundance (labor-abundant regions export labor-intensive goods; capital-abundant export capital-intensive).
- **New trade theory** (Krugman 1979+): intraindustry trade (countries export and import similar goods) arises from increasing returns and product differentiation, not just comparative advantage.
- **New economic geography** (Krugman 1991): agglomeration + trade costs + scale economies generates core-periphery patterns endogenously. Small differences in initial conditions produce large differences in long-run outcomes.

Trade costs have fallen dramatically: containerization (1950s-70s), air freight, the internet, jet travel, WTO liberalization. Result: global production networks that fragment production across continents. iPhone designed in California, components from Korea/Taiwan/Japan, assembled in China, sold globally.

**Gravity model of trade**: trade between two regions is proportional to their size (GDP) and inversely proportional to distance raised to a power (~1). One of the most empirically robust relationships in economics. Distance still matters enormously, even with cheap transport.

## Core-Periphery Patterns

Global economic activity exhibits persistent **core-periphery** patterns:
- A **core** of high-income, diversified, innovation-driven economies (North America, Western Europe, East Asia, Australasia).
- A **semi-periphery** of industrializing middle-income regions.
- A **periphery** of lower-income regions concentrated in primary-commodity or labor-intensive production.

Classifying economies into these zones is politically charged but empirically the patterns exist. Rostow's stages of growth (1960s), Wallerstein's world-systems (1970s), Krugman-Venables new economic geography (1990s) — all offer frameworks for these patterns.

Regional inequality persists within countries too. US GDP per capita in Mississippi vs. Massachusetts, Italian GDP per capita in Calabria vs. Lombardy, Chinese GDP per capita in Guizhou vs. Shanghai — all show ratios of 2-3×.

## Globalization and Its Counterforces

The late 20th century saw unprecedented trade and capital flows:
- Global trade rose from ~20% of world GDP in 1970 to ~60% by 2010.
- Multinational corporations extended global value chains.
- Capital mobility increased dramatically.

Effects:
- **Convergence**: many developing economies (China, India, Vietnam, Korea earlier) closed gaps with rich economies through export-led growth, technology transfer, and industrialization.
- **Inner-country divergence**: gains concentrated in coastal cities, regions connected to global networks. Interior regions often lost manufacturing.
- **Rich-country pressures on manufacturing workers**: competition from lower-wage economies put downward pressure on manufacturing wages in advanced economies. Political consequences emerged in 2010s-20s.

Globalization has partially reversed since 2018-2020 — trade wars, COVID supply shocks, geopolitical tensions, reshoring. The trend is not monotonic. Economic geography is responsive to policy and geopolitical choices.

## The Rise of Cities

Cities are where increasing returns operate most strongly. Global urbanization has concentrated economic activity further:

- **Megacities** (>10 million): Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, Sao Paulo, Mexico City, Cairo, Mumbai, Beijing, Dhaka, Osaka, Karachi, Chongqing, New York, Istanbul, Buenos Aires, many more.
- **Primate cities**: one city dominating national economic activity. Common in smaller economies (Santiago in Chile, Seoul in Korea, Bangkok in Thailand).
- **Polycentric urban regions**: networks of connected cities (Randstad Netherlands, Ruhr Germany, BosWash US, Pearl River Delta China).

Urban productivity advantage is robust but not automatic. Infrastructure, governance, housing supply, and rule of law determine whether cities translate density into productivity or whether they accumulate congestion and dysfunction. Lagos, Manila, Kinshasa have enormous populations without proportionate productivity; Shenzhen went from village to innovation hub in 40 years.

## Technology and the Death of Distance?

The internet and digital technologies prompted predictions that distance would matter less. Partly true, partly wrong.

True:
- Information goods (software, media, financial services) are produced and consumed across huge distances.
- Remote work spread from ~5% to ~20-30% of knowledge workers post-2020.
- E-commerce restructured retail geography.

Wrong:
- Silicon Valley, London, Singapore agglomerations grew more intense, not less.
- Knowledge spillovers remain local (patent citations drop sharply with distance beyond ~100 km).
- Face-to-face interaction for deals, management, complex negotiations is still preferred.
- Logistics and physical distribution still structure manufacturing geography.

The prediction that "the world is flat" was partly right for transmitting information, wrong for generating novel information. Innovation still clusters intensely.

## Development and Geography

Why are some countries rich and others poor? Geographic factors matter, but their role is contested:

**Classic geography hypotheses**: tropical disease burden, poor soils, remoteness from trade routes, access to coast reduce development prospects. Sachs, Diamond, and others emphasize these.

**Institutions** (Acemoglu, Robinson, North): geographic factors shaped institutions, but institutions — property rights, legal system, state capacity — became the proximate drivers. The comparison of Seoul vs. Pyongyang, or East vs. West Germany, highlights institutions more than geography.

**Historical path dependence**: colonial extraction patterns, slavery, resource curse, civil conflict legacies. Explains variance unaccounted for by geography or current institutions.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive. Geography matters, institutions matter, history matters — and they interact. No single-factor explanation for development survives scrutiny.

## Economic Geography of Climate Change

Climate change reshapes economic geography:
- **Agricultural viability shifts**: northward expansion in Canada, Russia; stress in tropical and sub-tropical belts.
- **Sea-level rise threatens coastal cities**: Miami, Jakarta, Ho Chi Minh City, Bangladesh delta, Shanghai. Relocation, adaptation, or abandonment.
- **Heat and drought**: reduce labor productivity in already-hot regions.
- **Extreme weather**: increases insurance costs, stresses infrastructure.
- **Migration**: millions to tens of millions likely displaced over the 21st century. Where they go and how they're received will reshape economic geography at scale.

Adaptation infrastructure (seawalls, cooling, water supply, new agriculture) favors already-capable regions; poorer regions with less capacity face larger impacts.

## Why This Level Matters

Economic geography explains why nearly all of the world's economic activity is spatially concentrated in a few places, why some regions develop while others don't, and why policies to reshape geography often fail. It is essential for urban planning, development economics, trade policy, infrastructure investment, and climate adaptation.

A manager deciding where to locate a factory, a policymaker targeting regional development, a city choosing infrastructure investments, a government negotiating trade agreements — all are implicitly (and should be explicitly) reasoning in economic geography terms. The forces are powerful and durable; fighting them is expensive; channeling them pays off.
