# Level 1: Physical Geography
*The shape of the world and why it is not the same everywhere*

## The Continents You Grew Up With

The world has seven continents in most English-language schoolbooks: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America, South America. This count is partially a cultural convention — Europe and Asia are one continuous landmass (Eurasia), and some geography traditions count only six continents, treating Europe-Asia as one.

Geographically, the more fundamental count is about **continental crust** — the thick, granitic crust that makes up land. There are several major blocks:

- **Eurasian Plate** (minus the Indian subcontinent, which rides on its own plate)
- **African Plate**
- **North American Plate**
- **South American Plate**
- **Australian Plate**
- **Antarctic Plate**
- **Indian Plate**

These plates drift, collide, and split. The current arrangement is about 66 million years old in broad outline and is changing on timescales of millions of years. India was a separate continent that smashed into Asia about 50 million years ago, which is why the Himalayas are rising still.

The Atlantic is widening by about 2.5 cm per year. The Pacific is shrinking at a similar rate as its floor subducts under the Americas and Asia. In 50 million years, the map will not look like this.

## The Biomes — Where Life Organizes Itself by Climate

Life on land organizes into large patterns called **biomes**, determined mostly by temperature and precipitation:

- **Tropical rainforest**: hot, wet year-round. Amazon, Congo, Indonesia. Highest species diversity on Earth.
- **Tropical savanna**: hot, with a pronounced wet and dry season. East Africa, Brazilian cerrado.
- **Desert**: under 250 mm rainfall per year. Sahara, Arabian, Gobi, Atacama, Australian interior, Sonoran.
- **Temperate grassland**: moderate rainfall, cold winters, warm summers. North American prairies, Eurasian steppe, Argentine pampas.
- **Temperate forest**: cool, moist, seasonal. Eastern US, Western Europe, East Asia.
- **Boreal forest (taiga)**: cold winters, short growing season. Northern Canada, Russia, Scandinavia. Largest terrestrial biome by area.
- **Tundra**: subfreezing most of the year; permafrost below. Arctic, high altitudes.
- **Mediterranean shrubland**: mild wet winters, hot dry summers. California, Mediterranean Basin, Chile, South Africa, southwestern Australia.
- **Mountain zones**: vertical stacking of biomes with altitude. The top of an equatorial mountain can have tundra-like conditions.

The biomes correspond to specific temperature and rainfall bands, and they exist because life has adapted to those bands. A plant in the Sonoran desert is built differently from a plant in the Amazon, and that difference has been selected for over tens of thousands of generations.

Climate change shifts these zones. Northern biomes are moving poleward at roughly 10–15 km per decade in many regions. Species that cannot migrate fast enough face local extinction.

## Climate Zones — The Three-Cell Structure

The atmosphere is organized into large circulation cells driven by solar heating and Earth's rotation:

- **Hadley cells** (0–30° latitude): warm air rises at the equator, travels poleward at altitude, descends around 30° latitude (where most of Earth's deserts are), returns equatorward at the surface as the trade winds.
- **Ferrel cells** (30–60° latitude): driven mostly by eddies and mid-latitude storms. Westerly winds at the surface.
- **Polar cells** (60–90° latitude): cold air descends at the poles, flows equatorward, rises around 60° latitude.

The intersections create predictable climate zones: wet equatorial rainforest, dry subtropical deserts, stormy mid-latitudes, cold polar regions. The global pattern explains why Europe has westerlies, why the Sahara is where it is, and why North Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and the Southwest US all sit in the same latitude band and all suffer similar aridity.

## The Oceans Between Continents

The Pacific is the largest ocean, larger than all the world's land combined. Then the Atlantic, Indian, Southern (around Antarctica), and Arctic.

Ocean basins are not uniform. Each has:

- A **continental shelf**: shallow water (<200 m), part of the continent's continental crust, extending up to a few hundred kilometers offshore. Most fisheries are here.
- A **continental slope**: the steep drop-off to the deep seafloor.
- **Abyssal plains**: vast, relatively flat seafloor at 3,000–6,000 m depth.
- **Mid-ocean ridges**: submarine mountain ranges where new seafloor is created. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge runs the length of the Atlantic.
- **Oceanic trenches**: where oceanic crust plunges back into the mantle. The Mariana Trench (11 km) is the deepest.
- **Islands and seamounts**: volcanic features rising from the seafloor.

The map of the ocean floor was largely unknown until the 1950s–60s. Mapping it helped confirm plate tectonics. We still have only partial bathymetric coverage at meaningful resolution — the seafloor is less mapped than the surface of the Moon.

## The Rivers — Earth's Plumbing

Rivers drain continents. Each river has a **drainage basin** — all the land whose water eventually flows into that river. The Amazon drains roughly 7 million square kilometers. The Mississippi drains roughly 3.2 million. The Nile, about 3.4 million. The Congo, about 4 million.

The world's longest rivers:

- **Nile**: 6,650 km (traditionally; some measurements put the Amazon at a similar or greater length)
- **Amazon**: 6,400 km (but carries about 20% of all river discharge on Earth)
- **Yangtze**: 6,300 km
- **Mississippi-Missouri**: 6,275 km
- **Yenisey-Angara**: 5,540 km

Rivers do two things: they carry water, and they carry sediment. The Yellow River (Huang He) in China is named for the loess sediment it carries — 1.6 billion tons per year, one of the highest sediment loads of any major river. The Amazon carries about 1.2 billion tons per year but dilutes it in enormous water volume.

Civilizations cluster along rivers because rivers provide water, transport, and alluvial soil. The Tigris-Euphrates, Nile, Indus, and Yangtze all hosted early civilizations. Most modern megacities still sit on rivers or coastal estuaries.

## Mountains: Where Continents Buckle

Mountains form mostly at continental collisions and subduction zones:

- **Himalayas**: India-Asia collision, still rising.
- **Alps**: Africa-Europe collision, still active.
- **Andes**: South American Plate overriding Pacific oceanic crust.
- **Rockies**: North American Plate, complex uplift history.
- **Appalachians**: ancient collision, now eroded.

A mountain's height is a balance of uplift versus erosion. Mountains rise until the load of rock plus the rate of erosion consumes the uplift. The Himalayas are near the theoretical maximum for mountain heights on Earth — beyond about 9 km, rock strength and erosion rates prevent further buildup.

Above about 5,000 m in most latitudes, trees cannot grow. Above about 7,000 m in the tropics, almost nothing lives. The "death zone" above 8,000 m has so little oxygen that humans cannot survive there for more than a few days.

## Population Geography — Where People Actually Live

Human population is not distributed evenly. Nearly half the world's people live in a dense belt across South and East Asia — India, China, Indonesia, Bangladesh, the Philippines, Japan. Europe, though small, is densely populated. Sub-Saharan Africa is becoming dense rapidly. Russia is vast but thinly settled east of the Urals. The interior of Australia, Saharan Africa, the Amazon Basin, northern Canada, Siberia, Tibet, and Greenland are nearly empty.

The basic drivers: water availability, arable land, coastal access, climate suitability, historical settlement patterns, political stability, economic opportunity. Few of these are changing on human timescales, but they can shift — the post-WWII Sun Belt migration in the US, and the ongoing megacity growth in coastal Asia, are current examples.

## Why This Level Matters

Physical geography tells you where things are and why. It is the background against which everything else — economics, politics, history, climate impact — happens.

You cannot explain why agriculture concentrated in certain regions without knowing about alluvial soils and river basins. You cannot explain where early civilizations arose without the Mediterranean climate, the Yangtze floodplain, the Fertile Crescent, and the Indus valley. You cannot explain why certain modern conflicts cluster where they do without knowing about oil basins, water sources, and drainage boundaries.

Geography is not destiny. But geography is a strong first approximation, and ignoring it leads to bad analysis.
