# Level 0: The Water You Drink
*Where everyone meets hydrology: at the tap*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook — Phase 2B stub -->

## The Glass in Front of You

There is water in front of you right now. In a cup, in a bottle, in the pipes in the wall, in the snow outside, in the air as humidity, in the bread in the cupboard (bread is ~35% water). You are about 60% water. The glass in front of you is old — every water molecule in it has almost certainly been in the atmosphere, the ocean, a glacier, a tree, an animal, and back. Water does not get destroyed. It moves.

## The Trip That Water Made

Follow the water in your cup backward. It came out of your tap. It came to your tap from a treatment plant. It came to the treatment plant from a reservoir or an aquifer or a river. Before the reservoir, it was rain. Before the rain, it was water vapor. Before the vapor, it was ocean (mostly), lake, soil moisture, or plant transpiration.

Before *that*, it was one of the same water molecules that was in a dinosaur's bloodstream, or in a Roman aqueduct, or in an ice sheet over Canada 20,000 years ago. The total amount of water on Earth has been roughly constant for 3 billion years. It just keeps cycling.

## The Two Kinds of Water

Of all the water on Earth:
- 97% is saltwater, in the ocean. You can't drink it.
- 2% is locked in glaciers and ice caps. You can't easily use it.
- Less than 1% is fresh liquid water, and most of that is groundwater.
- The rivers and lakes you see — that's maybe 0.007% of Earth's water.

Civilization runs on a sliver. The thin film of rain-fed surface water plus the slow refilling of aquifers is everything human agriculture, industry, and drinking supply depends on.

## The River Already in Your House

The pipes in your walls move water at a steady pressure, 24 hours a day. You turn a handle, water comes. This is a civilizational achievement that took thousands of years to build and is easy to not notice.

Roman aqueducts delivered about 1 million cubic meters a day to ancient Rome — which is a lot, but only about 200 liters per Roman. Modern US per-capita water use is around 500 liters per day (much of it landscaping and industry feeding you, not your direct drinking). A drought or a broken pipe system can collapse this in a week. Cape Town came within days of "Day Zero" in 2018. Flint lost the chemistry of its treatment and watched citizens get lead-poisoned for two years.

The water system is invisible until it fails. When it fails, civilization becomes very thin very fast.

## The Well That Isn't Refilling

Beneath large parts of the world — the US High Plains, northern India, northern China — there are ancient aquifers that were filled by rainfall over tens of thousands of years. Humans have been pumping them out for about a hundred years, faster than they refill. Some will be effectively empty within this century.

This is the groundwater version of fossil fuel: a stock not a flow. When it runs out, the agriculture that depends on it doesn't slow down; it stops. The Ogallala aquifer under the American Great Plains is already dropping about a meter per decade in heavily pumped regions.

Water looks renewable. Surface water is. Fossil aquifers are not, on human timescales.

## The Glacier That Is Dying

Glaciers are water banked in slow form. Snow falls in winter, compresses into ice, flows downhill over decades, melts out the bottom to feed rivers in summer. This is the water supply for about a billion people — the Himalayas feed the Ganges, the Indus, the Yangtze, the Mekong, the Brahmaputra. The Andes feed much of South America.

Most of the world's glaciers are losing mass faster than they're gaining it. For a while this increases summer river flow (more meltwater). Then the glacier is gone and the flow collapses. Some Himalayan river systems are on track for this transition within a century.

This is not speculation. It is measurement. Glaciologists weigh glaciers by satellite altimetry and ground surveys, and they are quite clear about what is happening.

## The Toilet That Saved More Lives Than Any Doctor

John Snow traced an 1854 London cholera outbreak to a single contaminated water pump on Broad Street. He pulled the handle off. People stopped dying. This is the founding act of public health epidemiology.

Clean water and sewer systems — keeping drinking water and human waste separate — is probably the single biggest life-saving intervention in human history, bigger than any vaccine or antibiotic. Childhood mortality in cities with clean water is an order of magnitude lower than in cities without.

About 2 billion people still don't have reliably safe drinking water. Another 2 billion don't have safe sanitation. The work is not finished.

## The First Lesson

Water is the one resource that nothing substitutes for. You can live weeks without food, days without sleep, but only hours to days without water. Civilizations rise where they can secure water and fall where they cannot.

The water you drink connects you to every ecosystem the rain passed through, every rock the groundwater flowed through, every human being upstream who put something in or took something out. The tap is the end of a very long pipe that includes the whole planet.
