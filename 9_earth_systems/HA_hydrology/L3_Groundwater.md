# Level 3: Groundwater
*Aquifers, wells, recharge, depletion, and the hidden water that sustains civilizations*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Hidden Water

L2 covered rivers and lakes — the visible surface water. L3 turns underground. About 30% of the world's freshwater is groundwater (the rest is ice and snow; surface freshwater is less than 1%). Groundwater supplies drinking water to ~2 billion people, irrigates ~40% of global crop production, and sustains rivers during dry seasons via baseflow. It moves slowly — days to millennia between recharge and discharge — and when poorly managed, it depletes invisibly until wells run dry and land begins to sink.

Groundwater is the hydrologist's "slow variable." Surface water responds to weeks of weather; groundwater integrates decades to centuries of climate. It is also the water most exposed to contamination that persists longest — once an aquifer is polluted, cleanup takes decades to centuries, if it's possible at all.

## Where Groundwater Lives

Groundwater occupies the pore spaces and fractures in rock and sediment. Key concepts:

**Porosity**: fraction of total rock volume that is void space. Gravels and sands: 25-40%. Shales: 5-15%. Crystalline rocks: <5% (porosity is mostly in fractures).

**Permeability**: how easily water flows through the material. Depends on pore size and connectivity, not just porosity. A clay can have 40% porosity but near-zero permeability because pores are tiny and poorly connected.

**Hydraulic conductivity (K)**: combines permeability and fluid properties. Units m/s. Ranges from ~10⁻² m/s (clean gravel) to ~10⁻¹² m/s (tight shale). Twelve orders of magnitude — the largest variation in any material property routinely encountered in earth science.

**Water table**: the surface below which the ground is saturated. Above is the unsaturated (vadose) zone where pore spaces contain both air and water.

**Aquifers**: geologic units that store and transmit useful quantities of water.
- **Unconfined aquifers**: water table is the upper boundary; direct connection to surface recharge.
- **Confined aquifers**: bounded above and below by low-permeability layers (aquitards). Water is under pressure; may rise above the top of the aquifer in wells (**artesian**).
- **Perched aquifers**: above the main water table, held on local impermeable layer.

Major aquifer types:
- **Unconsolidated sediments**: gravels, sands in river valleys, alluvial plains, coastal plains. Often the most productive.
- **Sedimentary rock aquifers**: sandstones, limestones. Limestones can develop karst — caves, sinkholes, rapid flow.
- **Volcanic aquifers**: fractured and vesicular lavas (Hawaii, Columbia Plateau, Iceland).
- **Crystalline rock aquifers**: flow through fractures only; low yield but important in shield terrains.

## Darcy's Law

Groundwater flow obeys **Darcy's law** (1856):

$$Q = -KA \frac{dh}{dl}$$

Where Q is flow rate, K hydraulic conductivity, A cross-sectional area, dh/dl the hydraulic gradient (change in head per distance).

Hydraulic **head** is the mechanical energy per unit weight: elevation + pressure head. Water flows from high head to low head. Not necessarily downhill — confined aquifers can have water flowing uphill if head gradient dictates.

Darcy's law applies in the **laminar regime** typical of most groundwater flow. Exceptions: karst systems, coarse gravels with high velocity, near pumping wells — flow becomes turbulent and Darcy breaks down.

**Dispersion**: spreading of a solute along and across flow direction. Matters for contaminant transport. Includes molecular diffusion plus mechanical mixing from velocity variations.

## Wells and Pumping

A well penetrates an aquifer, and pumping creates a **cone of depression** — lowered water table around the well. Extent depends on pumping rate and aquifer properties.

**Specific yield**: volume of water released per unit aquifer volume per unit water-table drawdown. Typical 0.1-0.3 for sand and gravel. Tells you how much storage is tapped.

**Transmissivity (T)**: hydraulic conductivity × aquifer thickness. Characterizes bulk flow capacity.

**Aquifer tests**: pump a well at known rate, measure drawdown in observation wells, fit models (Theis 1935 and variants) to estimate T and storage coefficient.

**Well design**: casing, screen, gravel pack, pump selection. Depth, diameter, and screen position determined by aquifer properties. Deep wells (>1 km) in confined aquifers can cost millions; shallow domestic wells tens of thousands.

## Recharge

**Recharge** is water entering the groundwater system. Mechanisms:
- **Diffuse infiltration**: rainfall/snowmelt percolating through soil and unsaturated zone.
- **Focused recharge**: through streambeds, playa lakes, karst features.
- **Mountain-front recharge**: runoff from mountains infiltrating basin-fill aquifers.
- **Irrigation return flow**: in agricultural areas.

Recharge rates vary enormously. Wet climates, permeable soils: hundreds of mm/yr. Semi-arid: tens of mm/yr. Arid: mm/yr or less — some desert aquifers receive essentially zero modern recharge and contain "fossil water" thousands to millions of years old.

**Dating groundwater** uses:
- **Tritium (³H)**: bomb-test pulse in 1960s, half-life 12.3 yr. Young water (<70 yr).
- **¹⁴C**: half-life 5730 yr. Water aged hundreds to tens of thousands of years.
- **Noble gases** (³⁶Cl, ⁸¹Kr, ⁴He accumulation): ranges to millions of years.
- **CFCs/SF6**: industrial chemicals with known atmospheric histories; date water from 1940s to present.

## Natural Discharge

Groundwater flows toward discharge points:
- **Springs**: where water table intersects surface, or where aquifer terminates against impermeable rock.
- **Streams (baseflow)**: groundwater maintains river flow between rain events. In temperate climates, ~50% of streamflow is baseflow.
- **Lakes, wetlands**: many are groundwater-connected.
- **Evapotranspiration**: where water table is shallow, plants transpire directly from groundwater (phreatophytes).
- **Submarine discharge**: groundwater discharges to ocean along coasts. Globally significant; delivers nutrients, carbon to marine systems.

Groundwater sustains ecosystems. Many wetlands, springs, and spring-fed rivers depend on stable groundwater levels. Pumping that lowers water tables kills these systems.

## Depletion

**Aquifer depletion** — pumping exceeding recharge — is one of the most widespread and serious water resource problems globally.

Major depleted aquifers:
- **High Plains / Ogallala** (central US): ~9% depleted overall since 1950, but >50% in southern High Plains. Irrigation of US grain belt depends on this water; eventual exhaustion will change agriculture fundamentally.
- **North China Plain**: depletion rates meters per year in some areas. Grain basket of China.
- **Indo-Gangetic Plain**: NW India depletion rates among highest in the world.
- **California's Central Valley**: multi-meter declines; land subsidence up to 9 m documented.
- **North African and Arabian aquifers**: essentially mining fossil water with no modern recharge. Nubian Sandstone, Saq-Ram aquifers.
- **Mexico City**: subsidence rates >30 cm/yr historically.

**Consequences**:
- **Falling water tables**: wells go dry, pumping costs increase, shallow wells must be replaced with deeper ones.
- **Land subsidence**: compaction of dewatered aquifer sediments. Irreversible storage loss.
- **Saltwater intrusion**: coastal aquifers draw in seawater as fresh water is extracted.
- **Reduced baseflow**: connected rivers dry up, ecosystems collapse.
- **Water quality degradation**: pumping mobilizes deeper brackish or contaminated water.

GRACE satellite gravity mission (2002-present) measures aquifer-scale mass changes; has documented depletion at continental scales across most major irrigated regions.

## Contamination

Groundwater contamination sources:
- **Industrial**: leaks from tanks, spills, landfills. TCE, PCE, petroleum, heavy metals, PFAS (forever chemicals).
- **Agricultural**: nitrate from fertilizer, pesticides. Nitrate a major drinking water concern globally.
- **Septic systems**: nitrogen, pathogens.
- **Mining**: acid mine drainage, heavy metals.
- **Saltwater intrusion**: from over-pumping coastal aquifers.
- **Naturally occurring**: arsenic (Bangladesh, Bengal, parts of US), fluoride (Africa, India, China), radon, uranium.

**Cleanup**: pump and treat (extract contaminated water, clean at surface), permeable reactive barriers, in-situ chemical oxidation, bioremediation, monitored natural attenuation. All slow — decades to centuries for many sites.

**Bangladesh arsenic crisis**: tube wells installed 1970s-80s to escape surface water pathogens drew from arsenic-bearing sediments. Millions chronically exposed; substantial cancer and morbidity burden. Awareness spread 1990s; mitigation continues.

## Groundwater Management

Legal frameworks:
- **Absolute ownership / rule of capture**: Texas historically; property owner owns water beneath. Encourages overpumping.
- **Reasonable use**: owners can use "reasonable" quantities; limits depend on state law.
- **Correlative rights**: proportional shares among overlying landowners during shortage.
- **Prior appropriation**: western US surface water doctrine increasingly extended to groundwater. "First in time, first in right."
- **Public trust / state as trustee**: various implementations recognizing groundwater as public resource.

Management tools:
- **Well permitting and metering**.
- **Pumping limits**.
- **Sustainable yield determination**.
- **Managed aquifer recharge**: intentional recharge via basins, injection wells, or channel modifications.
- **Conjunctive use**: managing surface and groundwater together to optimize both.
- **Economic instruments**: pumping fees, tradeable pumping rights.

California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA, 2014) is a major recent effort. Requires local agencies to bring stressed basins into balance by 2040. Implementation is difficult; political resistance strong where pumping reductions threaten agricultural economies.

## Karst

**Karst aquifers** (in soluble rocks — limestone, dolomite, gypsum) behave differently:
- Flow through enlarged fractures and conduits, often turbulent.
- Rapid recharge via sinkholes.
- High yields but also high vulnerability — contamination reaches aquifer quickly with limited filtration.
- Cave systems, springs, disappearing streams.

Major karst regions: Florida, much of Kentucky/Tennessee/Virginia, Yucatán, Dinaric Alps, southern France, much of China, Turkey, Cuba. Provide water to >10% of global population.

## Geothermal Groundwater

Groundwater is a heat resource:
- **Geothermal heat pumps**: ground-source heat pumps use shallow groundwater/ground for heating and cooling. Widely deployed.
- **Low-temperature geothermal**: warm groundwater (30-100°C) heats buildings directly (Iceland, Paris Basin, Idaho).
- **High-temperature geothermal**: volcanic areas, deep sedimentary basins. Electricity generation. Iceland, Philippines, Indonesia, Italy, US (The Geysers).
- **Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS)**: engineered fracturing in hot dry rock to create heat exchangers. Experimental but potentially vast resource.

## Why This Level Matters

Groundwater sustains civilization — drinking water, irrigation, industry, ecosystems. Depletion is one of the most serious and under-recognized resource challenges. Unlike surface water, depletion is invisible until wells fail. Unlike oil, groundwater depletion damages not just the resource but the land itself (subsidence) and connected ecosystems.

Managing groundwater well requires understanding of hydrogeology (physical system), monitoring (knowing what's happening), law and governance (rules of the game), and economics (incentives for users). Most water crises ahead will involve groundwater at their core.
