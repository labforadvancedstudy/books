# Level 1: The Hydrologic Cycle
*How water moves around the planet, and where it spends its time*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Inventory

Earth has about 1.386 billion cubic kilometers of water. Of this:

- **97.5% is salt water** (mostly in oceans)
- **2.5% is fresh water** — but of that:
  - 68.7% is locked in glaciers and polar ice
  - 30.1% is groundwater
  - 0.26% is in surface freshwater (lakes, rivers, swamps)
  - Tiny fractions are soil moisture, atmospheric water, living organisms

This is worth absorbing. When you say "the world has lots of water," most of it is salty and inaccessible without desalination. Of the fresh water, most is frozen or deep underground. The rivers and lakes that humans see, drink from, and mostly depend on are less than 0.01% of Earth's total water.

The atmospheric water — the clouds, the humidity, the water vapor that drives weather — is a tiny fraction (about 0.001%) of total planetary water. And yet it is in constant motion and drives the climate system.

## The Cycle

Water cycles between reservoirs. The main movements:

- **Evaporation**: liquid water becomes vapor, driven by solar energy. Oceans evaporate about 425,000 km³ per year; land adds another 71,000 km³ (from soil, vegetation, lakes).
- **Transpiration**: plants pull water up from soil and release it as vapor through leaves. Bundled with evaporation as **evapotranspiration**, which is a huge term in the land water budget.
- **Condensation**: water vapor cools and forms droplets (clouds, fog).
- **Precipitation**: droplets coalesce and fall as rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Oceans receive about 385,000 km³ per year; land receives about 111,000 km³.
- **Infiltration**: water soaks into soil.
- **Runoff**: water flows over the surface into streams and rivers, eventually returning to the ocean.
- **Groundwater flow**: slow underground movement, sometimes over thousands of years before returning to the surface or the sea.

Balance the budget: 40,000 km³ per year of net freshwater moves from ocean to land (evaporation from ocean exceeds precipitation to ocean, and the land compensates by running off that excess). This is the planetary freshwater supply.

The mean **residence time** of water in each reservoir varies:

- Atmosphere: ~8–10 days
- Rivers: weeks to months
- Large lakes: years to decades
- Soil moisture: weeks to months
- Shallow groundwater: years to centuries
- Deep groundwater: millennia
- Oceans: ~3,000 years
- Ice sheets: tens of thousands of years

A glass of water you drink contains molecules that were probably in the atmosphere a few weeks ago, but other molecules that were in the ocean 2,000 years ago, and a few that were in an ice sheet 50,000 years ago. The molecules don't care. They cycle.

## Where the Rain Falls (and Where It Doesn't)

Global average precipitation is about 990 mm per year, but the distribution is radically uneven.

- Mawsynram and Cherrapunji, India: over 11,000 mm/year. Among the wettest places on Earth.
- Mt. Waialeale, Hawaii: about 9,500 mm/year.
- Equatorial rainforests: 2,000–4,000 mm/year.
- US Midwest: 700–1,200 mm/year.
- Mediterranean climates: 400–700 mm/year.
- Saharan desert: under 25 mm/year — often zero for years on end.
- Atacama Desert, Chile: the driest non-polar place on Earth; some stations have never recorded rain.

The pattern is driven mostly by atmospheric circulation. Rising air cools and drops its moisture (rainforests at the equator). Descending air warms and absorbs moisture (deserts at 30° latitude). Mountains lift air on one side (wet windward) and dry it on the other (rain shadows — think the east side of the Sierra Nevada or the lee of the Andes).

Climate change is shifting these patterns. Most climate projections expect "wet regions get wetter, dry regions get drier," though regional predictions remain uncertain. What is certain is that precipitation extremes (both heavy rain and droughts) are becoming more frequent.

## The Watershed — Nature's Unit of Water Accounting

A **watershed** (or drainage basin) is all the land from which water flows to a single outlet — a river mouth, a lake, a coastline. Every point on Earth's land surface is in exactly one watershed. Watersheds nest: the watershed of a small stream is part of the watershed of the larger river it joins.

Watersheds are the natural unit for water management. You cannot manage downstream water quality without managing upstream land use. Every drop of water reaching the Gulf of Mexico from the Mississippi passed over some piece of land first, and picked up whatever fertilizer, sediment, or pollution was on that land.

The main watersheds of the world drain to their corresponding ocean basins. The **continental divides** — the ridges that separate watersheds on different drainages — run along major mountain chains. In North America, the Continental Divide runs through the Rockies, separating Pacific from Atlantic/Arctic drainage.

## Groundwater — The Invisible Reservoir

Water in the ground exists in two zones:

- The **vadose zone** (unsaturated zone): soil and rock with air and water in pore spaces. Plants draw from here.
- The **saturated zone**: all pore spaces filled with water. The top of this zone is the **water table**.

An **aquifer** is a layer of rock or sediment that stores and transmits usable amounts of water. Good aquifers are porous (enough space to hold water) and permeable (water can flow through). Gravel, sand, and fractured limestone make good aquifers. Clay and solid granite do not.

Humans pump groundwater faster than it recharges in many regions. The Ogallala Aquifer beneath the US Great Plains has been heavily mined for decades; water levels have dropped 30+ meters in parts. Northern India and the North China Plain are similarly depleted. These are fossil waters — they took millennia to accumulate. Once drained, they don't refill on human timescales.

Wells that once tapped the water table 10 m down may now need to reach 50 m. At some point, pumping becomes uneconomic. That is when agriculture in these regions has to adjust — by switching crops, by reducing acreage, by using more efficient irrigation, or by abandoning farming.

## Rivers — The Visible Cycle

A river's flow (discharge) is measured in cubic meters per second. The Amazon averages about 209,000 m³/s at its mouth — 20% of all river discharge on Earth. The Congo is second at about 41,000. The Mississippi averages about 17,000. The Nile about 2,800 (much reduced by human withdrawals).

Rivers **flood**. The magnitude of floods is characterized by recurrence interval: a "100-year flood" has a 1% chance of occurring in any given year. This is probabilistic, not scheduled — a 100-year flood can happen twice in a decade. Climate change is changing the baseline statistics, and historical flood frequencies are no longer reliable predictors of future ones.

Rivers also carry sediment, which builds deltas. The Nile Delta, the Mississippi Delta, the Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta — these are flat, fertile, densely populated, and sinking. Human controls (dams, levees) trap sediment upstream, starving the deltas. New Orleans is protected by levees but losing land at the coast. Bangladesh has a similar problem with more people.

## Ice — Frozen Freshwater

Most of Earth's fresh water is ice. The Antarctic Ice Sheet holds about 30 million km³ (equivalent to 58 meters of sea-level rise if fully melted, though it won't melt quickly at current rates). The Greenland Ice Sheet holds about 2.9 million km³ (about 7 meters of sea level).

Mountain glaciers — in the Himalayas, Andes, Alps, Rockies, Caucasus — are small in total volume but regionally critical. They store winter snowfall and release it slowly through spring and summer as meltwater. Billions of people depend on glacier-fed rivers for dry-season water.

Most mountain glaciers are retreating. Globally, they are losing mass at roughly 300–400 Gt per year (latest estimates). Downstream communities will see changed flow timing — more water earlier in the melt season, less later — and eventually reduced total flow as glaciers shrink.

## Why This Level Matters

Water is the most consumed resource on Earth after air. Agriculture uses about 70% of human freshwater use, industry about 20%, and domestic use about 10%. Water shortages are about to become political more than scientific: the issue isn't usually whether a region has water, but whether it has enough in the right place at the right time.

Understanding the hydrologic cycle is a prerequisite for understanding agriculture, cities, power generation (hydro, thermal cooling), ecosystem health, and sea-level rise. The cycle is coupled to climate, to land use, and to human infrastructure in ways that no single discipline owns.
