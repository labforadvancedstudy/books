# Level 1: Seawater
*What the ocean is made of, and why it moves the way it does*

## The Salt and the Rest

Seawater is roughly 96.5% water and 3.5% dissolved solids by mass. The "3.5%" is called **salinity**, and it varies — the Baltic Sea sits near 0.7%, most of the open ocean hovers between 3.3% and 3.7%, and the Red Sea and Persian Gulf reach 4%. Salinity is reported in psu (practical salinity units), numerically equivalent to parts per thousand.

If you evaporated the world ocean dry, you would be left with roughly 50 million cubic kilometers of salt. By mass, the dissolved solids break down approximately as:

- **Chloride (Cl⁻)**: 55%
- **Sodium (Na⁺)**: 31%
- **Sulfate (SO₄²⁻)**: 8%
- **Magnesium (Mg²⁺)**: 4%
- **Calcium (Ca²⁺)**: 1.2%
- **Potassium (K⁺)**: 1.1%
- Everything else (bicarbonate, bromide, boron, strontium, fluoride, and traces): <1%

The ratios of these major ions are nearly constant throughout the world ocean. This is the **principle of constant proportions** — you can measure any one major ion and infer the rest. It implies the ocean is well-mixed on long timescales (the ocean's mixing time is roughly 1,000–2,000 years).

The ocean is salty because rivers have been carrying weathered minerals into it for billions of years. The ocean loses water to evaporation (which leaves salt behind) and gains water back as rain. Over geological time, the salt accumulated.

## Temperature, Density, and Why the Deep Ocean Is Cold

Seawater density depends on both temperature and salinity. Cold water is denser. Salty water is denser. Cold *and* salty water is the densest.

Surface ocean temperatures range from about -2°C (polar seas, just above the freezing point of seawater) to about 30°C (tropics). Seawater freezes at -1.9°C, not 0°C, because dissolved salts depress the freezing point.

Below about 1,000 meters, nearly all of the ocean is between 0°C and 4°C, regardless of latitude. The reason: cold, dense water forms at high latitudes — especially in the North Atlantic and around Antarctica — and sinks. It fills the deep basins. The tropical surface is warm, but that warm layer is only a few hundred meters thick at most. The vast bulk of the ocean is cold.

This creates the **thermocline**: a layer, typically 100–1,000 m deep, where temperature drops sharply with depth. Above the thermocline, water is warm and mixes with the atmosphere. Below it, water is cold and moves slowly along deep circulation pathways. The thermocline is a barrier to vertical mixing and controls everything from fisheries productivity to tropical cyclone intensity.

## The Ocean Conveyor

Cold, dense water sinks in the North Atlantic (off Greenland) and around Antarctica. It flows along the ocean bottom, eventually reaching the Pacific and Indian Ocean basins. There it upwells slowly, joining a return flow of warmer surface water back toward the Atlantic. One complete circuit takes roughly 1,000 years.

This is the **thermohaline circulation**, also called the global conveyor belt. It transports enormous amounts of heat. The North Atlantic component (the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, or AMOC) carries warm water north in the Gulf Stream, releases heat to the atmosphere over the North Atlantic, and sends cold water back south at depth. This is why Western Europe is milder than its latitude would suggest.

The AMOC has been slowing in recent decades, likely from Greenland ice melt freshening the North Atlantic and inhibiting the dense-water formation that drives sinking. If the AMOC slowed substantially, Western European climate would shift; tropical rainfall patterns would also shift. This is a major concern in climate science.

## The Currents You See

Wind-driven surface currents form the top part of the ocean. The major ones:

- **Gulf Stream**: transports 100 million cubic meters per second of warm water from the Gulf of Mexico up the US East Coast and across the Atlantic.
- **Kuroshio**: the Pacific equivalent, off the coast of Japan.
- **Antarctic Circumpolar Current**: the only current that circles the globe unobstructed by continents; transports about 150 million cubic meters per second.
- **Equatorial currents, California Current, Peru Current, Agulhas Current**: regionally enormous flows.

These currents organize into rough circular patterns called **gyres**, one in each ocean basin (North Atlantic, South Atlantic, North Pacific, South Pacific, Indian). The gyres rotate clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern — Coriolis effect at basin scales. In the center of each gyre is a slow region where floating plastic debris accumulates (the "garbage patches").

## Waves: How Surface Disturbances Propagate

Most ocean waves are wind-driven. Wind blowing over water transfers momentum through friction and pressure differences. The size of the resulting waves depends on:

- **Wind speed**: faster wind, bigger waves (up to a saturation limit).
- **Fetch**: the distance over which wind has been blowing.
- **Duration**: how long the wind has been blowing.

A strong storm with long fetch can produce waves exceeding 15 meters. The largest reliably measured single wave, during hurricane Luis in 1995, was about 29 meters tall. Rogue waves — single waves significantly taller than surrounding waves — are real and occasionally sink ships.

Swells (waves generated elsewhere and traveling outward) can cross entire ocean basins with little energy loss. A Pacific swell generated by Antarctic storms can produce surf in California two weeks later.

Tsunamis are different. They are not wind waves; they are surface manifestations of underwater earthquakes, landslides, or volcanic eruptions. Tsunamis have wavelengths of hundreds of kilometers (wind waves have wavelengths under a kilometer) and travel at the speed of a jet aircraft across deep ocean. They grow terrifyingly tall only as they enter shallow water near coasts.

## The Ocean as Carbon Reservoir

The ocean contains about 50 times more CO₂ than the atmosphere. It has absorbed roughly 30% of human-emitted CO₂ over the industrial era. Without this uptake, atmospheric CO₂ and global warming would be substantially worse.

But dissolved CO₂ reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid, then bicarbonate and carbonate ions. As more CO₂ enters, ocean pH drops. Over the industrial era, ocean pH has dropped from about 8.2 to 8.1 — a 30% increase in hydrogen ion concentration (pH is logarithmic). This is **ocean acidification**.

Acidification threatens organisms that build calcium carbonate shells: corals, some plankton, mollusks. Their shells dissolve more easily in acidified water, and they have to spend more energy to build and maintain them. This is one of the more certain consequences of elevated atmospheric CO₂, and its effects are already visible in coral reefs and some shellfish nurseries.

## The Ocean Under Pressure

Water is nearly incompressible, but not perfectly so. At the bottom of the Mariana Trench, 11 km down, pressure is about 1,100 times atmospheric. Water density there is about 4.5% higher than at the surface due to compression.

Pressure plus cold plus darkness defines the deep-ocean environment. Sunlight penetrates only the upper 200 meters or so. Below that is the twilight zone, then the aphotic zone where no sunlight reaches. Life in the deep ocean runs on marine snow (organic debris falling from above) and at hydrothermal vents (where chemosynthetic bacteria convert chemical energy from vent fluids).

Diving submersibles and remote-operated vehicles have explored less of the ocean floor than satellites have mapped the surface of Mars. The deep ocean remains one of the least-known environments on Earth.

## Why This Level Matters

The ocean is a single, interconnected, physically stratified system. Salinity, temperature, density, currents, and waves are all coupled. The ocean regulates climate on timescales from weeks to millennia. It absorbs most of the excess heat from the atmosphere and a substantial fraction of the excess CO₂.

It also feeds roughly 3 billion people as a significant source of protein, moves about 90% of international trade on its surface, and hosts the largest living systems on Earth.

Once you understand the physical structure of seawater, you can start asking how life organizes around it, how climate and ocean dynamics interact, and how human activities are pushing the system toward new regimes.
