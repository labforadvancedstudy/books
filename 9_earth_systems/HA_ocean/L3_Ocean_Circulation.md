## The Moving Ocean

L2 covered marine life — the biosphere of the ocean. L3 turns to the fluid itself. The ocean is in constant motion at every scale — from millimeter-scale turbulence to global circulations that take a thousand years to close. It moves heat from equator to pole, carbon between atmosphere and deep sea, nutrients from depth to surface. It sets regional climates (Europe's mildness, Peru's fisheries, the monsoons) and paces some of the slowest changes the planet undergoes.

Understanding ocean circulation requires physics — forces, pressure, density, rotation, stratification — applied to a spherical fluid in a spinning reference frame. The governing equations are the same Navier-Stokes and thermodynamics as atmosphere. But the ocean's density stratification is sharper, its basins shape circulation more tightly, and its time scales stretch longer — from days (wind-driven response) to millennia (deep-ocean turnover).

## Forces and Balances

Large-scale ocean flow is approximately **geostrophic**: pressure gradient balances Coriolis force. A sloping sea surface (highs and lows in sea surface height) corresponds to currents flowing parallel to isobars, analogous to atmospheric geostrophic winds. Sea level rises ~1 m across the Gulf Stream, which the ~1 m/s current balances.

**Ekman transport**: wind stress at the surface transfers momentum to the water. Coriolis deflects the flow 90° to the right in the Northern Hemisphere (left in Southern). Net transport over the Ekman layer (top ~50-100 m) is perpendicular to wind.

Ekman divergence and convergence drive **upwelling** and **downwelling**:
- Alongshore winds parallel to a coast drive offshore or onshore Ekman transport; the former drives coastal upwelling (rich fisheries of Peru, California, West Africa, Portugal).
- The interior of subtropical gyres experiences downward Ekman pumping; the interior of subpolar gyres experiences upward Ekman suction.

**Thermal wind balance**: vertical shear of horizontal currents is linked to horizontal density gradients. Explains why currents weaken with depth when surface waters are warmer than deep.

## Wind-Driven Circulation: The Gyres

The subtropical and subpolar **gyres** are the dominant surface features, one in each ocean basin per hemisphere.

**Subtropical gyres** (North Pacific, South Pacific, North Atlantic, South Atlantic, Indian): anticyclonic (clockwise in NH, counterclockwise in SH). Driven by the mean wind pattern — trade winds equatorward, westerlies poleward. Contain:

- **Western boundary currents**: narrow, fast, deep. Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Agulhas, Brazil Current, East Australian Current. Transport tens of Sverdrups (1 Sv = 10⁶ m³/s).
- **Broad interior returns**: slow, broad, southward (NH) or northward (SH) flow.
- **Eastern boundary currents**: cooler, slower. California Current, Canary Current, Benguela, Peru (Humboldt), Leeuwin.

The **western intensification** (Stommel, 1948) arises because the Coriolis parameter f varies with latitude (β-effect) combined with friction. All western boundaries in subtropical gyres are strong, narrow, warm currents; eastern boundaries are diffuse, cold.

**Subpolar gyres** (North Pacific, North Atlantic): cyclonic. Weaker in S. Hemisphere because the Antarctic Circumpolar Current dominates there.

**Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC)**: the only current that circles the globe, unblocked by continents. Transports ~140 Sv eastward around Antarctica. Critical for global ocean circulation and carbon cycling.

## Equatorial Dynamics

Near the equator, Coriolis vanishes, and dynamics are qualitatively different. Wind-driven Ekman flow diverges from the equator in both hemispheres, driving equatorial upwelling — nutrient-rich cold tongue across the Pacific and Atlantic. Equatorial undercurrents flow eastward at 50-100 m depth.

**Equatorial Kelvin waves**: trapped waves that travel eastward at ~2-3 m/s. Propagate in hours-to-weeks; central to ENSO dynamics.

**Rossby waves**: slower westward-propagating waves; the mechanism by which ocean adjusts to changed wind forcing. Take months to cross the Pacific.

ENSO (covered at climate dynamics L3) is fundamentally an ocean-atmosphere coupled mode driven by these wave dynamics in the equatorial Pacific.

## Thermohaline Circulation

Wind drives surface circulation. Density drives deep circulation. Cold, salty water is denser and sinks; the resulting **thermohaline circulation** (THC) or **meridional overturning circulation (MOC)** is the slow global conveyor belt.

Main features:
- **North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW)** formation: in the Labrador and Nordic seas, winter cooling and sea ice formation produce cold (<4°C), saline water that sinks to 2-3 km and flows southward.
- **Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW)** formation: in the Weddell and Ross Seas, brine rejection during sea ice formation produces the densest water in the global ocean, flowing along the seafloor.
- **Upwelling**: diffuse upward flow across the global thermocline, plus wind-driven upwelling in the Southern Ocean.
- **Return flow**: warm surface waters flow from Indo-Pacific and Atlantic toward the North Atlantic, closing the loop.

Complete turnover takes ~1000-2000 years. This sets the maximum rate at which the deep ocean can exchange heat and carbon with the surface.

**Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC)**: the Atlantic component, ~15-18 Sv, carries ~1 PW of heat northward. Responsible for the North Atlantic's mild climate. Evidence suggests AMOC has weakened ~15% since mid-20th century; likely to weaken further under greenhouse warming. Collapse scenarios (covered in paleoclimate) show abrupt regional cooling in Europe and NE North America.

## Water Masses

The ocean consists of identifiable **water masses** — volumes with characteristic temperature-salinity (T-S) signatures reflecting where and when they formed.

Major water masses:
- **Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW)**: densest, 0-1°C, 34.6 PSU.
- **North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW)**: ~3°C, 34.9 PSU.
- **Antarctic Intermediate Water (AAIW)**: ~5°C, 34.3 PSU. Formed at subantarctic front.
- **Mode waters**: subduction of winter mixed layers in subtropical gyres.
- **Mediterranean Water**: warm, saline outflow through Gibraltar; spreads through Atlantic.

T-S diagrams trace water masses through the ocean; mixing among them is tracked by tritium, CFCs, ¹⁴C, helium isotopes — age tracers that calibrate circulation timescales.

## Eddies and Mesoscale

Surface ocean circulation, like the atmosphere, has a rich **mesoscale** — eddies, rings, meanders at scales of ~10-200 km. Eddies dominate ocean kinetic energy; the Gulf Stream pinches off rings that travel for months.

Eddies transport heat, salt, nutrients, and tracers. Ocean models below ~25 km resolution (eddy-resolving) produce qualitatively different circulation than coarser ones. Satellite altimetry since 1992 has revolutionized eddy observation.

Sub-mesoscale (1-10 km) and smaller scales matter for mixing and biology. The full turbulent cascade connects large-scale circulation to millimeter-scale dissipation; understanding mixing rates in the ocean interior remains a research frontier.

## Ocean-Atmosphere Coupling

Ocean and atmosphere are coupled through fluxes at the sea surface:
- **Heat flux**: shortwave, longwave, sensible, latent. Dominated by latent (evaporation) in tropics, by sensible in winter high latitudes.
- **Momentum flux**: wind stress drives ocean circulation; ocean SST affects atmospheric convection and winds.
- **Freshwater flux**: evaporation minus precipitation plus runoff. Drives salinity patterns.
- **Gas fluxes**: CO₂, O₂, other gases exchange across sea surface. Global ocean absorbs ~25% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions.

Modes of variability — ENSO, PDO, NAO, Indian Ocean Dipole — all emerge from ocean-atmosphere coupling. Regional climates depend on these coupled modes as much as on atmospheric dynamics alone.

## Sea Level

**Global mean sea level** has risen ~20 cm since 1900 and is accelerating. Current rate >4 mm/yr. Components:
- **Thermal expansion**: warmer water occupies more volume. ~40% of recent rise.
- **Glacier melt**: mountain glaciers losing mass globally. ~20-25%.
- **Greenland ice sheet loss**: accelerating. ~10-15%.
- **Antarctic ice sheet loss**: dominated by West Antarctic; acceleration concerning. ~5-10%.
- **Terrestrial water storage**: groundwater depletion, reservoir impoundment. Small net contribution.

**Regional sea level** varies much more than the global mean — up to factor of several in both directions. Drivers: ocean dynamics (wind, currents, temperature), gravitational redistribution from ice loss, vertical land motion (tectonic, glacial rebound, subsidence from groundwater extraction).

Sea level projections to 2100 range from ~0.3 m (low emissions, low ice-sheet instability) to >1 m (high emissions, with possible ice-sheet instability contribution); tail risks of 2+ m cannot be ruled out under plausible ice-sheet dynamics scenarios.

## Ocean Carbon Cycle

The ocean is a major carbon reservoir — ~40,000 GtC, vs. ~800 GtC in atmosphere. Carbon exchange:
- **Physical pump**: cold water dissolves more CO₂; high-latitude sinking transports dissolved carbon to deep ocean.
- **Biological pump**: phytoplankton fix CO₂; some organic matter sinks below mixed layer, locking carbon away for centuries to millennia.
- **Carbonate pump**: calcifiers (corals, coccolithophores, foraminifera) produce CaCO₃; dissolution in deep water redistributes alkalinity and carbon.

**Ocean acidification**: CO₂ uptake forms carbonic acid, lowering pH. Surface pH has dropped ~0.1 since pre-industrial (~30% increase in H⁺ concentration). Projected to drop another 0.2-0.3 by 2100 under high emissions. Threatens calcifiers — corals, pteropods, shellfish.

Ocean is absorbing ~25% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions but this rate could weaken or reverse in some scenarios. Deep ocean remains undersaturated on human timescales; full equilibration takes millennia.

## Observation and Modeling

Ocean observation:
- **Argo**: ~4000 autonomous profiling floats measuring T and S to 2000 m, continuously since 2000s. Transformative for ocean state monitoring.
- **Satellite altimetry** (TOPEX/POSEIDON, Jason series, Sentinel-6): sea surface height globally to cm precision.
- **Moorings and gliders**: targeted observations of key regions (TAO array in equatorial Pacific, RAPID array across Atlantic).
- **Research ships**: water column profiles (CTD casts), biology, geochemistry.
- **Drifters, XBTs, Seagliders**: distributed observations.

Ocean general circulation models (OGCMs) solve same primitive equations as atmospheric models. Modern global ocean models use ~10-25 km horizontal resolution; regional models finer. Coupled to atmosphere, sea ice, biogeochemistry in Earth System Models.

## Why This Level Matters

The ocean mediates climate on timescales from seasonal to millennial. It carries heat that moderates Europe's climate, drives fisheries that feed a billion people, absorbs carbon that buffers atmospheric CO₂ increase, and may contain tipping points (AMOC collapse, ice-sheet-driven sea level surge) that could reorganize global climate abruptly.

Ocean science is also where the boundary between physics and biology is sharpest — water masses carry specific nutrients that control biological productivity; biology pulls down carbon to depth. Understanding the coupling is essential for predicting both physical climate and marine ecosystem futures.
