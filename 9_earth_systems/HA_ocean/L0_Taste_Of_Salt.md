# Level 0: The Taste of Salt
*Where everyone meets the ocean: in a mouthful of seawater*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook — Phase 2B stub -->

> "We are tied to the ocean. And when we go back to the sea, whether it is to sail or to watch it, we are going back from whence we came." — JFK, 1962

## The Salt That Isn't Just Salt

You take a wave in the mouth. It burns. You spit. The taste — salt, bitter, slightly metallic — is the chemistry of 4 billion years of rock weathering. Rain falling on land dissolves ions from rock, rivers carry them to the sea, the water evaporates but the ions stay. That slow leaching has been running since continents existed. The ocean is the dissolved rind of every continent that ever eroded.

Seawater is not salty the way table salt is salty. It contains roughly 35 grams per kilogram of dissolved minerals: chloride (55%), sodium (31%), sulfate (8%), magnesium (4%), calcium (1%), potassium (1%), and traces of everything. Every element on the periodic table is in seawater somewhere, at some concentration.

## The Bigger Ocean You Can't See

The surface is not the ocean. It is the lid. The ocean is on average 3.7 kilometers deep. Most of its volume is cold (2-4°C), dark, and under enormous pressure. If you stacked Mount Everest on its deepest point, the summit would still be 2 km underwater.

Most photos you've seen of the ocean show the top few meters. There is a whole second planet below, full of creatures that never see the sun, powered partly by the slow rain of dead surface life falling down, and partly by chemosynthesis at deep-sea vents. We have mapped Mars better than we have mapped the deep ocean.

## The River That Has No Banks

The ocean is not a bathtub. It has currents — persistent, organized flows as powerful as the largest rivers on land combined.

The Gulf Stream carries about 30 million cubic meters of water per second past Florida. That is 150 times all the world's rivers combined. It carries tropical heat north, keeps Western Europe warm, shapes the climate of the entire North Atlantic.

Below that, in the deep, there is a slower current that sinks near Greenland, flows south along the ocean floor, loops around Antarctica, comes up in the Pacific, and eventually returns. A single water molecule takes roughly a thousand years to complete this loop. This is the thermohaline circulation, and it moves more heat than anything else on the planet.

## The Breath You Borrow

Every second breath you take contains oxygen that was made by ocean plankton. Roughly half the planet's oxygen production happens in seawater, mostly by microscopic photosynthetic organisms you will never see.

You owe every odd-numbered breath to single-celled creatures floating in salt water. This is not metaphor. This is gas chemistry.

## The Buffer You Don't Notice

The ocean has absorbed roughly 30% of human CO₂ emissions since the industrial revolution. It has also absorbed roughly 90% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases. Without the ocean, the atmosphere would already be far warmer than it is.

But this service comes with a cost. Dissolving CO₂ in water makes the water more acidic. The ocean's average pH has dropped about 0.1 units since 1850 — which sounds small until you remember pH is logarithmic. That is a 30% increase in hydrogen ion concentration. Coral reefs and shell-forming plankton are the first to notice. They're losing ground.

## The Waves That Never Belonged to You

Watch a wave come in. That wave has been traveling. The wind that started it might have blown two thousand kilometers away, two days ago. The energy in that wave is not from the water you see; the water stays mostly in place, only the energy moves. When the wave hits a shore, water that has been circling in open ocean for weeks finally hits rock, turns foamy, and dies.

Each wave is the last few meters of a very long journey of pure energy across the sea.

## The Beach That Used to Be Somewhere Else

The sand between your toes was probably mountain once. Or river sediment. Or reef fragments. Or shell. Beaches are temporary — they exist where the physics of waves and currents deposits loose material faster than it erodes it. Move one jetty and entire beaches migrate over decades.

Sea level itself is not fixed. It is rising, currently about 3.4 millimeters per year, driven by thermal expansion (warm water takes more volume) and melting ice. This sounds slow. It is not. Coastal cities — Shanghai, Miami, Dhaka, Jakarta — are already budgeting for centuries of storm surge on top of a higher baseline.

## The First Lesson

The ocean is not a place. It is a system. Currents that span continents, chemistry that buffers the atmosphere, life that makes the oxygen you breathe, a memory of heat and carbon that will outlast civilizations. The surface you swim in is the interface between a big hot engine and the sky above it.

You taste salt. Behind that salt is most of what makes Earth livable.
