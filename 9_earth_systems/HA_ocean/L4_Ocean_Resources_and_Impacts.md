## The Ocean as Commons, Resource, and Victim

L3 covered ocean circulation — how the ocean moves. L4 turns to the ocean as a human resource and as a system under human impact. The ocean covers 71% of Earth's surface, contains 97% of the water, produces ~50% of atmospheric oxygen (through phytoplankton), absorbs ~25% of anthropogenic CO₂, absorbs >90% of the heat trapped by greenhouse gases, supports fisheries that feed billions, underpins $1.5+ T of annual shipping, and holds growing energy and mineral resources. It is also a global commons with ambiguous governance and a repository for pollution.

Human impacts on the ocean have accelerated dramatically in the industrial era. Overfishing, eutrophication, plastic pollution, noise, shipping emissions, deep-sea extraction, and — overshadowing them all — warming, acidification, and deoxygenation from climate change are stressing marine systems at rates unprecedented in millions of years.

This level covers fisheries, coastal systems, marine biodiversity, shipping, offshore energy, deep sea, and the governance frameworks that shape outcomes.

## Fisheries

Marine capture fisheries harvest ~90 Mt/yr globally. Plus aquaculture ~85 Mt/yr. Together ~$400B/yr industry; ~60M people employed directly; ~10% of global protein.

**State of stocks** (FAO): ~35% of monitored stocks overfished, ~60% fished at maximum sustainable level, <10% underfished. Overfishing has continued despite decades of reform attempts.

**Major fisheries**:
- **Anchoveta (Peru)**: ~5 Mt/yr. Highly variable with El Niño.
- **Alaska pollock**: well-managed, high yield.
- **Skipjack tuna**: largest tuna fishery.
- **Atlantic cod**: historically massive (Grand Banks); collapsed 1992 after decades of overfishing; slow/partial recovery.
- **Northern bluefin tuna**: overfished; managed recovery ongoing.

**Overfishing dynamics**:
- **Tragedy of the commons**: individual incentives to maximize catch exceed collective sustainability.
- **Subsidies**: ~$35B/yr globally, significant fraction harmful (encouraging overcapacity, distant-water fishing). WTO agreement 2022 to reduce harmful subsidies; implementation limited.
- **Illegal, Unreported, Unregulated (IUU) fishing**: 10-30% of global catch; hard to quantify.
- **Bycatch**: non-target species caught and often discarded. Turtles, sharks, seabirds, juvenile fish.
- **Ghost fishing**: lost gear continues to catch.
- **Fishing down food webs**: as large predators deplete, fisheries shift to smaller species; indicator of degraded ecosystem.

**Management tools**:
- **Total allowable catch (TAC)**: science-based quota. Iceland, Norway, New Zealand successes.
- **Individual transferable quotas (ITQs)**: privatized catch shares reduce race-to-fish. Mixed equity outcomes.
- **Marine protected areas (MPAs)**: spatial closures. "Fully protected" MPAs ~3% of ocean; effective MPAs less. 30×30 goal (30% of ocean by 2030) pushed but contested.
- **Gear restrictions**: bans on bottom trawling in sensitive areas, restrictions on net types.
- **Seasonal closures, size limits**.
- **Monitoring, control, surveillance**: satellites, observers, electronic monitoring, port controls.

**Successes**: US fisheries (Magnuson-Stevens Act 1976, reauthorized); Namibian hake; Chilean jack mackerel recovery; New Zealand comprehensive system. Evidence that well-managed fisheries recover and sustain.

**Distant-water fleets**: China, Taiwan, Korea, EU, Russia fish beyond their EEZs. Complex regulation under regional fisheries management organizations (RFMOs); effectiveness varies.

**High seas fishing**: ~12% of global catch from beyond EEZs. Poorly regulated; new BBNJ (Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction) agreement 2023 creates framework.

## Aquaculture

Fastest-growing food sector. Surpassed wild capture ~2014. Now ~50% of fish consumed.

**Species**: salmon (Norway, Chile), shrimp (SE Asia, Ecuador), tilapia, catfish, carp, oysters, mussels, kelp.

**Land-based vs. ocean-based**: net pens in coastal waters common; onshore recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS) reduce environmental interaction but higher energy.

**Environmental concerns**:
- **Wild fish feed**: many species need fishmeal/oil from wild capture. Improving feed conversion and alternative feeds (algae-based, insect-based) reducing pressure.
- **Disease and escapes**: concentrated stocks spread disease; escapees affect wild populations.
- **Effluent**: nutrients, antibiotics, parasiticides.
- **Habitat**: coastal mangrove destruction for shrimp farms historically; regulation and restoration improving.

**Positive trends**:
- **Shellfish, seaweed**: net environmental positives; absorb nutrients, require no feed.
- **Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture**: combines species to recycle nutrients.
- **Offshore farms**: deeper water with better flushing.

## Coastal Systems

Coastal zones — the most populated and economically important. Land use, fishing, shipping, recreation, industry all compete.

**Coral reefs**:
- Cover <0.1% of ocean area but host ~25% of marine species.
- Mass bleaching (warm water expels symbiotic algae) now common. 1998, 2010, 2014-17, 2023-24 major global bleaching events.
- ~50% of reefs lost in last 50 years.
- Acidification weakens calcifying organisms.
- At 1.5°C warming, ~70-90% of reefs degraded; at 2°C, >99% lost per IPCC.
- Intervention research (assisted evolution, selective breeding, outplanting) emerging.

**Mangroves**: coastal tropical forests. Nursery habitat, coastal protection, high carbon storage.
- ~35% lost over last few decades; rate slowing.
- Restoration increasingly funded as "blue carbon" credit.

**Seagrass**: similar role; declining; restoration in some places (Chesapeake, UK).

**Kelp forests**: temperate; cold-water equivalents of reefs for biodiversity. Some declining (California, Tasmania) due to warming and urchin overgrazing after sea otter/lobster declines.

**Salt marshes**: coastal wetlands; storm protection, carbon, biodiversity. Sea level rise can drown if sediment supply low.

**Estuaries**: mixing zones, fisheries nurseries. Nutrient pollution stresses; dead zones in many (Gulf of Mexico, Chesapeake, Baltic).

**Beaches and barrier islands**: erosion, sea level rise, storm damage. Beach nourishment (pumping sand) common but expensive and temporary. Managed retreat sometimes alternative.

**Sea level rise**: major coastal issue. Projected 0.3-1.0 m by 2100 depending on emissions and ice sheet behavior; much more thereafter. Compound impacts with subsidence, storm surge, king tides.

## Marine Biodiversity

~250,000+ known marine species; estimates of total range 1-10M.

**Threats**:
- **Fishing** (covered above).
- **Climate change**: warming, acidification, oxygen loss affecting distributions and physiology.
- **Pollution**: nutrients, plastics, chemicals, oil.
- **Habitat destruction**: coastal development, bottom trawling damaging seabed.
- **Invasive species**: via ballast water (now regulated), hull fouling.
- **Noise**: shipping, sonar, seismic exploration affects whales especially.
- **Shipping strikes**: North Atlantic right whale at critical risk.

**Conservation**:
- **CITES**: international trade restrictions on endangered species.
- **MPAs**: spatial protection.
- **Species-specific management**: turtle nesting protection, marine mammal regulations.
- **Bycatch reduction**: turtle excluder devices, bird-scaring lines.

**Success stories**: gray whale recovery, some seabirds, Mediterranean monk seal beginning. Slow but possible.

## Ocean Acidification

CO₂ dissolved in seawater forms carbonic acid. Ocean pH has dropped ~0.1 units (preindustrial to present), ~30% increase in hydrogen ion concentration.

Effects:
- **Calcifying organisms** struggle: corals, some mollusks, some plankton (pteropods, coccolithophores).
- **Shellfish industry**: Pacific Northwest oyster crisis 2005-08 linked to upwelled acidic water. Adaptive hatchery practices developed.
- **Food web**: impacts on foundational species cascade.

At projected emissions, ocean chemistry in 2100 may have no analog in millions of years.

## Deoxygenation

Warming reduces gas solubility; stratification reduces mixing. Ocean has lost ~2% of oxygen since 1960.

**Oxygen minimum zones (OMZs)** expanding. Low-oxygen "dead zones" in coastal waters from nutrient pollution increasing in number and area.

Consequences: habitat compression for oxygen-needing species; altered microbial communities (including those producing greenhouse gases).

## Plastic Pollution

Ocean plastic ~11 Mt/yr input; cumulative tens-hundreds of millions of tons.

**Macroplastics**: bottles, bags, fishing gear. Ghost fishing, entanglement of marine life.

**Microplastics** (<5 mm): fragments, fibers, pellets. Ubiquitous in seawater, sediment, marine life, including fish we eat. Health effects debated.

**Garbage patches**: rotating ocean gyres concentrate floating debris. Great Pacific Garbage Patch highest-profile; Atlantic, Indian also.

**Sources**: ~80% land-based (rivers, wastewater, dumping); 20% from ships and fishing.

**Responses**:
- **Single-use plastic bans**: many jurisdictions, variable scope.
- **Extended producer responsibility**: making producers fund recovery.
- **Cleanup technologies** (Ocean Cleanup, others): captures fraction; inherently downstream.
- **Reduction at source**: by far most effective.
- **UN Plastics Treaty**: negotiations ongoing 2024-25.

## Shipping

Global shipping carries ~80% of goods by volume. ~100,000 commercial vessels.

**Emissions**:
- ~3% of global CO₂.
- NOx, SOx (regulated 2020 to <0.5% fuel sulfur globally, dramatic improvement).
- Black carbon.
- Noise.

**IMO (International Maritime Organization)**: sets emission and safety standards. 2023 strategy: net-zero GHG by ~2050; carbon pricing in discussion.

**Decarbonization**:
- **Efficiency**: slow steaming, hull optimization, wind assistance (rotor sails).
- **Alternative fuels**: LNG (interim, methane slip concerns), methanol, ammonia (future), hydrogen, biofuels.
- **Electric short-sea and inland**.
- **Port electrification** (shore power, terminal electrification).

**Arctic routes**: Northern Sea Route and Northwest Passage opening with ice retreat. Shortens Asia-Europe routes. Environmental concerns (spills, wildlife).

## Offshore Energy

**Offshore oil and gas**: ~30% of global oil production. Gulf of Mexico, North Sea, Brazil pre-salt, Middle East, West Africa, new discoveries in Guyana, Namibia.

**Offshore wind**: fast-growing; ~60 GW installed 2023, targets of hundreds of GW. Europe leader (UK, Germany, Denmark, Netherlands); China largest installer recently; US build-out starting.

**Floating wind**: for deeper water; early commercial. Unlocks Pacific, Japanese, and deep European waters.

**Wave and tidal**: small; niche applications; research continues.

**Ocean thermal energy conversion (OTEC)**: theoretical; few pilots; economics challenging.

**Offshore hydrogen**: produced from offshore wind; potential to transport as ammonia or liquid hydrogen.

## Deep Sea and Seabed

Deep sea (>200m) is largest habitat on Earth. Understudied.

**Biodiversity**: unique ecosystems (cold seeps, hydrothermal vents, seamounts). Slow-growing, long-lived species.

**Deep-sea mining**:
- **Polymetallic nodules**: on abyssal plains; contain Mn, Ni, Co, Cu. Clarion-Clipperton Zone (Pacific) largest deposit.
- **Seafloor massive sulfides**: at hydrothermal vents.
- **Cobalt-rich crusts**: on seamounts.

**International Seabed Authority (ISA)** regulates in international waters. Several exploration licenses; no commercial extraction yet (as of 2024-25). Environmental concerns substantial; science incomplete; moratorium/precautionary pause supported by many countries and scientists.

**Submarine cables**: ~99% of intercontinental data traffic via seabed fiber cables. Vulnerable to accidents, fishing, geological events. Military significance.

**Military**: submarine warfare, sonar effects on marine life, undersea infrastructure security.

## Ocean Governance

**UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982)**:
- **Territorial sea**: 12 nm from coast.
- **EEZ**: 200 nm; exclusive resource rights.
- **Continental shelf**: extended rights for seabed resources to continental margin.
- **High seas**: beyond EEZs; freedom of navigation, fishing (subject to conservation).
- **Area**: seabed beyond national jurisdiction, common heritage of mankind; ISA administers.

**BBNJ Agreement (2023)**: implementing agreement under UNCLOS for biodiversity in ABNJ. Includes MPA framework, impact assessment, genetic resource access, capacity building. Entry into force underway.

**Regional seas agreements**: Mediterranean (Barcelona Convention), North-East Atlantic (OSPAR), Baltic (HELCOM), Caribbean, others. Generally effective for coordination.

**Regional fisheries management organizations**: specific stocks and regions. Effectiveness highly variable — some (ICCAT, IATTC) strong, others weak.

**Climate governance**: UNFCCC applies to ocean indirectly; ocean increasingly integrated.

**Challenges**:
- Commons dynamic: ocean hard to fence.
- Jurisdictional complexity: flag state vs. port state vs. coastal state.
- Enforcement at scale limited.
- Emerging issues (microplastics, noise, deep-sea mining) ahead of regulation.

## Blue Economy

"Blue economy" frames ocean sectors as economic opportunity aligned with sustainability. ~$1.5-2.5T annual output globally; projected to grow.

Sectors: fisheries, aquaculture, shipping, tourism, offshore energy, biotechnology, seabed mining, desalination, carbon sequestration, climate adaptation services.

Tensions: sustainability vs. growth, equity (coastal communities vs. distant-water industries), governance gaps.

## Blue Carbon

Carbon stored in marine ecosystems:
- **Mangroves, salt marshes, seagrass**: per-area carbon stocks among highest of any ecosystem. Protecting and restoring = climate mitigation + ecosystem services.
- **Kelp**: role debated; harvested kelp's carbon fate uncertain.
- **Open ocean**: biological pump transfers carbon to depth; long-term storage. Manipulations (iron fertilization, artificial upwelling) proposed but controversial.

Blue carbon credits emerging in carbon markets; need robust verification.

## Ocean Observation and Science

Investment in monitoring growing:
- **Argo floats**: ~4,000 autonomous profilers measure temperature, salinity, biogeochemistry.
- **Satellites**: altimetry (sea level), color (chlorophyll, sediments), temperature, wind.
- **Moorings**: long-term point measurements.
- **Ships**: research cruises; increasingly autonomous vessels.
- **Gliders, AUVs**: flexible platforms.
- **eDNA (environmental DNA)**: biodiversity monitoring from water samples.
- **Underwater acoustic networks**: for whales, infrastructure, science.

**Decade of Ocean Science (2021-30)**: UN framework for coordinated ocean research.

## Why This Level Matters

The ocean is the largest life-support system on Earth and humanity's most abused commons. Its state in 2100 will depend on decisions made in the next decades about fishing, pollution, coastal development, offshore energy, deep-sea extraction, and above all climate.

For billions of people, the ocean is direct livelihood (fishing, aquaculture, shipping, tourism). For the rest, it is oxygen, climate regulation, protein, and weather. For every human, it is half of a planetary biogeochemistry that will either stabilize or destabilize our civilization.

Engineers, biologists, economists, policymakers, and citizens engage with ocean issues through different frames but share the underlying reality: we have one ocean, it has limits, and current trajectories are not sustainable. Better governance, better science, and better economics can change outcomes. The window for many of the most vulnerable ecosystems (coral reefs particularly) is closing fast.
