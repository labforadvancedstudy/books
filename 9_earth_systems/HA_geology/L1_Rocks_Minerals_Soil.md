# Level 1: Rocks, Minerals, Soil
*Where geology stops being scenery and starts being an inventory*

## Three Rock Families, No More

Every rock on Earth belongs to one of three families based on how it formed:

- **Igneous**: cooled from molten rock. Granite on a mountain summit, basalt in a highway cut, obsidian from a volcanic glass flow.
- **Sedimentary**: built up grain by grain, usually under water, then compressed. Sandstone, shale, limestone. Most of the rock you see on the surface of continents is sedimentary because sediments pile up where humans live.
- **Metamorphic**: existing rock cooked and squeezed into something new. Shale becomes slate becomes schist becomes gneiss as temperature and pressure climb. Limestone becomes marble. Sandstone becomes quartzite.

The three families interconvert. Magma cools into igneous rock. Wind and water break it down into sediment. Sediment compacts into sedimentary rock. Deep burial metamorphoses it. Remelting sends it back to magma. This is the **rock cycle**, discovered by James Hutton in the 1780s, and it runs on timescales of millions to billions of years.

Every rock in your hand is somewhere in this cycle. The quartz pebble on the beach was probably once part of a granite mountain that eroded a hundred million years ago.

## Minerals Are the Vocabulary, Rocks Are the Sentences

A rock is a mixture. A mineral is a single chemical compound with a definite composition and an ordered crystal structure. Quartz is SiO₂, always. Feldspar is a family of silicates with potassium, sodium, or calcium. Mica is layered, which is why it peels.

Of roughly 5,000 named minerals, about a dozen make up most of the Earth's crust. In descending order of abundance: feldspar (about 60%), quartz (12%), pyroxene, amphibole, mica, olivine, clay minerals, and the carbonates (calcite, dolomite). Almost everything else is rare.

**Identification is a bench craft.** Geologists use:
- Hardness (the Mohs scale, 1–10, where talc is 1 and diamond is 10)
- Color (unreliable on its own — many minerals vary)
- Streak (the color when ground to powder — more reliable)
- Luster (how light reflects — metallic, glassy, dull, pearly)
- Cleavage and fracture (how it breaks — along flat planes or conchoidally)
- Density (feel it in your hand)
- Crystal shape (when visible)

A student with a hand lens, a hardness kit, a streak plate, and a bottle of dilute HCl can identify the common minerals in about 30 seconds each. Limestone fizzes in HCl. Dolomite fizzes only when scratched first. Quartz does nothing. This is knowledge a field geologist uses every day.

## The Soil You're Walking On

Soil is not just dirt. It is a layered, biologically active, partially weathered rock-organic hybrid, and it is the thin layer that keeps terrestrial life alive.

A vertical cut through mature soil (a **profile**) typically shows:
- **O horizon**: surface organic matter — leaves, twigs, decomposing material.
- **A horizon** (topsoil): mineral soil enriched with humus. This is what you grow crops in.
- **B horizon** (subsoil): clay and minerals leached down from above. Often redder or yellower due to iron oxides.
- **C horizon**: weathered parent rock — the bedrock underneath, broken down but not yet fully soil.
- **R horizon**: unweathered bedrock.

Soil forms slowly. One inch of topsoil can take 500–1,000 years to develop naturally. Industrial agriculture erodes topsoil at roughly ten times the rate of soil formation in many regions. This is not sustainable. It is one of the slow-moving crises of the 21st century.

**The ingredients of soil, by rough volume**:
- 45% mineral matter (sand, silt, clay — derived from weathered rock)
- 25% water
- 25% air (in pore spaces)
- 5% organic matter (a number that matters enormously — the difference between a fertile field and a dust bowl is often a few percent)

## Why Steel Doesn't Occur in Nature

Most minerals that humans use industrially are not pure elements. Iron comes from iron oxide ores (hematite, magnetite). Aluminum comes from bauxite (aluminum hydroxides). Copper comes from sulfide and oxide ores. Gold, silver, and platinum are found as native metals only because they are chemically unreactive — most reactive metals long ago bonded to oxygen or sulfur.

Mining is geology turned economic. A **deposit** is a concentration of an ore mineral in quantities large enough and grades high enough to extract profitably. Whether a deposit is economic depends on the metal's price, the ore's concentration, the depth, the local infrastructure, and the environmental regulations. The richest copper deposits in the early 20th century were 5–10% copper. Today we mine ores at 0.3–0.5% copper because the rich deposits are largely exhausted and extraction technology has improved.

This is a Red Queen's race. Every generation mines lower-grade ores than the previous one, using more energy to extract the same amount of metal. Recycling metal is much cheaper energetically than smelting new ore. This is one reason electric-vehicle sourcing strategies increasingly plan for end-of-life battery recovery.

## The Rocks You Can't See But Stand On

The continents are mostly granite and sediment. The ocean floors are mostly basalt, covered in a thin layer of seafloor sediment. Below both, the **mantle** — 2,900 km thick, made mostly of a rock called peridotite, rich in the mineral olivine.

We have almost no direct samples of the mantle. The deepest mine on Earth (Mponeng, South Africa) reaches about 4 km. The deepest borehole ever drilled (Kola Superdeep, USSR, terminated in 1995) reached 12.3 km. The mantle begins at an average of 35 km below continents and 7 km below oceans. We know what it's made of from:

- Seismic waves (how fast different waves travel through it)
- Mantle xenoliths (chunks of mantle rock brought up by volcanoes)
- Meteorites (chondrites likely resemble primordial mantle composition)
- Laboratory experiments at mantle-equivalent pressures

This indirect geology is how we know the Earth has an iron-nickel core (magnetic field + density calculations + meteorite analogies + seismic shadow zones). Nobody has ever seen it.

## Weathering and the Shaping of Landscapes

**Weathering** is what breaks rock down in place. Freeze-thaw cycles split boulders. Plant roots pry open joints. Rainwater, slightly acidic from dissolved CO₂, dissolves limestone (making caves and karst topography). Oxygen rusts iron-bearing minerals (making the red soils of tropics and the orange of sandstone cliffs).

**Erosion** is what carries the weathered material away. Water (rivers, glaciers, waves) is the biggest agent on Earth. Wind is significant in deserts. Gravity (landslides, rockfalls, slow soil creep) matters everywhere there's slope.

The Grand Canyon is a mile deep because the Colorado River has been cutting downward for 5–6 million years at roughly 0.2–0.4 mm/year. That's 200–400 millimeters per thousand years. Patience, applied to a continent, is how landscapes are made.

Mountains are built by tectonics and taken apart by erosion. Most mountain ranges are in dynamic equilibrium — tectonic uplift and erosion roughly balance over millions of years. When tectonic forces stop, erosion wins and the mountains eventually flatten. The Appalachians were once as tall as the modern Alps. They've been eroding for 200 million years and now the highest peak is under 2 km.

## Why This Level Matters

Geology at this level is an inventory: what rocks exist, what minerals make them up, how they formed, how they weather, and how they become the soil and resources civilization depends on.

Every thing you eat came out of soil. Every thing you build came out of ore. Every place you live rests on rock or sediment with specific properties that determine whether the foundation is stable, the groundwater is drinkable, and the ground will stay where it is during the next earthquake.

Geology at the surface is where chemistry, biology, and civil engineering meet. A geologist reading a roadcut can tell you whether this slope will fail in the next heavy rain, whether the aquifer below is vulnerable to contamination, and whether a mile away there might be economically useful deposits. The reading is not magic. It is what this level of knowledge enables.

## The Transition to Level 2

Once you can name rocks and minerals and read soils, the next question is: what moves them around at the scale of continents and oceans? Why does the entire Pacific Rim light up with earthquakes and volcanoes? Why is the Atlantic widening and the Pacific shrinking?

That is Level 2 — plate tectonics — the unifying theory that turned geology from a catalog into a dynamic science.
