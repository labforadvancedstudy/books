# Level 0: The Ground Under Your Feet
*Where everyone meets geology: by standing on it*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook — Phase 2B stub -->

> "The present is the key to the past." — James Hutton, 1785

## The Rock You've Held

Pick up a rock. Any rock. A pebble from a beach, a chunk of driveway gravel, a piece of the decorative landscaping in front of an office building. You are holding, in your hand, something older than most of what humans have ever built. Older than your country. Older, most likely, than your entire species.

The granite countertop in someone's kitchen is often two billion years old. Half the age of the planet. It was formed when the only life on Earth was single-celled and the sky had no oxygen. And now it holds a coffee cup.

## The Slow Explosion You're Standing On

Everything feels still. The floor doesn't move. The ground in your backyard has been there your whole life, and will be there long after. This is the first lie geology tells.

In reality you are standing on a thin rigid plate, maybe 100 km thick, floating on something that flows. The continent you live on is drifting at roughly the speed your fingernails grow — one to ten centimeters a year. Slow, yes. But it never stops. Never has, never will, not as long as the interior of the planet stays hot.

North America is moving west and slightly south. Africa is slamming into Europe, pushing up the Alps. India hit Asia fifty million years ago and is still pushing, which is why the Himalayas keep rising faster than erosion can wear them down.

The ground isn't still. You just don't live long enough to notice.

## The Mountain That Used to Be a Seafloor

Most of the limestone you see in buildings, sidewalks, and decorative facades is made of the compressed bodies of ocean creatures. Tiny shelled plankton, corals, sea urchins. They lived, died, fell to the bottom, got buried, got compressed, got lifted out of the sea, and eventually got quarried into the wall of a bank.

The White Cliffs of Dover, the pyramids of Giza, and the Empire State Building's limestone cladding are all made of dead sea animals older than flowers.

This isn't rare. It is the rule. The rocks near the top of Mount Everest are marine fossils. The entire summit was once underwater. The Earth moves rocks around with complete disregard for how things "should" be.

## The Earthquake You Haven't Felt Yet

Somewhere right now, a rock is breaking. Deep underground, two pieces of crust that have been locked together for decades, centuries, millennia, finally slip past each other. The release sends seismic waves through the rock of the planet. If it's close and strong, buildings crack and people die. If it's far or weak, nobody notices except the seismometers.

There are a million small earthquakes a year on Earth. Most are too small for you to feel. A few dozen a year are serious. One or two a year are catastrophic.

The planet is not a solid ball. It is a stack of plates constantly under stress, slowly building up energy, periodically releasing it. Your safety on any given day depends on where you live, how your building was built, and how recently the plates near you last slipped.

## The Soil You Walk Past

Soil is not dirt. Dirt is what gets on the kitchen floor. Soil is a living layered system of mineral particles, decayed organic matter, bacteria, fungi, worms, water, and air — built up over centuries by the slow conversion of bedrock into something plants can grow in.

An inch of topsoil takes somewhere between 100 and 1,000 years to form. A forest clear-cut and plowed can lose that inch in a single heavy rain. This is why farming is hard at civilization scale and why the next few centuries of agriculture depend on soil we are still figuring out how not to waste.

## The Deep Clock

The rock in your hand has a clock inside it. Tiny amounts of radioactive uranium, potassium, rubidium, have been decaying since the rock formed. By measuring how much has decayed versus how much is left, geologists can read the age of the rock directly — not infer it, not guess, *measure* it.

This is how we know the Earth is 4.54 billion years old. Not "very old." Not "older than we can imagine." 4.54 billion years, with an error bar of about 1%. The evidence is in every rock, readable with the right instrument.

Humans have been around for the last 0.004% of that time. Civilizations for 0.0002%. Whatever you worry about today is happening in the final margin of a very, very long story written in stone.

## The First Lesson

The ground is not ground. It is a process caught in the middle of doing something. The mountains are growing, the continents are drifting, the rocks are cycling between melt and crystal and sediment and melt again. What looks like permanence is actually very slow motion.

Everything you own, every building you've entered, every road you've driven on, sits on a geological process that was moving before humans existed and will be moving after humans are gone. Geology is the scale on which civilization is a dust-layer.
