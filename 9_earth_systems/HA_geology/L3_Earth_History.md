## Reading the Rock Record

L2 covered plate tectonics — the present-day machinery of the solid Earth. L3 unwinds the tape. Rocks record a history that stretches 4.5 billion years, and reading that record requires a distinct set of skills: stratigraphy, geochronology, isotope geochemistry, paleontology, paleomagnetism. Each layer in a sequence is a page; each mineral grain in a sandstone was somewhere else before; each zircon carries a uranium-lead clock accurate to fractions of a percent over billions of years.

Earth history is the only test we have of how a rocky planet with oceans, atmospheres, and life behaves over deep time. Mars, Venus, Mercury, the Moon give us pieces; Earth gives us the whole book, though much of it is torn and out of order. The questions this level asks: when did plates start moving? when did life begin? what drove the major transitions — the Great Oxidation, the Cambrian explosion, the five mass extinctions, the Cenozoic cooling? Understanding these matters both for geology as a science and for framing the present — climate, biodiversity, resource availability are all contextualized by what the planet has done before.

## Geologic Time Scale

The **geologic time scale** divides Earth history into eons, eras, periods, epochs, ages — a nested hierarchy calibrated by stratigraphy and radiometric dating.

Main divisions:

- **Hadean (4.56-4.0 Ga)**: Earth's formation, Moon-forming impact, magma ocean, late heavy bombardment. Few rocks survive; the record is from detrital zircons in younger rocks.
- **Archean (4.0-2.5 Ga)**: earliest crust, first life (by ~3.5 Ga, possibly earlier), no free oxygen. Greenstone belts, banded iron formations begin.
- **Proterozoic (2.5 Ga-541 Ma)**: Great Oxidation Event (~2.4 Ga), formation of stable cratons, supercontinents, first eukaryotes, first multicellular life, Snowball Earth glaciations.
- **Phanerozoic (541 Ma-present)**: "visible life." Cambrian explosion to present. Divided into Paleozoic (541-252 Ma), Mesozoic (252-66 Ma), Cenozoic (66 Ma-present).

The Phanerozoic is what most paleontology and fossil-based stratigraphy covers. The preceding ~4 billion years — 88% of Earth history — is the Precambrian, with a very different biological and geochemical regime.

## Radiometric Dating

Radioactive isotopes decay at known rates, providing absolute chronometers:

- **U-Pb**: two separate clocks (²³⁸U → ²⁰⁶Pb and ²³⁵U → ²⁰⁷Pb) in zircon crystals. The concordance between them validates closed-system behavior. Precision at ±0.1% is routine; ±0.01% achievable with careful work. The workhorse for Precambrian and older Phanerozoic dating.
- **K-Ar / Ar-Ar**: decay of ⁴⁰K to ⁴⁰Ar in K-bearing minerals. Good for volcanic rocks, ash beds. Dates most of the Cenozoic and Mesozoic.
- **Rb-Sr**: whole-rock isochrons for metamorphic and igneous rocks.
- **Sm-Nd, Lu-Hf, Re-Os**: specialized systems, each with advantages for specific rock types.
- **¹⁴C**: only useful for last ~50,000 years, but critical for the late Quaternary and archaeology.
- **Cosmogenic nuclides** (¹⁰Be, ²⁶Al, ³⁶Cl): produced by cosmic ray spallation at surface; date erosion rates, glacial retreat, meteorite exposure.

Modern geochronology is highly technical and statistics-intensive. A robust date requires multiple crystals or grains, concordance between systems, and geological context. Single-grain ion-microprobe dating of zircons has revolutionized Precambrian and Mesozoic chronology.

## Stratigraphy and Correlation

**Stratigraphy** orders rocks by relative age, building a framework before absolute dates are hung on it.

Principles (Steno, Hutton, Lyell — 17th to 19th centuries):
- **Superposition**: in an undeformed sequence, older is below.
- **Original horizontality**: sediments deposit horizontally; tilted beds were tilted after.
- **Lateral continuity**: beds extend laterally until they pinch out or are cut.
- **Cross-cutting**: features that cut across other features are younger.
- **Inclusions**: rock fragments inside another rock are older than the host.

**Biostratigraphy** uses fossils to correlate sequences across the world — particular species occupy specific time windows; finding the same index fossil in two sections means they overlap in age. Graptolites, ammonites, conodonts, foraminifera each define specific intervals.

**Chemostratigraphy** uses isotope ratios (carbon, oxygen, strontium) and other geochemical signatures as time markers. The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM, ~56 Ma) is marked by a negative carbon isotope excursion identifiable globally.

**Magnetostratigraphy** uses the Earth's magnetic reversal history as a correlation tool. The last reversal was 780,000 years ago; the sequence of reversals is well-calibrated back ~170 million years.

## The Early Earth and Origin of Life

The first ~500 million years of Earth history left very few rocks. What we know comes mostly from detrital zircons — the oldest (Jack Hills, Western Australia) are 4.37 Ga old, indicating crust and liquid water almost immediately after Earth formation.

The **Moon-forming impact** (Theia, ~4.5 Ga) is inferred from lunar chemistry and isotopic composition. It left Earth with an unusually large iron core, a unique angular momentum partitioning with the Moon, and possibly set initial conditions for a mantle with distinct reservoirs still detectable isotopically.

**Life**: the oldest putative biosignatures — graphite in Greenland gneiss ~3.7 Ga, stromatolites (microbial mats) in Australia ~3.5 Ga, specific carbon isotope signatures — are contested for every claim above ~3.5 Ga and firmer by 3.0-2.7 Ga. The Archean biosphere was microbial — bacteria and archaea.

Origin-of-life hypotheses include RNA world (self-replicating RNA precedes DNA and proteins), hydrothermal vent hypothesis (chemiosmotic gradients at alkaline vents), chemoautotrophic origins, panspermia. No consensus exists, but the field has become experimentally productive — prebiotic chemistry, in-vitro evolution, metabolic network reconstruction.

## The Great Oxidation Event

For ~2 billion years, Earth's atmosphere had essentially no free oxygen. Then, around 2.4 Ga, oxygen levels rose — the **Great Oxidation Event (GOE)**. Evidence:

- Disappearance of mass-independent sulfur isotope fractionation (a UV-driven signature requiring O₂-free air).
- Appearance of red beds (oxidized iron in sediments).
- End of banded iron formations (anoxic deep oceans deposited iron; once oxygenated, iron precipitated locally).
- Appearance of sulfate minerals requiring oxidized sulfur.

The cause was biological — oxygenic photosynthesis had arisen earlier in cyanobacteria, but the atmospheric signal lagged until sinks (reduced iron, volcanic gases) were overwhelmed. A second pulse of oxygenation — the **Neoproterozoic Oxygenation Event** ~800-550 Ma — may have set up the Cambrian explosion.

Oxygenation restructured biochemistry, enabling efficient aerobic respiration (~15× more ATP per glucose than fermentation) and eventually large multicellular life. It also changed geochemistry permanently — weathering, sediment chemistry, elemental availability.

## Snowball Earth

The Neoproterozoic (~720-635 Ma) saw the most severe glaciations in Earth history — evidence of ice at sea level at equatorial paleolatitudes. The **Snowball Earth hypothesis** posits near-global ice cover, escaped by volcanic CO₂ buildup warming the planet once the ice-albedo feedback was broken.

Evidence: tillites (glacial sediments) on every continent, capped by carbonate layers (indicating sudden warming), distinctive iron and manganese deposits, banded iron formations returning during the ice phase.

The glaciations cluster just before the Ediacaran biota (~575 Ma) and the Cambrian explosion. Whether causal — stress on life drove evolutionary innovation — or coincidental is debated. The broader pattern of oxygenation and glaciation in the Neoproterozoic likely shaped what followed.

## Phanerozoic Life

The **Cambrian explosion** (~541-520 Ma) saw the rapid appearance of most modern animal phyla — arthropods, chordates, mollusks, echinoderms — in the fossil record. Causes debated: oxygenation, ecological novelty (predators drove defenses and diversification), genetic potential (Hox genes enabling body plan variation), or faster evolution under extreme conditions. The Burgess Shale and Chengjiang biotas preserve exquisite soft-body details.

Paleozoic (541-252 Ma): first vertebrates (Cambrian), first terrestrial plants (Ordovician-Silurian), first tetrapods (Devonian), Carboniferous coal swamps, major reef systems.

Mesozoic (252-66 Ma): "age of dinosaurs" — but also first mammals (late Triassic), first flowering plants (mid-Cretaceous), supercontinent Pangea breakup, warm climate with minimal ice.

Cenozoic (66 Ma-present): mammalian radiation, grassland expansion, cooling into the Quaternary Ice Ages, hominin evolution.

## Mass Extinctions

Five major extinction events punctuate the Phanerozoic:

- **End-Ordovician (~444 Ma)**: ~85% species loss. Driven by glaciation.
- **Late Devonian (~372 Ma)**: extended multi-pulse event. Causes debated; anoxic oceans likely.
- **End-Permian (~252 Ma)**: "great dying," ~96% marine species lost. Siberian Traps volcanism + ocean anoxia + CO₂/CH₄ release. Longest recovery time (~5-10 million years).
- **End-Triassic (~201 Ma)**: ~50% species loss. Central Atlantic Magmatic Province volcanism.
- **End-Cretaceous / K-Pg (~66 Ma)**: ~76% species loss including non-avian dinosaurs. Chicxulub asteroid impact at Yucatan + Deccan Traps volcanism. Alvarez et al. (1980) iridium anomaly was the first firm impact evidence.

Extinction dynamics teach that biospheres are not arbitrarily resilient — they have break points. The current biodiversity crisis from human activity is comparable in rate to these events, though cumulative magnitude is still far below.

## Quaternary Glaciations

The last ~2.6 million years have seen repeated glaciations — the **Pleistocene Ice Age**. Major ice sheets covered North America, northern Europe, Patagonia during glacial periods (roughly every 100,000 years). Interglacials — our present is one, the Holocene — are shorter warm intervals.

Drivers:
- **Milankovitch cycles**: orbital variations in Earth's eccentricity (100 kyr), obliquity (41 kyr), precession (23 kyr) modulate insolation, pacing glaciations.
- **CO₂ feedbacks**: glacial CO₂ was ~180 ppm, interglacial ~280 ppm. Amplifies orbital forcing.
- **Ice-albedo, dust, ocean circulation feedbacks**.

Human ancestors evolved through these cycles. Agriculture arose ~12,000 years ago, roughly when the Holocene began and climate stabilized. The current interglacial is unusually long and stable — likely a factor in civilization's development.

## Paleontology and Evolution

The fossil record is the primary evidence for evolutionary history. Key patterns:

- **Punctuated equilibrium** (Eldredge, Gould 1972): lineages show long stasis punctuated by relatively rapid change. Contested vs. gradualism; both patterns exist in different groups.
- **Adaptive radiations**: rapid diversification after mass extinctions or into new environments (mammalian radiation post-K-Pg, Cambrian explosion).
- **Convergent evolution**: similar body plans evolve repeatedly in similar niches (marsupial vs. placental "wolves," eye evolution >40 times independently).
- **Transitional fossils**: *Tiktaalik* (tetrapod), *Archaeopteryx* (bird), whale ancestors, hominin sequence. Each predicted by evolutionary framework, each eventually found.

Paleontology has become increasingly quantitative — morphometric analysis, phylogenetic reconstruction integrating fossils and molecular data, taphonomic correction for preservation bias.

## Why This Level Matters

Understanding Earth history changes the frame of nearly every geological and environmental question. Present climate is calibrated against paleoclimate; current biodiversity against past extinctions; current tectonic configuration against supercontinent cycles; current atmospheric chemistry against oxygenation history. The past is not just background — it's the natural experiment against which we test hypotheses about planetary systems.

Earth history also provides the longest and most detailed record of how a planet responds to forcing — including CO₂ forcing, which is the single most important question in current climate science. The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum, rapid carbon injection events in the geological record, and the glacial-interglacial cycles all constrain climate sensitivity and tipping-point behavior in ways no model alone can.
