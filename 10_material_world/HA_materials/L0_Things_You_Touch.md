# Level 0: The Things You Touch
*Where everyone meets materials science: by picking things up*

## The Inventory of a Hand

Reach out. Touch five things without moving from your seat.

A table: probably wood, or an engineered wood-product (MDF, plywood), or laminate, or metal, or some combination. A phone: aluminum alloy shell, Gorilla Glass front, plastic gaskets, a lithium polymer battery, rare-earth magnets in the speaker, silicon chips under everything. A pen: polymer body, steel ball at the tip, pigmented ink. A cup: ceramic (porcelain), glass (silica + soda + lime), or plastic (polypropylene mostly). A shirt: cotton (cellulose polymer grown by a plant), polyester (polymer from oil), wool (protein from a sheep), or blend.

Each of those substances is the output of thousands of years of accumulated know-how about how to take atoms and put them in the arrangement that gives the property you want.

## The Weirdness of Metal

Metals bend. Rock doesn't. Glass doesn't. Why?

Because in a metal, the outer electrons are not attached to specific atoms. They form a shared "sea" flowing among positive ion cores. When you push a metal, the ion cores can slide past each other a bit, the electron sea follows, and no bond breaks. The metal deforms without shattering.

In a ceramic or glass, the atoms are locked in covalent or ionic bonds. There is no sliding. When stress exceeds the bond strength, the bonds snap and a crack propagates across the whole object. This is why porcelain breaks in one moment and lead bends.

Same atoms, same forces, different arrangements, completely different behavior. Materials science is the study of how arrangement produces property.

## The Steel You Undervalue

Steel is iron with a small percentage of carbon. This sounds trivial. It is not.

Pure iron is soft. Pure carbon (graphite) is smudgy and weak, or (diamond) hard but brittle. Iron plus 0.2-2% carbon — carefully distributed — is one of the strongest, toughest, cheapest structural materials humans have ever produced. The steel in a bridge, a skyscraper, a car, a surgical scalpel, a shipping container, is the output of blast furnaces that run at 1,500°C, consume coal by the trainload, and produce about 1.9 billion tons a year globally.

Before steel, civilization built with wood and stone and had strict limits on how high or how long a structure could be. Steel rewrote the geometry of what humans could build.

## The Plastic You Take for Granted

Plastics are polymers: long chains of repeating molecular units. In nature, polymers include cellulose (wood and cotton), proteins (including you), and DNA. Synthetic plastics — polyethylene, PVC, polyester, polycarbonate — are a 20th-century invention. There was barely any plastic in the world in 1900. Now there is a planet's worth.

Plastic is weightless compared to its utility: it's cheap, moldable, chemically inert, electrically insulating, food-safe when formulated right, and can be engineered for almost any application. It is also effectively immortal in nature, which is the reason the ocean now has plastic in every water column at every depth.

The same property that makes plastic useful (slow chemical decay) makes it a pollution disaster. Materials that don't break down don't go away.

## The Glass You See Through

Glass is a liquid that has cooled so fast it never crystallized. It has no repeating structure; it is atoms frozen in disorder. Its transparency comes from the fact that visible light does not have enough energy to excite any electron transition in the silica network, so photons pass through unabsorbed.

This is a rare combination: rigid enough to build with, transparent enough to see through, chemically durable for centuries. Ancient Romans had glass. Medieval cathedrals had stained glass. Modern civilization runs on glass fiber optics, glass microscope lenses, glass test tubes, glass phone screens.

Every window is a tiny miracle of supercooled silica.

## The Chip You Can't See Into

The silicon chip in your phone was made by taking high-purity silicon (99.99999999% pure — nine nines), growing it into a single perfect crystal, slicing it into wafers, and then using light, photoresist, and etching chemistry to pattern billions of transistors onto it with features 5-10 nanometers wide.

A single chip can contain over 100 billion transistors. No human has ever seen a transistor on a modern chip with their naked eye. The manufacturing tolerances are so tight that a speck of dust is catastrophic; fabrication happens in cleanrooms with fewer particles per cubic meter than a hospital operating room.

Materials science made this possible. Before semiconductors, the idea of fitting a billion switches on your fingernail was unthinkable.

## The First Lesson

Every object is a material. Every material is atoms in a specific arrangement. Every arrangement produces specific properties. Humans learned, slowly, to design the arrangement to get the property. The history of civilization is the history of this learning.

The difference between stone, bronze, iron, steel, plastic, silicon is not what the atoms are. It is what arrangement humans figured out how to make.
