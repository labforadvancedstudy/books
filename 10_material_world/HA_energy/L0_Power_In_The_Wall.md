# Level 0: The Power in the Wall
*Where everyone meets energy: at a light switch*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook — Phase 2B stub -->

## The Flick

You flip a switch. A light comes on. You charge your phone. You boil water. You run a refrigerator 24 hours a day for the entire span of your life, without thinking about it once.

Somewhere, at the exact instant you drew that power, a generator adjusted to meet your demand. That generator is part of a grid that balances supply and demand continuously at 50 or 60 cycles per second. If the balance breaks for more than a few seconds, the grid trips and a region goes dark.

The grid is one of the most complex, most reliable machines humans have ever built. You only notice it when it fails.

## The Furnace You Don't See

About 60-70% of global electricity still comes from burning something — coal, natural gas, or oil — to boil water, turn a turbine, spin a generator. The furnace is somewhere else. The power plant might be 500 km away. You get the electricity without ever seeing the combustion.

This is very convenient. It is also how a world of 8 billion people ended up putting 40 billion tons of CO₂ into the atmosphere every year without most individuals realizing they were doing it. The consumption is invisible because the combustion happens elsewhere.

## The Other 30-40%

Electricity that isn't from combustion comes from:

- **Hydro** (~15% of global electricity): water falling through turbines. Reliable, dispatchable, place-limited.
- **Nuclear** (~10%): uranium fission boiling water. Zero-carbon, but capital-intensive and politically fraught.
- **Wind** (~7% and rising): turbines driven by air moving from high to low pressure. Intermittent.
- **Solar PV** (~5% and rising fast): photons knocking electrons out of silicon. Intermittent but cheap.
- **Geothermal / biomass / other** (~3%).

These percentages have been shifting annually for a decade. Solar and wind are growing fastest because their per-kWh costs have fallen by roughly 80-90% since 2010. Coal is declining in most rich countries. Natural gas is flexible and is partly replacing coal. Nuclear is flat globally but growing in a few countries.

## The Power You Eat

Food is energy. Specifically, food is chemical energy stored by plants (photosynthesis) or animals (by eating plants). A human adult burns about 2,000 kcal/day, which is about 8.4 MJ or 2.3 kWh. Your body is a ~100-watt heater.

Modern industrial agriculture, however, puts about 7-10 calories of fossil fuel into the food system for every calorie of food that reaches your plate. Fertilizer synthesis, tractor fuel, pumped irrigation, processing, refrigeration, and transport all run on oil, gas, and electricity. The modern diet is therefore very energy-intensive, even before you count your car trip to the grocery store.

## The Gas Tank

About a third of end-use energy in rich countries is liquid transport fuel: gasoline and diesel. A gallon of gasoline contains about 33.7 kWh of chemical energy. A typical internal combustion engine converts maybe 20-25% of that to useful motion. The rest is wasted as heat.

An electric motor is 85-95% efficient. This is why switching to electric vehicles reduces energy use by about a factor of three per mile, even if the electricity came from a fossil fuel plant. EV adoption is therefore simultaneously a climate story and an energy efficiency story.

## The Battery You Hold

The lithium battery in your phone stores about 15-20 Wh (watt-hours). The battery in an EV stores about 50,000-100,000 Wh. Grid-scale batteries can store 100 MWh (100 million Wh) or more.

Energy storage at grid scale is the hinge of the renewable energy transition. The challenge is not generating clean electricity — solar panels are cheap. The challenge is having enough electricity at the right times, when the sun isn't shining and the wind isn't blowing. Storage makes this possible. Lithium-ion batteries have fallen 90% in cost since 2010. Other technologies (flow batteries, pumped hydro, compressed air, green hydrogen) are competing for specific niches.

## The Cost You Don't Pay

The price on your electricity bill is typically $0.10-0.30/kWh in developed countries. That price covers fuel, capital, operations, and taxes. It does not cover the climate damage from emissions, or the health damage from air pollution, or the mining damage from extracting coal and uranium.

Economists estimate the "social cost of carbon" — the full cost of the climate damage caused by a ton of emitted CO₂ — somewhere between $50 and $400 per ton, depending on assumptions. That translates to an extra $0.05-$0.40/kWh for coal-fired electricity. Most of that is not in your bill. Somebody else, somewhere else, somewhen else is paying it.

## The First Lesson

Energy is the one resource that lets you do anything else. Food, transport, heat, manufacturing, information — all of it runs on energy. A civilization's energy flow is the single best indicator of how it lives. Americans consume about 80 GJ/person/year. Europeans about 40. Global average about 20. Global subsistence about 5.

More energy has meant more comfort, more options, more freedom, more pollution, more climate change, all at once. The 21st century's defining question is whether we can uncouple the good parts of that curve from the bad parts. The technical answer is probably yes. The political and economic answer is not settled.
