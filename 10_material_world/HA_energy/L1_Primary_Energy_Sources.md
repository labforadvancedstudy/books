# Level 1: Primary Energy Sources
*Where civilization's energy actually comes from*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Accounting

Global primary energy consumption in the mid-2020s is roughly 180,000 TWh/year — around 600 exajoules. The sources, in rough order:

- **Oil**: ~31% — transportation fuel, petrochemicals, heating.
- **Coal**: ~27% — electricity generation, steel smelting, cement.
- **Natural gas**: ~23% — electricity, heating, industrial processes.
- **Hydroelectric**: ~6% — electricity generation.
- **Nuclear**: ~4% — electricity generation.
- **Wind + solar**: ~5% and rising fast — electricity generation.
- **Biofuels and traditional biomass**: ~7% — cooking fuel, transport fuel, heating. Most of this is wood and dung burned in developing countries.
- **Geothermal, tidal, other**: <1%.

Fossil fuels (oil + coal + gas) still account for over 80% of global primary energy. The energy transition is real and accelerating in electricity generation, but slower in transportation, industry, and heating.

Important framing: **primary energy** is the gross energy in the raw source. Much is lost as heat in conversion. The **useful energy** actually delivered to end uses is roughly one third to two thirds of primary energy, depending on the pathway. Burning coal to make electricity is about 35% efficient; modern combined-cycle natural gas can hit 60%; electric motors running on that electricity are ~90%. Electrifying end uses is a major efficiency multiplier.

## Fossil Fuels

Fossil fuels are concentrated ancient solar energy. Hundreds of millions of years ago, plants (for coal) and plankton (for oil and gas) absorbed sunlight, died, accumulated in sediment, got buried, cooked underground. The chemical energy they captured became the basis of industrial civilization.

**Coal**: carbon-rich solid. Mined from shallow seams and deep mines. Types range from lignite (low quality, high moisture) to anthracite (hard, high-carbon). Energy density about 15–35 MJ/kg. Dominant fuel of the 19th century; still the largest single source of electricity globally as of 2024.

**Oil (petroleum)**: hydrocarbon liquid. Pumped from wells, refined into gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, kerosene, heavy fuel oil, and petrochemical feedstocks. Energy density ~42 MJ/kg, and conveniently liquid at room temperature. This is why oil is the transportation fuel of choice: it has the highest practical energy density per unit volume of anything we routinely move around.

**Natural gas (methane)**: gaseous hydrocarbon. Pipeline-delivered or liquefied (LNG) for ocean shipping. Cleaner burning than coal or oil — fewer particulates, no sulfur (usually), less CO₂ per unit energy than coal (~50% of coal's emissions per kWh). Often called a "bridge fuel" for the transition away from coal, with the caveat that methane leakage from the supply chain undercuts the climate benefit.

Fossil fuels' advantages are enormous energy density, existing infrastructure (wells, pipelines, refineries, power plants), and mature supply chains. Their disadvantage is CO₂ emissions. Burning all the carbon we've been burning at the rate we've been burning it has raised atmospheric CO₂ by about 50% over the industrial era and is changing the climate.

## Nuclear

A nuclear reactor is a heat source: uranium-235 or plutonium-239 atoms split (**fission**), releasing about 200 MeV per fission — roughly a million times the energy per atom of a chemical reaction. The heat boils water, spins a turbine, generates electricity.

One kilogram of enriched uranium yields roughly 500 GJ of thermal energy, vs. about 30 MJ/kg for coal. Nuclear fuel is small by volume, and nuclear plants emit essentially no CO₂ during operation. Over the full life cycle (mining, construction, waste handling), nuclear has CO₂ emissions comparable to wind and solar — far lower than fossil fuels.

Nuclear produces about 10% of global electricity. It could produce much more. It hasn't because:

- **Construction cost and time**: large nuclear plants routinely run over budget and over schedule. Vogtle 3 and 4 in Georgia cost about $35 billion for two reactors and took 15+ years from groundbreaking to operation.
- **Accidents**: Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986), Fukushima (2011). Each dramatically changed public perception and regulatory posture in the affected region. Fukushima directly caused about 1 radiation fatality but triggered evacuations, stress-related deaths, and Japan shutting down its fleet for years.
- **Waste**: spent fuel is highly radioactive for thousands of years. Geological storage is technically feasible (Finland's Onkalo facility is nearly operational). Political willingness to site and fund it has been limited almost everywhere.
- **Proliferation concerns**: enrichment and reprocessing technologies overlap with nuclear weapons.

Smaller reactor designs (SMRs, small modular reactors) and alternative fuel cycles (thorium, molten salt) aim to address cost, safety, and waste. Whether any of them achieve commercial success at scale in the 2020s–30s is an open question.

## Hydroelectric

Water falling through a turbine produces electricity. Hydro has been used for over a century and is the largest source of renewable electricity globally (by generation volume).

The advantages: clean, reliable, storage-capable (pumped hydro is currently the dominant grid-scale energy storage globally), and cheap once built. Some hydro facilities have operated continuously for 80+ years.

The limits: you need a river with usable head drop, and you need a viable dam site. Most of the world's best hydro sites are already developed in the rich world. The big remaining potential is in Africa and parts of Asia, often in politically complex transboundary basins. Large dams also have large ecological and social costs — displaced populations, sediment disruption, fisheries destruction, methane emissions from flooded vegetation in some tropical reservoirs.

**Pumped hydro storage** uses cheap (nighttime or surplus-renewable) electricity to pump water uphill, then releases it through turbines when electricity is needed. Round-trip efficiency is 70–80%. The technology is mature. The limit, again, is site availability.

## Wind

Wind turbines convert kinetic energy of moving air into electricity. A modern utility-scale wind turbine has a rotor diameter of 120–200 meters and a capacity of 4–15 MW. Offshore turbines are larger and more productive than onshore.

Wind's contribution has grown from negligible in 2000 to about 7% of global electricity by 2024. The cost has fallen by ~70% since 2010 — one of the largest cost reductions in energy history. Wind is now the cheapest new electricity source in many regions (onshore, in good wind resource areas).

Limits: wind is variable. A turbine produces maximum output perhaps 30–50% of the time, and near-zero at other times. Grid integration requires either storage, transmission to balance across regions, or flexible backup generation. These are solvable problems but add system cost not captured in the per-kWh cost of the turbines themselves.

## Solar

Photovoltaics convert sunlight directly into electricity via the photoelectric effect in semiconductors (mostly silicon). A modern utility-scale solar panel converts 20–23% of incident sunlight into electricity.

Solar cost has fallen even more dramatically than wind — about 90% since 2010. Solar is now the cheapest new electricity in most regions with good solar resource. Global solar capacity in the mid-2020s is over 1,500 GW and growing at 30–40% per year.

Like wind, solar is variable. Unlike wind, it's diurnal (every day, predictable) and seasonal (more production in summer in mid-latitudes, less in winter). Pairing solar with batteries is increasingly common for daily load shifting, but multi-day or seasonal storage remains an unsolved problem at affordable cost.

## The Storage Problem

The primary challenge of high-renewable grids is storage. When wind and solar produce, you use it. When they don't, you need something else — stored energy, flexible demand, or backup generation.

Storage options:
- **Lithium-ion batteries**: expensive per kWh but dropping rapidly; good for hours-scale shifting.
- **Pumped hydro**: cheap per kWh but site-constrained.
- **Other technologies**: compressed air, flow batteries, gravity storage, iron-air batteries, thermal storage, hydrogen electrolysis + fuel cells. Various maturity levels.

Current grids solve the storage problem by running fossil generation (especially gas) as a flexible backup. Decarbonizing will require replacing that flexibility with some combination of storage, transmission, demand flexibility, and dispatchable clean generation (nuclear, geothermal, hydro, or fossil with carbon capture).

## Why This Level Matters

Energy is the master resource. Everything else in civilization — food, water, materials, manufacturing, transportation, information — depends on energy. The 20th century was largely the story of abundant fossil fuel energy enabling mass prosperity. The 21st century will be the story of decarbonizing that energy system.

Knowing the actual mix, scale, and limits of each source is the prerequisite for any useful energy debate. Advocacy arguments based on fantasy capacity (everything solar, everything nuclear, everything gas) collapse as soon as you do the numbers. The realistic path involves all of the above, in different mixes for different regions.
