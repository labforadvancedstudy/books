# Level 2: Conversion and Transmission
*From primary sources to useful work — generators, grids, and the infrastructure of modern energy*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## From Source to Socket

Primary energy sources — oil, gas, coal, uranium, sunlight, wind, flowing water — are not useful on their own. They must be converted into forms that drive motors, heat buildings, charge devices, run factories. The conversion and delivery system is as important as the source.

In 2023, global primary energy consumption was ~620 EJ (exajoules, 10¹⁸ J). Of that:
- ~45% became useful end-use energy.
- ~30% was lost in conversion (especially in thermal power plants throwing away ~60% of fuel energy as heat).
- ~15% was used directly for heat.
- The rest was lost in transmission, distribution, and final conversion.

The thermal-efficiency bottleneck is the largest single loss. Electrification of end uses — replacing combustion with electric motors, heat pumps, and resistive heating — substitutes high-efficiency electric conversions for low-efficiency combustion. Electrification plus a decarbonized grid is the primary strategy for reducing overall emissions.

## Electricity Generation — Thermal

Most electricity is still generated by heating water into steam that drives turbine-generators. The heat source varies:

- **Coal**: burn coal in a boiler. Thermal efficiency 30–45% for modern supercritical plants. Still ~35% of global electricity. Most polluting.
- **Natural gas**: combined-cycle plants (gas turbine + heat recovery steam turbine) achieve 55–63% thermal efficiency. Simpler peaker plants (gas turbine alone) ~35–40%. Cleanest fossil generation but still CO₂.
- **Nuclear**: fission heat drives steam cycles. 33–37% thermal efficiency (lower than fossil because of temperature limits). Very low operating CO₂.
- **Biomass and waste-to-energy**: similar steam cycles with wood, agricultural residues, or municipal waste. Typically lower efficiency and smaller scale.
- **Concentrated solar thermal (CSP)**: mirrors focus sunlight to heat a working fluid, which drives a steam cycle. Adds thermal storage capability. Small but growing.

A modern combined-cycle gas plant, a coal plant, and a nuclear plant all share fundamentally similar generator technology. What differs is the heat source and the thermodynamic cycle's temperature limits.

## Electricity Generation — Non-Thermal

Several generation types bypass the heat engine:

- **Hydroelectric**: flowing water drives turbine-generators. Very efficient (85–95% for water-to-electricity), very flexible for load following, long lifetime (dams built in the 1930s–50s still operate). Limited by available sites.
- **Wind**: kinetic energy of wind drives turbines. 2–5 MW per turbine common now; offshore turbines reaching 15 MW. Capacity factors 25–50% depending on location.
- **Solar photovoltaic (PV)**: sunlight directly excites electrons in semiconductor cells. Now the cheapest source of electricity in most sunny regions. Capacity factors 15–25% (sunny regions) down to 8–12% (cloudy northern latitudes).
- **Tidal and wave**: niche, resource-constrained.
- **Geothermal**: hot underground water drives generators. Limited to geothermally active regions, but highly reliable (capacity factor 80–95%) where it works.

Wind and solar are **variable** — output depends on weather. That creates system integration challenges (discussed below) but not generation challenges per se. The turbines and panels run fine; the grid has to adapt.

## The Generator

At the heart of most power plants is the **synchronous generator** — a machine that converts mechanical rotation into electricity. Key components:

- **Rotor**: spinning electromagnet driven by the turbine.
- **Stator**: stationary windings in which the rotating magnetic field induces AC current.
- **Excitation system**: supplies current to the rotor electromagnet.
- **Control systems**: regulate output voltage and power factor.

Synchronous generators physically force the grid to run at exactly 50 or 60 Hz — their rotation defines the frequency. Big rotating masses provide **inertia** that resists sudden frequency changes (a loss of supply slows the grid gradually rather than collapsing it). This inertia has been free from the large fleet of fossil and nuclear generators; it becomes a design problem as they retire.

Solar PV and most wind turbines connect through **inverters** — power electronics that convert DC (or variable-frequency AC from wind) into grid-synchronous AC. Inverter-based resources can be made to provide synthetic inertia and fast frequency response, but need to be designed for it explicitly. Grids with high inverter-based generation are learning to operate with less physical inertia.

## Transmission

Electricity generated at a power plant must be delivered to customers, often hundreds or thousands of kilometers away.

**Transmission** moves bulk power at high voltage over long distances. Voltage is stepped up (200–1000 kV) because transmission loss is proportional to current squared — higher voltage means lower current for the same power, and lower losses.

**High-voltage AC transmission**: dominant historically. Losses ~3% per 1000 km at 500 kV. Long distances require reactive power management.

**High-voltage DC (HVDC) transmission**: preferred for very long distances (>1000 km) and for submarine cables, and for interconnecting asynchronous grids. Losses ~3% per 1000 km but no reactive power issues. Requires expensive converter stations at each end; cost-effective only at scale. China has built the world's largest HVDC network; Europe uses HVDC for offshore wind and grid interconnections.

**Transmission constraints** limit what generation can be delivered to where. A cheap wind farm in Texas doesn't help Californians if no transmission connects them. Texas's 2021 winter grid failure (Uri) was partly a transmission/import capacity problem — Texas's grid was isolated from neighboring regions, limiting its ability to import during the crisis.

## Distribution

From transmission, electricity passes through **substations** that step down voltage to **distribution** levels (typically 4–35 kV for primary, 120–480 V for secondary customer service).

Distribution networks are the "last mile" — radial lines running down streets to customers. Most outages happen at the distribution level: trees falling on lines, equipment failures, animals shorting transformers, vehicles hitting poles.

Distribution is being reshaped by:

- **Distributed generation**: rooftop solar, small wind, battery storage behind the meter. Distribution networks designed for one-way flow now have to handle two-way flow.
- **EV charging**: high-power loads (fast chargers up to 350 kW, equivalent to a small industrial customer) showing up on residential and commercial circuits.
- **Smart meters and load control**: digital metering allows time-of-use pricing, demand response, and better outage management.

Old distribution infrastructure (much of it 40–80 years old in developed countries) is the weakest link in grid reliability. Upgrading it is expensive and slow — millions of components, many owners, little glamour.

## The Grid

The grid is a synchronous machine — every generator and every load connected together, operating at a common frequency. A disturbance anywhere propagates through the grid within milliseconds.

Grid operators (**ISOs/RTOs** in US, TSO in Europe, national utilities elsewhere) continuously balance supply and demand. They:

- **Forecast load** hourly to daily ahead.
- **Commit generators** (decide which to run over the next 24 hours).
- **Dispatch** generators in real time to match load.
- **Maintain reserves** for contingencies.
- **Manage frequency and voltage**.
- **Plan transmission** to meet expected growth.

Grids are engineered to survive single-contingency events (the sudden loss of any one generator, line, or transformer). Multiple simultaneous contingencies are more serious — the 2003 Northeast blackout cascaded from a few line failures and operator errors to affect 55 million people.

## Storage

Storage separates generation and consumption in time. Types in current use:

- **Pumped hydro**: pump water uphill when power is cheap, release through turbines when power is expensive. Dominant form of grid storage by capacity (~95% of global grid storage). Limited by geography and site availability.
- **Batteries**: lithium-ion batteries now deployed at grid scale. Costs have dropped ~85% since 2010. Good for 4–8 hour storage; rarely economic beyond that.
- **Compressed air energy storage (CAES)**: compress air into underground caverns, expand through turbines to generate power. Several operating plants globally.
- **Flywheels, capacitors**: very short-term (seconds to minutes) frequency regulation.
- **Thermal storage**: hot tanks in concentrated solar plants; ice storage for building cooling. Matures by application.

Storage demand is growing with variable renewables. Current deployment in the GW-hour range; projections see terawatt-hour-scale by 2040. New technologies (flow batteries, gravity storage, hydrogen-based storage, and thermal storage) are at various stages of development and deployment.

## Heat Pumps

For heating, conventional thinking was simple: burn a fuel. **Heat pumps** break that by moving heat from outside to inside using a refrigeration cycle, delivering 2–5 units of heat per unit of electricity input (coefficient of performance, COP, 2–5).

A well-designed heat pump in a moderate climate delivers 3–4× more heat per unit of input energy than a gas furnace delivers per unit of fuel energy. If the electricity comes from low-carbon sources, the emissions advantage is dramatic.

Cold-climate heat pumps (rated for operation down to −20°C or colder) have become common in Scandinavia and are spreading in North America. Ground-source (geothermal) heat pumps are more expensive but even more efficient.

Heat pumps are one of the single largest efficiency opportunities in buildings. Widespread deployment is already transforming heating in Norway, Sweden, Finland, and parts of France; it's beginning in the UK, Germany, Japan, and the US.

## Mobility Electrification

Internal-combustion engines in light-duty vehicles convert ~20–30% of fuel energy into motion (with considerable variation by drive cycle). Electric motors convert ~85–95% of battery energy into motion. The efficiency gap is huge.

The full well-to-wheels comparison is more nuanced — it depends on the source of electricity, the manufacturing emissions of the battery, and the driving profile. In a gas-dominated grid, EVs are cleaner than gasoline cars by ~30–50% over their life. In a coal-dominated grid, the advantage shrinks or inverts. As grids decarbonize, the advantage grows.

Similar efficiency arguments apply to:
- **Electric trucks**: feasible for urban delivery, longer routes still range-limited.
- **Electric buses**: widely deployed in China, Europe, and increasingly elsewhere.
- **Trains**: already largely electrified in Europe and Japan; electrification expanding in North America.
- **Shipping and aviation**: battery energy density insufficient for long range. Hydrogen, ammonia, synthetic fuels, and efficiency improvements are the current research directions.

## The Decarbonized Grid

Integrating high shares of wind and solar is the core grid-planning problem of the 2020s–2040s. Key techniques:

- **Geographic diversification**: spread generation across large regions so local weather doesn't dominate.
- **Storage**: shift excess daytime solar to evening peak.
- **Flexible demand**: shift consumption (EV charging, water heating, industrial processes) to times of high renewable output.
- **Firm low-carbon generation**: nuclear, hydro, geothermal, biomass, or future options (carbon capture, long-duration storage, small modular reactors).
- **Interconnections**: bigger grids smooth local variability.

The "last 10–20%" of decarbonization is harder than the first 80%, because the remaining hours are ones when renewables aren't available. Getting from 80% to 100% clean electricity requires technologies and policies still being developed.

## Why This Level Matters

Energy is the universal enabler of modern civilization. How we convert and transmit it determines what's possible, what's affordable, and what's clean. Understanding the conversion-transmission system — its physical constraints, economic logic, and reliability requirements — is essential to reasoning about energy policy, technology choices, and industrial strategy.

The transition to low-carbon energy is not primarily about generation anymore. Wind and solar are cheap. The hard parts are rebuilding grids, storing at scale, integrating flexible demand, and handling the last portion of decarbonization. Those are conversion-and-transmission problems.
