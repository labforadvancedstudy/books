## Why Energy Is Different

L2 covered energy conversion and transmission — the physical machinery. L3 turns to economics and policy. Energy is not a normal commodity. It is universal input: nearly every product and service consumed depends on energy somewhere in its production. It has national security dimensions — oil, gas, and increasingly critical minerals are strategic. It has enormous externalities — climate change, local air pollution, nuclear proliferation, resource extraction impacts — that pure markets don't price. It has long investment horizons — power plants last 40-60 years, transmission lines 50+, nuclear plants potentially 80. These features mean energy markets are the most regulated and politically contested markets in most economies.

Understanding energy markets, prices, subsidies, and the politics of transition is essential for assessing climate policy, industrial strategy, geopolitical dynamics, and household economics. The transition from fossil fuels to low-carbon energy — underway but incomplete — is the largest coordinated infrastructure change humans have attempted.

## Energy Markets

**Oil**: the most global and most fungible market. Brent and WTI are benchmark crudes; prices set by global supply and demand. OPEC coordinates ~40% of global production, providing a floor via production cuts in surplus conditions. The 1973 OPEC embargo, the 1979 Iranian revolution, the 1986 price collapse, the 2014 US shale boom, the 2020 COVID demand shock, the 2022 Ukraine war — each reshaped the oil economy for years.

**Natural gas**: partially global but more regional. LNG (liquefied natural gas) has globalized a portion of trade, but regional price differentials persist. European gas prices in 2022 spiked to 10× US prices as Russian supply was cut.

**Coal**: largely regional. International thermal coal and metallurgical coal markets exist but domestic markets dominate where production and consumption co-locate (China, India, US).

**Electricity**: inherently regional because transmission losses limit long-distance flow. Wholesale markets exist in liberalized regions (US RTOs, EU, Australia, Nordic, Japan) with day-ahead, real-time, and capacity markets. Regulated monopolies still common elsewhere.

**Nuclear fuel**: uranium market with limited transparency. Enrichment services dominated by Russia historically (Rosatom ~40% market share).

**Critical minerals** (lithium, cobalt, nickel, rare earths): rapidly emerging markets. Highly concentrated by country; some by company.

## Pricing

Energy prices vary across fuels, regions, and time:

- **Oil**: 2020s range $60-120/bbl for crude. Retail gasoline varies 2-5× across countries due to taxes and subsidies.
- **Natural gas**: varies 3-10× between regions and across time. $2-3/MMBtu US historical average; $30+ European in 2022 peak.
- **Coal**: ~$50-200/t thermal coal; metallurgical coal higher.
- **Retail electricity**: ~¢5-50/kWh by country and customer class. Residential prices in Germany 3-4× US; Hawaii 3-4× US mainland; developing-country rates often lower but unreliable supply.
- **Levelized cost of electricity (LCOE)**: modeled average cost over plant lifetime. Wind and solar LCOE has fallen 70-90% since 2010; now cheapest new generation in most regions.

**Cost components**: capital (CAPEX), operations (OPEX), fuel, financing. Capital-intensive technologies (nuclear, hydro, renewable) are sensitive to interest rates and financing terms. Fuel-intensive technologies (gas, coal) are sensitive to fuel prices.

## Subsidies

Energy subsidies are enormous and complicated:
- **IEA estimates**: global fossil fuel consumption subsidies ~$500-700B/yr. Often for fuel price controls in oil-producing and lower-income countries.
- **IMF estimates with externalities**: ~$7 trillion/yr implicit subsidy — fossil fuels not paying full environmental and health costs.
- **Renewable support**: feed-in tariffs, tax credits, renewable portfolio standards. US Inflation Reduction Act commits ~$400B+ over a decade; EU support ongoing.
- **Nuclear**: direct operating subsidies (some US states), loan guarantees for new builds, government-funded R&D.

Subsidies distort markets and often produce unintended consequences. Egypt, Iran, Venezuela, Saudi Arabia have historically subsidized fuel to extent that demand balloons and fiscal costs overwhelm budgets. Reform attempts (Indonesia 2005, Iran 2010, various countries) have mixed records.

**Carbon pricing** attempts to correct underpricing of fossil fuels. Two main types:
- **Carbon tax**: fixed price per ton CO₂. Sweden ($130+), British Columbia (~$65), France, Canada federal.
- **Cap-and-trade**: emissions cap, tradeable allowances. EU ETS (~€60-90/t CO₂ recently), California, RGGI, China national system.

Carbon prices cover ~25% of global emissions; typically at prices too low to drive transformation (IMF suggests $75+/t needed by 2030, rising).

## Externalities and Social Cost

Energy externalities are large:
- **Climate change**: social cost of carbon estimates range $50-300+/t CO₂. Reflects damage from a marginal ton of CO₂ across decades to centuries.
- **Air pollution**: PM2.5, NOx, SO₂, ozone cause premature deaths globally. ~4-7 million deaths/yr attributed to air pollution. Much comes from energy combustion.
- **Water pollution**: mining, drilling, thermal discharge.
- **Land use**: mining footprints, drilling pads, pipeline ROWs, wind turbine/solar farm land.
- **Nuclear**: waste disposal, accident risk, proliferation concerns.
- **Extraction**: community impacts, indigenous rights, resource curse dynamics in producer countries.

These costs are largely external to market prices without policy intervention. Internalizing them via taxes, regulation, or liability is the basic economic approach; progress is partial and uneven.

## Energy Transition Economics

The shift from fossil fuels to low-carbon energy is now central economic and political question.

**Cost crossover**: solar PV and onshore wind are now cheapest new generation in most regions. Offshore wind, grid-scale storage, and low-carbon hydrogen approaching or at parity. LCOE for new build is cheaper than new fossil in most competitive markets.

**Existing fossil capacity** is often still cheaper to run (marginal costs) than build new low-carbon replacements. Transition economics depend on comparing existing marginal costs to new-build levelized costs, accounting for expected lifetimes and policy.

**Stranded assets**: fossil fuel reserves and infrastructure that may not be burned/used if climate goals are met. Estimated $1-4 trillion in fossil assets at risk. Who bears the losses — shareholders, customers, governments — is politically contested.

**Transition finance**: low-carbon investments ~$1.5 trillion globally in 2023, needs to roughly triple by 2030 to meet climate goals. Directed at power, grid, buildings, transport, industry. Sources include corporate investment, institutional investors, banks, development finance institutions, governments.

**Green premium** — additional cost of low-carbon alternative vs. fossil baseline — varies enormously by sector. Near-zero for some electricity markets; 50-100% for low-carbon cement, steel, aviation fuel. Reducing green premium requires technology progress, policy, and scale.

## Energy Security

Energy security has multiple dimensions:
- **Supply reliability**: physical availability of fuel and electricity when needed.
- **Affordability**: prices manageable for consumers and industry.
- **Strategic independence**: reduced vulnerability to adversarial action.
- **Resilience**: ability to recover from disruptions (cyber, weather, attack).

Historical energy security was framed around oil imports. Post-Ukraine 2022, gas security dominates in Europe. US shale boom transformed US from import-dependent to net exporter. China and India face ongoing import dependence for oil and gas.

Low-carbon transition changes security landscape:
- **Reduced oil/gas dependence** if electrification and renewables scale.
- **Increased critical mineral dependence** — lithium, cobalt, nickel, rare earths. Many concentrated by country or controlled by single supplier (China for processing of many).
- **Grid dependence** if electricity increasingly dominates end use.
- **Cyber risk** on digital grid and new technologies.

Security policies: strategic petroleum reserves, natural gas storage mandates, diversified suppliers, domestic production support, critical mineral strategies, grid hardening.

## Political Economy

Energy decisions are inherently political:
- **Fossil fuel industries** are among the largest, most politically influential corporations. Exxon, Saudi Aramco, Gazprom, PetroChina, Shell, BP, Chevron. Employ millions, fund political activity, shape regulation.
- **Utility politics**: rate cases, regulatory capture, transmission siting. Local and state level in US; national elsewhere.
- **Labor politics**: coal mining regions (Appalachia, Silesia, Shanxi), oil workers, refinery jobs. Transition creates displacement and political backlash.
- **Consumer politics**: fuel prices, electricity bills, heating costs. Populist flashpoints.
- **Environmental politics**: climate movements, NIMBY opposition to infrastructure (wind farms, transmission lines, nuclear plants).
- **Geopolitics**: petrostates, resource-producer cartels, strategic leverage from energy dependence.

Energy transition requires political coalitions that sustain support over decades. History suggests this is hard. Reversals (Brazil 2019, US 2017-2020, various European countries partially) are common.

**Just transition**: policy frameworks for managing worker and community impacts of energy shifts. Germany's coal regions, Spain's mining regions, US Inflation Reduction Act provisions include transition support. Effectiveness contested.

## Developing Country Energy

Energy poverty remains widespread:
- ~700 million lack electricity access; ~2 billion lack clean cooking fuel.
- Firewood, charcoal, dung combustion causes ~3 million premature deaths annually from indoor air pollution — mostly women and children.
- Lack of reliable power constrains productive enterprise in many African and South Asian countries.

Energy transitions in developing countries differ:
- **Building infrastructure** while deploying low-carbon tech — leapfrogging potential.
- **Financing constraints**: high cost of capital, limited access to low-cost international finance.
- **Development imperatives**: growing energy demand as economies grow. India, Southeast Asia, Africa projected to drive most future demand growth.
- **Climate finance**: rich-country commitments (e.g., $100B/yr Paris pledge) have fallen short. Just Energy Transition Partnerships with South Africa, Indonesia, Vietnam emerging.

Distributed solar with battery storage has transformed access in some regions. Mini-grids, pay-as-you-go solar home systems reach remote populations that grid extension won't.

## Why This Level Matters

Energy economics and policy will shape whether and how fast the world decarbonizes — and therefore how much climate change is locked in. They'll shape whether energy remains affordable during transition. They'll shape which countries and companies dominate the 21st-century energy economy. They'll shape national security and international power dynamics.

Engineering decisions about power plants, building systems, vehicles, industrial processes operate within this economic and political landscape. A project that pencils financially today may be stranded in 15 years if carbon prices rise; conversely, a project that looks expensive today may be standard in 10 years as policies and costs shift. Understanding the pressures on energy policy is part of responsible infrastructure and industrial decision-making.
