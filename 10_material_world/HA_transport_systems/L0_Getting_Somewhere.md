# Level 0: Getting Somewhere
*Where everyone meets transport: by moving*

## The Commute

You got somewhere today. Walking, driving, bus, subway, bike. You didn't think about it much. The trip was 10 minutes, 30 minutes, 2 hours. It was annoying or pleasant. You arrived.

Now multiply that by 8 billion people making multiple trips per day, plus every object any of them used, plus every piece of food any of them ate. The transport system of Earth moves roughly that much every day, all day, all year, and has for decades.

## The Container That Changed Everything

In 1956 a man named Malcolm McLean put 58 trailer-sized metal boxes on a converted oil tanker and shipped them from Newark to Houston. This was the first container ship. Before containers, loading a cargo ship meant dock workers physically moving barrels and crates by hand, one at a time, for days. A ship sat in port for most of its life.

After containers, a ship of 20,000 TEU (20-foot equivalent units) can be unloaded and reloaded in under a day. Sea freight costs dropped by roughly an order of magnitude. That drop made global supply chains economically feasible. Everything you own with "Made in China" or "Made in Vietnam" on it exists partly because of McLean's steel box.

## The Invisible Vehicle

When something arrives at your house from an online order, the last step — the delivery truck — is visible. The earlier steps are not. The item probably traveled:

- By truck from factory to regional hub
- By truck or rail to a port
- By container ship across an ocean (1-6 weeks)
- By rail or truck to a distribution center
- By truck to a fulfillment center
- By van to your door

Five to seven different vehicles, each optimized for a specific distance and bulk tradeoff. Ships are cheap per ton-mile and slow. Aircraft are fast per ton-mile and expensive. Rail is middle. Trucks are the flexible glue that makes the others connect to actual addresses. Pipelines move fluid cargo with the lowest per-ton cost of all. Each mode has a niche.

## The Road That Ate America

Most of the developed world, after 1945, made a massive bet on the private car. Suburbs spread. Highways multiplied. Rail declined. City centers emptied. The car became not just a vehicle but a unit of identity.

This bet paid off in freedom of movement and paid a cost in sprawl, pollution, road deaths (~1.3 million per year globally), air quality, and physical inactivity. Cities that resisted the bet (many European, some East Asian) now look enviable compared to those that went all-in (most American, Australian, Canadian cities).

The choice is not reversible in 5 years. It is partially reversible in 50. Decisions made 70 years ago are still shaping where and how you live.

## The Flight That Doesn't Make Sense

Aircraft have not fundamentally changed in 60 years. A 1960 Boeing 707 and a 2020 Boeing 737 are roughly the same thing — a tube with swept wings and two turbofans. Engines got quieter and more efficient. Electronics got better. Seats got tighter. Speed did not change. Most of the gains went to cost reduction and passenger volume, not performance.

Aviation is also the single most carbon-intensive common activity. A single round-trip transatlantic economy seat is roughly 1-2 tons of CO₂ — more than many people in poor countries emit in a year. About 2.5% of global emissions are from aviation, rising as the middle class expands globally.

Solutions exist but are early: sustainable aviation fuels (biomass or synthetic), battery-electric for short flights, hydrogen for medium range. None scale to cover global aviation anytime soon.

## The City Designed Around One Mode

Walk the old part of any pre-car city — Paris, Kyoto, Rome, Hanoi. Streets are narrow, buildings are dense, everything is within walking distance, rail and tram lines are integrated, cars feel like intruders.

Walk a post-car suburb. Wide streets, low density, big parking lots, few sidewalks, nothing within walking distance, rail lines are absent, life without a car is genuinely difficult.

These are different products of different transport assumptions. Neither is intrinsically "natural." They are designs, made by people, within political and economic constraints. And they shape who can live where, get which jobs, access which services.

## The Pipeline You Don't See

Oil, natural gas, and chemicals move through pipelines. So does water, sewage, heating steam, compressed air, slurry. Pipelines are the cheapest way to move fluid bulk cargo per ton-mile. They are also invisible in daily life until one ruptures (oil spill, gas explosion) or is attacked (Nord Stream, Colonial Pipeline).

Millions of miles of pipelines crisscross every developed country. They move more tonnage than trucks in many regions. They are easy to ignore because nobody sees them. They are not easy to do without.

## The First Lesson

Transport is the connective tissue of civilization. Without it you do not have cities, supply chains, food security, global trade, or a modern economy. The design of a transport system — which modes get investment, how they integrate, who can afford to use them — silently shapes everything else about how a society runs.

Every trip you take is built on a stack of decisions made by other people decades ago. Every package you receive took routes engineered by logistics companies you will never hear the name of. It all looks mundane because it works.
