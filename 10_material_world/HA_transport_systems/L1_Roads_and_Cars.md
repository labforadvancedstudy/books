# Level 1: Roads & Cars
*The dominant mode of transport in most of the world*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Scale of Cars

There are roughly 1.5 billion motor vehicles in the world as of the mid-2020s. About 1.1 billion are cars; the rest are commercial vehicles, motorcycles, and trucks. Global vehicle production runs at roughly 85–90 million new units per year.

The US has about 290 million registered vehicles for a population of 330 million — nearly one per person. Europe averages around 500 vehicles per thousand people. China has risen from under 50 per thousand in 2005 to over 200 per thousand by the mid-2020s. Africa remains under 50 per thousand in most countries.

The combined distance driven per year globally is roughly 15 trillion vehicle-kilometers. The fuel and electricity burned to move those vehicles represents about 20% of global CO₂ emissions and a similar share of global oil consumption.

## What Is a Car, Mechanically

A car is a controllable box of metal and plastic with:

- **Powertrain**: engine (or motor), transmission, driveshaft, differential.
- **Chassis**: frame or unibody structure holding everything together.
- **Suspension**: springs, shock absorbers, control arms. Isolates the passenger from road roughness.
- **Steering**: rack and pinion mechanism turning wheel angle.
- **Brakes**: hydraulic discs (and sometimes drums) converting motion into heat.
- **Wheels and tires**: the only parts that touch the road. Tires are one of the most heavily engineered products in existence.
- **Body**: crash structure, aerodynamic surface, weatherproof passenger compartment.
- **Electronics**: engine management, safety systems, infotainment, increasingly driver assistance.

A modern car has 20,000–30,000 parts. It is produced in a facility capable of making one every 60–90 seconds, with robotic welders, stamping presses, paint lines, and final assembly lines. The total labor hours per vehicle dropped from ~200 in the 1970s to ~20 by the 2000s — a productivity revolution driven by lean manufacturing.

## The Internal Combustion Engine

Most cars still run on internal combustion engines (ICE) burning gasoline or diesel. Simplified cycle:

1. Intake: piston pulls air (and fuel, in gasoline engines) into the cylinder.
2. Compression: piston pushes up, compressing the mixture.
3. Power: fuel ignites (spark in gasoline, compression in diesel), gas expands, drives piston down.
4. Exhaust: piston pushes up again, expelling burnt gases.

This four-stroke cycle repeats thousands of times per minute in a typical car engine. The torque produced spins a crankshaft, passes through a transmission that matches engine RPM to wheel RPM, and ultimately turns the wheels.

Gasoline engines are optimized for power and smoothness. Diesel engines are optimized for torque and fuel efficiency (~30% better mileage than gasoline for the same work, at the cost of higher particulate emissions unless expensively treated).

ICE efficiency is fundamentally limited by thermodynamics. Modern gasoline engines peak at ~35% efficiency; diesels at ~45%. Most of the fuel's energy becomes waste heat.

## Electric Vehicles

An EV replaces the engine, transmission, and fuel system with:

- **Battery pack**: a few hundred to over a thousand lithium-ion cells in series and parallel, providing 50–120 kWh of energy storage.
- **Electric motor(s)**: typically permanent-magnet synchronous motors or induction motors, delivering 100–400 kW peak power.
- **Power electronics**: inverter converting DC battery power into AC for the motor, plus regenerative braking.
- **Charging system**: AC charger for level 1/2 home charging; DC fast-charging hardware for highway stops.

EVs are ~90% efficient at converting stored energy to motion, vs. ~20–25% for ICE cars. They have no tailpipe emissions. Their lifecycle emissions depend on the grid: charged from coal, an EV has similar total emissions to a modern ICE; charged from a clean grid, an EV is far cleaner.

Battery cost has dropped from ~$1,100/kWh in 2010 to under $150/kWh by the mid-2020s — an 85%+ reduction. This is the enabling change. EVs became cost-competitive with ICE in purchase price around the late 2020s for most segments. Total cost of ownership (fuel + maintenance) has been lower for several years.

Limits of EVs: charging infrastructure (improving fast), charging time (a gasoline car refuels in 5 minutes; an EV fast-charge is 20–30 minutes), range in cold weather (battery capacity drops 20–30%), and battery lifecycle (current batteries last 8–15 years; recycling infrastructure is still maturing).

## Roads

Roads are civil engineering with specific design criteria:

- **Horizontal alignment**: curves have minimum radius based on design speed. A 100 km/h highway curve might require a radius of 400+ m; a 30 km/h residential street, 20 m.
- **Vertical alignment**: grades (slopes) limited by vehicle power and sight distance. Interstate highways rarely exceed 6%.
- **Cross-section**: lane width (usually 3.5–3.7 m), shoulders, median, drainage.
- **Pavement structure**: subgrade (native soil), sub-base (compacted gravel), base (asphalt-treated or cement-treated), surface (asphalt or concrete).
- **Drainage**: crown in the pavement to shed water, ditches or storm drains at the edges.

Pavement life depends on traffic load, climate, and construction quality. A heavily-loaded interstate might need resurfacing every 10–15 years and reconstruction every 40–50. A residential street might last longer per pass but see far less traffic.

The global road network totals roughly 40 million kilometers of paved and unpaved roads. The US Interstate Highway System alone is about 78,000 km. China built over 170,000 km of expressways between 1990 and 2020 — the fastest large-scale road construction in history.

## Traffic, Physics of

A highway's capacity (vehicles per hour per lane) depends on speed and spacing. Maximum flow typically occurs around 60–80 km/h — above that, drivers leave more space and flow declines despite higher speed; below that, slower speed dominates. This is why congested highways are slow — they've passed peak flow and entered the congested regime.

Traffic signals coordinate flow at intersections. Signal timing is a surprisingly deep subject: platoon propagation, green waves, coordination schemes. Well-tuned signals can roughly double intersection capacity vs. stop signs or uncoordinated signals.

Human factors dominate everything: reaction time (~1 second), perception-reaction distance, panic braking, distraction, fatigue, inebriation. The single largest cause of traffic crashes is human error, not mechanical failure. This is the promise of autonomous driving — and its difficulty, since the long tail of edge cases where computers fail turns out to be larger than expected.

## Safety

Global traffic deaths number roughly 1.2 million per year, with tens of millions of injuries. This is a catastrophic ongoing human toll, and in many developing countries, road crashes are a leading cause of death for young adults.

The US saw about 40,000 traffic deaths in 2022 — roughly one every 13 minutes. Europe is substantially safer per capita (France: ~6 per 100,000; Sweden: ~2.5; US: ~12). The difference is mostly infrastructure (safer road design), vehicle regulations (active safety systems), and enforcement (speed, drunk driving).

Vehicle safety has improved dramatically: crumple zones, airbags, electronic stability control (mandatory in many markets since the early 2010s), automatic emergency braking. A driver in a 2024 car is perhaps 3× less likely to die in a crash at the same speed than one in a 1970 car, despite the roads being the same.

## Urban vs. Intercity

Car-dominated transport works very differently in cities vs. between cities:

**Intercity**: cars (or buses) on highways move people and goods reasonably efficiently at moderate cost. Rail competes for heavier freight and higher passenger volumes on corridors with enough demand. Flying dominates for longer distances.

**Urban**: cars are inefficient. A typical car carries 1.2 people and occupies ~15 m² of road plus parking. A bus carries 30–80 with much better space efficiency. A subway line carries tens of thousands per hour in a narrow corridor. When cities scale up population, private cars stop working — you cannot build enough road and parking.

Asian and European cities — Tokyo, Seoul, Paris, Amsterdam, Copenhagen — largely stopped prioritizing private cars decades ago. They built rail, subway, bus, and cycling infrastructure, plus pedestrianized city centers. North American and Australian cities still struggle with car dependence, with Los Angeles and Houston as extreme examples.

## Why This Level Matters

Cars and roads are the default of American and suburban European transport, and are how most people think about getting around. They are also the mode with the worst scaling properties in dense settings, the largest CO₂ footprint per passenger-kilometer, and the highest safety cost per trip.

Understanding how they actually work — vehicle mechanics, road engineering, traffic physics, safety data — is prerequisite for any serious transportation planning or policy discussion. The next decades will see partial but not complete electrification, continued but slow improvement in safety, and in dense cities, active substitution of car infrastructure with transit and active-mobility infrastructure.
