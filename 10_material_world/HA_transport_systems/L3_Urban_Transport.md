# Level 3: Urban Transport
*Moving people through cities — modes, congestion, transit, and the geography of access*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Daily Commute

L2 covered rail, ship, and air — long-distance freight and passenger movement. L3 zooms into the city. Most trips humans make are short and urban: commuting, shopping, school, medical, recreation. Urban transport is not a subset of long-distance transport — it operates on different physics, economics, and governance. A city's transport system shapes where people live, what jobs they can reach, how long their workday effectively is, how much pollution they breathe, how often they die in traffic, and how much of their income goes to movement. Few systems matter more to daily life; few are more politically contested.

This level covers the main urban modes, their tradeoffs, and how they fit together. The framing question: how do cities move millions of people reliably, affordably, and sustainably, given finite street space, finite budgets, and very different travel needs across populations?

## The Modes

Urban transport modes differ in capacity, speed, flexibility, cost, and footprint.

**Walking**: the default. ~5 km/h, zero fuel, full flexibility for short distances (<2 km). Requires sidewalks, crossings, and land uses that put destinations within reach. Underinvested in many cities.

**Cycling**: 15-25 km/h. Low cost, healthy, zero emissions. Extends reach of walkable city. Requires protected infrastructure to feel safe for most riders. Netherlands, Denmark, some German cities achieve 20-40% cycling mode share; most car-oriented cities <5%.

**Mass transit**:
- **Bus**: most flexible; routes can change. 20-40 km/h in mixed traffic; 25-50 km/h with dedicated lanes; 30-60 km/h bus rapid transit (BRT). Capacity ~40-120 passengers per bus; throughput ~3,000-8,000 passengers per hour per direction on good corridor.
- **Light rail / tram**: 20-30 km/h average including stops. Higher capacity than bus, lower than metro.
- **Metro / subway / heavy rail**: 30-40 km/h including stops. 60,000+ passengers per hour per direction at peak. Grade-separated, high capacity, expensive to build ($100M-$1B+ per km).
- **Commuter rail / regional rail**: connects suburbs and satellites. 40-80 km/h average. Moderate frequency typical.
- **BRT (Bus Rapid Transit)**: bus-based with features of metro (dedicated lanes, stations, payment). 50-70% of metro capacity at ~10-20% of capital cost. Curitiba, Bogotá, Jakarta, Istanbul notable systems.

**Private car**: door-to-door convenience, flexible timing, but urban average speeds 15-25 km/h due to congestion (well below highway speeds). Biggest urban space footprint per person-trip.

**Taxi / ridesharing (Uber, Lyft, Didi, Ola)**: convenience of car without ownership. Increased urban vehicle-miles in many cities (drivers cruising), not decreased.

**Motorcycles and scooters**: dominant in many Asian cities. ~100 cc motorbikes cheap to own and operate; dangerous but effective. E-scooters and e-bike shares growing.

**Micromobility**: bike share, e-scooter share, pedicabs. Complementary to transit and walking for "last mile."

## Capacity and Urban Space

The key engineering reality: urban space is finite. One lane of urban street can move:
- **Private cars**: ~1,500-2,000 vehicle per hour, ~2,000-2,600 people per hour (~1.3 people/vehicle).
- **Buses in mixed traffic**: ~3,000 people per hour.
- **Dedicated bus lane / BRT**: ~8,000-20,000 people per hour.
- **Light rail on street**: ~5,000-15,000 people per hour.
- **Metro**: ~30,000-80,000 people per hour in one direction.
- **Sidewalk**: walkers ~4,000-12,000 per hour per 2-meter width.
- **Protected bike lane**: ~5,000-10,000 cyclists per hour.

This is why dense cities require transit to function. No amount of road building provides enough capacity at private-car densities to serve major metros. Auto-oriented cities handle lower densities only, enforce urban sprawl, and accept congestion.

## Congestion

Congestion arises when demand exceeds capacity at peak times. **Fundamental diagram** of traffic flow: throughput increases with density up to a critical point, then collapses as further density reduces speed faster than it increases flow. Above critical density, adding vehicles makes everyone slower without adding throughput.

Congestion dynamics:
- **Peak hours**: severe rush-hour congestion in most metros. Off-peak relatively free.
- **Induced demand**: new road capacity attracts trips that would not otherwise happen, filling capacity. "Fundamental law of road congestion" (Downs, Duranton-Turner).
- **Bottlenecks**: single slow segments cascade delay upstream.

**Congestion pricing**: charging vehicles to enter central city or at peak times. London (2003+), Stockholm (2006+), Singapore (since 1975), Milan. Effective at reducing traffic 15-30%; politically difficult to implement. New York City zone pricing started 2025.

**Cordon pricing** vs. **distance/time-based pricing**: different designs trade off simplicity and targeting. Electronic tolling technology (license plate reading, transponders) enables complex schemes.

## Transit Planning

Transit design involves routing, frequency, span, speed, fare, transfer policy:

- **Grid vs. radial networks**: grids (Chicago, Berlin) offer many origin-destination pairs; radial (Paris, London, Tokyo) concentrate on CBD connections.
- **Transfer-based vs. direct-service**: transferring reduces lines needed but adds trip time and cognitive cost.
- **Frequency**: "frequency is freedom." 10-minute headways make transit feel usable without schedule; hourly service requires planning.
- **Span of service**: early morning, late night, weekend service affects accessibility for shift workers, healthcare, social trips.
- **Speed**: average speed competitive with car matters. Dedicated ROW (metro, BRT) vs. mixed traffic makes huge difference.

**Farebox recovery**: fraction of operating cost covered by fares. Varies widely:
- **Hong Kong MTR**: >150% (profitable, plus property development).
- **Tokyo**: 100%+ on core lines.
- **New York MTA**: ~40-50%.
- **European systems**: typically 40-70%.
- **US transit typical**: 20-40%.

Subsidies are the norm. Justification: positive externalities (reduced congestion, pollution, accessibility benefits), equity, land-use coordination.

**Fare integration**: one fare for multimodal trips reduces transfer penalties. Contactless payment (Oyster London, Suica Tokyo, SmarTrip DC) dominates. Distance-based vs. flat fares; zone systems vs. uniform.

## Transit Ridership and Modal Split

Transit share of urban trips varies dramatically:
- **Hong Kong, Singapore, Tokyo**: ~60-85% transit + walking mode share.
- **Western European cities (Vienna, Berlin, Stockholm, Paris)**: 40-60%.
- **Seoul, Taipei, Moscow, Shanghai**: 50-70%.
- **US major metros (NYC, DC, Boston, Chicago)**: 15-35% in core; much lower in suburbs.
- **Most US and Australian cities**: <5% transit mode share.

Drivers of ridership: density, mix of land uses, transit quality (frequency, speed, coverage), fare, parking availability, street network, demographic patterns.

**Post-pandemic** urban transit has partially recovered but not fully. Transit ridership was hit especially hard (-60% at peak). Recovery uneven: NYC at ~75% of pre-pandemic, Paris ~95%, some US systems still <65%. Remote work has reshaped commute patterns, with Tuesday-Wednesday-Thursday peaks and reduced Monday/Friday ridership.

## Urban Form

Urban form and transport are co-determined. Higher density, mixed uses, walkable networks support transit; auto-orientation favors lower-density single-use development.

**Transit-oriented development (TOD)**: dense mixed-use around transit stations. Tokyo (development rights tied to transit), Hong Kong (MTR owns property), Arlington Rosslyn-Ballston corridor, Stockholm, Copenhagen station areas.

**Sprawl** — low-density, car-dependent development — emerged in US post-WWII with highway subsidies, single-family zoning, and cheap land/fuel. Replicated in much of Australia, Canada, and increasingly elsewhere. Challenges: long commutes, obesity, social isolation, transit infeasibility, environmental footprint.

**15-minute city**: Paris mayor Anne Hidalgo's framing. Everyone should have essentials within 15-minute walk or bike. Oriented toward local amenities, mixed uses, neighborhood identity. Applied variably; resistance from drivers and some political groups.

## Electrification and Clean Vehicles

Urban transport electrification underway:
- **Electric buses**: several cities fully or nearly fully electric (Shenzhen completely, many European cities transitioning). Battery ranges now support full service; cost competitive with diesel.
- **Electric taxis / rideshare**: some cities mandating fleets electric by specific dates.
- **Electric cars**: rising rapidly. Norway >80% new-car sales electric; other European markets, China at 20-40%; US still low but growing. Urban range requirements modest; charging at home/work addresses most needs.
- **E-bikes and scooters**: exploded in many cities.
- **Hydrogen**: limited urban penetration; fleet applications (some buses) in niche markets.

Electrification addresses tailpipe pollution (major urban health benefit) and CO₂ (if grid decarbonizes). Doesn't address congestion, space use, or accident patterns.

## Road Safety

Traffic crashes killed ~1.3 million people globally in 2021. ~50 million injured. Leading cause of death for ages 5-29. Disproportionately affects pedestrians, cyclists, motorcyclists, lower-income populations.

**Vision Zero**: Sweden-originated approach targeting zero traffic deaths. Emphasizes road design that protects humans, speed limits appropriate for context, enforcement, driver education. Stockholm, Oslo achieved near-zero pedestrian fatalities; New York, others adopted with partial results.

Safety design: separated infrastructure (protected bike lanes, grade-separated crossings), speed management (20 km/h urban zones), intersection design (reduced curb radii, raised crossings), lighting, enforcement.

US and Australian traffic deaths have risen in the 2020s against global trend — attributed to larger vehicles (SUVs, pickups), distracted driving, declining pedestrian infrastructure investment.

## Autonomous Vehicles

**Autonomous vehicle (AV)** progress since ~2010 has been faster than critics said, slower than proponents said. Current state (2025):
- **Level 4 commercial deployment**: robotaxi services in specific cities (Waymo in Phoenix, SF, LA; Cruise troubled; Chinese companies in multiple cities). Limited geofenced areas, adjust to weather/conditions.
- **Level 2/3 advanced driver assistance (ADAS)** widely deployed in consumer vehicles. Helps but remains human-supervised.
- **Full autonomy** still not commercialized at scale.

Implications still uncertain:
- **Reduced traffic**: if high occupancy and smoothed flow.
- **Increased traffic**: if empty vehicles cruising, more trips taken because easier.
- **Labor**: taxi, delivery, truck driving jobs reshaped.
- **Urban form**: less need for parking near destinations; implications for curb use, dropoff/pickup zones.
- **Safety**: potentially dramatic reductions but transition period complex.

## Accessibility and Equity

Transportation is essential to economic opportunity. Lack of reliable transport is a major barrier to employment, healthcare, education for low-income populations.

Key issues:
- **Transit deserts**: areas with poor transit access.
- **First/last mile**: connections from transit to origin/destination.
- **Affordability**: fare burden on low-income households.
- **Paratransit**: service for those unable to use regular transit (disability, elderly).
- **Safety and perception**: safety concerns, especially for women and marginalized groups, affect transit use.

Universal design: level boarding, wheelchair accessibility, audible/visual information, wayfinding accessible to diverse users.

**Transport and climate justice**: low-income populations often live farthest from jobs, spend more income on transport, suffer most from pollution, and have least voice in transportation decisions. Transit and cycling investment can expand opportunity; highway/displacement projects have historically harmed these same populations.

## Why This Level Matters

How people move through cities determines quality of life for billions. Urban transport shapes economic opportunity, health outcomes, environmental impact, social cohesion, and daily experience. The choices cities make about transport investment — highways vs. transit vs. cycling, road pricing, parking policy, land use coordination — lock in patterns that persist for generations.

The transition to electric, autonomous, shared mobility is underway but uncertain in pace and direction. Meanwhile, fundamental tradeoffs remain: street space is finite, transit requires density, cars move few people per lane, and safe walking/cycling need dedicated infrastructure. These constraints don't go away with technology.
