# Level 2: Rail, Ship, Air
*The long-distance movers — railroads, ocean shipping, and aviation*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## Beyond Roads

Roads and cars solved the short-distance, door-to-door movement problem. They did not solve movement of bulk freight across continents, movement of containers across oceans, or movement of people across hemispheres in hours. Three different modes — rail, ship, and air — handle those problems, each with its own physics, economics, and infrastructure.

Together with road, these four modes form the global transport system. They compete at the margins (rail vs. truck for medium-distance freight, high-speed rail vs. short-haul air) but mostly occupy distinct niches set by the physics of distance, mass, and time.

## Rail — The Steel Wheel

The fundamental advantage of rail is low rolling resistance. Steel wheel on steel rail has a rolling resistance coefficient of ~0.001, compared to ~0.007 for rubber tire on asphalt. A locomotive can haul tonnage that would require hundreds of trucks, burning a fraction of the fuel per tonne-km.

Rail's disadvantages: it's stuck on fixed tracks, schedules have to be coordinated across a network, and door-to-door delivery requires handoff to trucks. Rail competes where tonnage and distance make these costs worth paying.

### Heavy-Haul Freight

In North America, rail moves coal, grain, chemicals, auto parts, intermodal containers, and forest products. A single Class I railroad (Union Pacific, BNSF, CSX, Norfolk Southern, Canadian National, Canadian Pacific Kansas City) operates 15,000–30,000 km of track with tens of thousands of rail cars. Trains of 10,000+ tonnes are routine; iron ore and coal unit trains exceed 20,000 tonnes.

Australia takes heavy haul further. The Pilbara iron ore networks (BHP, Rio Tinto, FMG) run trains up to 40,000 tonnes and 2.5 km long, shuttling ore from mines to ports continuously.

Energy efficiency of freight rail is exceptional: ~50–100 tonne-km per liter of diesel for well-designed networks. Intermodal containers moved by rail emit ~70% less CO₂ per tonne-km than moved by long-haul truck.

### Passenger Rail

Modern passenger rail falls into tiers:

- **Heavy rail commuter**: Tokyo, New York, Chicago, London, Paris, Seoul — dense urban/suburban networks moving millions daily.
- **Intercity conventional rail**: 100–160 km/h typical, serves medium distances. US Amtrak, regional European services.
- **High-speed rail (HSR)**: 250 km/h and above. Japan's Shinkansen (since 1964), France's TGV (since 1981), China's CRH (since 2008) dominate. Europe has extensive HSR; China has built more HSR in 15 years than the rest of the world combined.
- **Maglev**: magnetic levitation, bypassing wheel-rail contact. Shanghai's airport maglev (~430 km/h) and Japan's planned Chuo Shinkansen (~500 km/h) are the operating and near-operating systems.

HSR competes with short-haul air on 400–1000 km routes. Where built, it often dominates (Madrid-Barcelona, Paris-Lyon, Tokyo-Osaka) — faster city-center to city-center than flying when you include airport overhead.

### Rail in the US vs. Elsewhere

US rail is freight-dominant: ~40% of US freight tonne-km moves by rail, compared to ~15% in Europe. US passenger rail is weak outside the Northeast Corridor. Reasons: US distances favor freight; low population density makes passenger rail economics harder; highway investment dominated post-WWII.

Europe and East Asia are passenger-dominant. Freight shares are lower (10–15% of tonne-km in Europe) because shorter distances make trucking more competitive and because most rail capacity is dedicated to passenger services.

Rail electrification: ~75% of European rail traffic and ~100% of Japanese and South Korean rail is electrified. US mainline freight rail is almost entirely diesel-electric. Full electrification would reduce emissions substantially but requires major capital investment.

## Ocean Shipping — The Steel Hull

If rail moves continents, ships move planets. The numbers:

- ~80% of global trade by volume moves by sea.
- ~11 billion tonnes of cargo are loaded at ports each year.
- ~100,000 merchant vessels are in service worldwide.
- Main ship types by tonnage: bulk carriers (ore, grain, coal), oil tankers (crude, products), container ships, gas carriers (LNG, LPG), general cargo, roll-on/roll-off (ro-ro).

### Container Revolution

Before 1956, ocean cargo was "break bulk" — loaded piece by piece, stowed carefully by dockworkers, a labor-intensive operation that dominated ship turnaround time.

Malcolm McLean's container — an 8×8×20 foot steel box that could transfer directly between truck, rail, and ship without unpacking — transformed everything. Ports shrank in labor requirements while growing in throughput. Container ships grew from ~700 TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units) in the 1960s to over 24,000 TEU by the 2020s.

The world's container fleet carries ~60% of global maritime trade by value in standardized boxes. The cost to move a TEU from Shanghai to Los Angeles is typically $1,500–3,000 (varies with market cycles), vs. tens of thousands for pre-container break-bulk. Globalization — the ability to make a thing in one country, ship a container to another country, and still sell it cheaper than local production — depends on the container.

### Ship Size and Economics

Ships scale favorably: doubling capacity doesn't double crew, fuel per TEU, or building cost. Shipping economics therefore push ship sizes up, limited mostly by port depths and canal dimensions.

Key size classes:
- **Handysize** (~20,000–40,000 DWT): flexible, fits most ports.
- **Panamax** (~75,000 DWT): sized for old Panama Canal locks.
- **New Panamax** (~120,000 DWT): sized for the 2016 canal expansion.
- **Capesize** (~150,000–400,000 DWT): too big for Panama Canal, sails around Africa (Cape of Good Hope). Iron ore and coal.
- **VLCC / ULCC** (very/ultra large crude carriers, 200,000–550,000 DWT): crude oil.
- **Largest container ships**: ~24,000 TEU, ~400 m long, ~60 m wide. Cannot use the old Panama Canal; fit the new one.

Ever Given (the ship that blocked the Suez Canal in 2021) is a New Panamax container ship. The Ever Ace (2021) is larger, at ~24,000 TEU.

### Ports and Chokepoints

Major ports: Shanghai, Ningbo-Zhoushan, Singapore, Shenzhen, Guangzhou, Qingdao, Busan, Tianjin, Hong Kong, Rotterdam, Jebel Ali (Dubai), Los Angeles / Long Beach, Antwerp.

Seven maritime chokepoints carry a significant fraction of global trade:
- Strait of Hormuz: ~20% of global oil trade.
- Strait of Malacca: ~25% of traded goods, main East Asia-Europe route.
- Suez Canal: ~12% of global trade, shortcut to the Mediterranean.
- Panama Canal: ~5% of global trade, Atlantic-Pacific for the Americas.
- Bab-el-Mandeb: Red Sea entrance.
- Bosphorus / Turkish Straits: Russia's Black Sea access.
- Danish Straits: Baltic access.

Disruption of any one reverberates globally within days. The Suez closure in 2021 (Ever Given grounding) diverted dozens of ships around Africa, adding ~10 days and ~20% to fuel costs for the affected cargo. Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping since late 2023 have had similar effects — ships rerouting around Africa, extending supply chains.

### Shipping Emissions

Ocean shipping produces ~3% of global CO₂ emissions. Per tonne-km, shipping is more efficient than any other freight mode, but the tonnage is immense. It's also historically among the dirtiest forms of transport — ships burn **bunker fuel** (residual fuel oil with very high sulfur content) that would never be allowed in road or aviation fuel.

International regulation has reduced this: the IMO's 2020 sulfur limit cut ship sulfur emissions dramatically. Progress on CO₂ is slower. LNG-fueled ships, methanol-fueled ships, and the first ammonia-powered vessels are in service or development. Hydrogen and synthetic fuels are further out. Fleet turnover is slow (ship lifetimes 20–30 years), so even aggressive new-ship rules take decades to transform the whole fleet.

## Aviation — The Steel Tube

Commercial aviation moves passengers fast and freight by value. The scale:

- ~4 billion passenger-trips per year pre-COVID (2019). Recovered to near those levels by 2024.
- ~27,000 commercial aircraft in service worldwide.
- Global aviation fuel use: ~300 million tonnes per year, ~2.5% of global CO₂ emissions.
- Air freight: only ~0.5% of global freight by tonne, but ~35% by value (electronics, pharmaceuticals, urgent shipments).

### Commercial Aircraft

The fleet is dominated by two manufacturers:

- **Boeing**: 737 (narrow-body, short/medium-haul), 777 and 787 (wide-body, long-haul), 747 (retired from passenger but still freight), 767 (largely freight).
- **Airbus**: A320 family (narrow-body), A330, A350 (wide-body), A380 (retired from production 2021).

Regional jets: Embraer E-Jets, Mitsubishi SpaceJet (canceled), various Russian/Chinese aircraft.

Narrow-body workhorses (737, A320 family) make up ~70% of the active fleet. They serve short and medium-haul routes (1–6 hours typical). Wide-body aircraft (A330, A350, 777, 787) serve long-haul and transoceanic routes.

### Airline Economics

An airline's major costs: fuel (~20–30%), labor (~20–30%), aircraft ownership (~10–15%), maintenance (~10%), airport fees (~10%), other. A typical airline operating margin is ~5% in good times, negative in bad times. The industry has a long record of unprofitability across full cycles.

Airlines operate on **hub-and-spoke** networks (connecting flights through hubs — efficient routing, fewer fleet types, more connections) or **point-to-point** (direct flights — faster for passengers, but lower aircraft utilization). Most major airlines operate both modes, with hubs at their home airports.

Low-cost carriers (Southwest, Ryanair, EasyJet, IndiGo, etc.) operate simpler networks, single aircraft types (usually 737 or A320), and aggressive operational efficiency. They captured substantial market share in 2000–2020.

### Air Traffic Control

Aircraft are separated by air traffic control (ATC) — a system of radars, radios, computers, and human controllers that manages ~100,000 flights per day globally. Modern ATC uses transponder codes (ADS-B) to identify and track aircraft; controllers direct traffic with voice radio and automation support.

Airspace is divided into zones managed from area control centers. Enroute aircraft follow airways at assigned altitudes, transition to approach control near airports, and finally to tower control for takeoff/landing clearance.

Capacity bottlenecks are usually at airports (runway capacity, gate capacity, crew scheduling) not in the air. Runway capacity peaks around 40–60 landings per hour per runway — the reason big airports keep adding runways, not just bigger terminals.

### Aviation Emissions

Aviation's 2.5% of global CO₂ understates the warming impact. Aircraft contrails and NOx emissions at altitude roughly double the climate impact. Aviation is also the hardest major sector to decarbonize — batteries are too heavy for long flights, hydrogen requires major aircraft and airport infrastructure changes, sustainable aviation fuel (SAF, biofuel or synthetic) is available but expensive and limited in supply.

Current decarbonization path: efficiency improvements (new aircraft ~15–20% more fuel-efficient per seat-km than the generation before), SAF (a few percent of fuel use today, aiming for 10% by 2030 and much more beyond), offsetting schemes (CORSIA), and long-term electrification of short-haul and hydrogen for longer ranges. Most projections show aviation emissions continuing to grow before declining — absent a major technology breakthrough, it's a sector that will be hard to zero out.

## Multi-modal Logistics

Real supply chains are rarely single-mode. A container from a Shanghai factory to a Chicago distribution center:

1. **Truck**: factory to Shanghai port.
2. **Ship**: Shanghai to Los Angeles or Long Beach (~13 days transpacific).
3. **Rail**: port to Chicago (~3 days).
4. **Truck**: rail terminal to destination (hours).

Total: ~20 days door-to-door, typically $2,000–5,000 per container depending on markets.

Intermodal handoffs are the friction points. Ports, rail yards, and trucking terminals are where delays accumulate. Digital logistics platforms, automated terminals, and better coordination are the operational frontier.

## Why This Level Matters

Roads and cars organize daily life. Rail, ship, and air organize industrial and commercial life at continental and global scales. Without them, there is no global economy — just disconnected regional economies with limited trade.

Each mode has distinct physics and economics. Understanding which mode fits which problem — when to put a container on a ship vs. a plane, when to move freight by rail vs. truck, when to build an airport vs. a port vs. a rail line — is the core of logistics strategy, supply chain design, and trade policy.
