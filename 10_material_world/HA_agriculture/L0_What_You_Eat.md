# Level 0: What You Eat
*Where everyone meets agriculture: three times a day*

## The Plate

Look at today's lunch. Bread from wheat grown somewhere. Cheese from milk from a cow that ate corn and soybeans. A tomato that was picked green and ripened in a truck. Olive oil from trees that might be 200 years old. Salt from an evaporation pond or a mine.

Each item on the plate has a trail. The trail includes farmers, fertilizer plants, tractors, irrigation pumps, harvesters, trucks, elevators, mills, factories, packaging plants, warehouses, refrigerated trailers, wholesalers, grocery stores, and you. Along the trail, roughly 30% of all food produced globally is lost or wasted, which is astonishing when you consider how many people are still hungry.

## The Twenty Plants

Humans get most of their calories from a surprisingly small list:

- Rice, wheat, maize (corn) — the big three grains, ~50% of global calories.
- Potato, cassava, sweet potato — starchy roots and tubers.
- Sugar cane, sugar beet — concentrated carbohydrates.
- Soybean, peanut, sunflower, canola — oil and protein.
- Banana, orange, apple, grape, tomato — the mass-market fruits and vegetables.
- A few dozen more supply regional staples (sorghum, millet, yam, plantain, teff, quinoa).

Out of perhaps 300,000 plant species, about 20 feed the world. This is efficient but fragile. Banana production is currently threatened by a single fungal disease (TR4). Wheat production in specific regions is threatened by a single rust strain (Ug99). Monoculture at civilizational scale has monoculture risk at civilizational scale.

## The Nitrogen You Don't Think About

Before 1909, the amount of plant growth on the planet was limited by how much biological nitrogen fixation could happen — bacteria in root nodules of legumes, a little lightning, a little volcanic activity. That ceiling could feed maybe 2 billion people.

In 1909 Fritz Haber figured out how to pull nitrogen out of the air and combine it with hydrogen under high pressure and temperature to make ammonia, NH₃. In 1913 Carl Bosch scaled the process to industry. Ammonia becomes urea, becomes ammonium nitrate, becomes the fertilizer that grows the wheat that feeds the world.

Today, Haber-Bosch consumes roughly 1-2% of all energy used on Earth, and the nitrogen in roughly half the protein in your body came through that process. The estimate — that about 4 billion of the 8 billion humans alive would not be alive without synthetic fertilizer — is a reasonable back-of-envelope.

## The Tractor That Replaced a Village

In 1800, farming occupied 80-90% of the population in most countries. Today in the US it occupies about 1.5%. One modern farmer produces what once took hundreds. The tractor, the combine harvester, the irrigation system, the refrigerated trailer, and the GPS-guided planter are the reason.

This is one of history's great labor reallocations. Most of everybody used to farm. Now almost nobody does. The people who were freed went into cities, factories, offices, research labs, and the knowledge economy. The entire modern world runs on the surplus labor that mechanized agriculture released.

## The Soil That Is Vanishing

Soil forms at roughly 1 centimeter per century. It erodes, under plow agriculture, at 1 centimeter per decade on average, faster where the land is steep or the practices are careless. Do the math: we are losing topsoil about ten times faster than it forms.

The Dust Bowl of the 1930s was one visible version of this. Much of the modern version is invisible: soil that looks fine on the surface but has lost most of its organic matter and microbial diversity. Regenerative agriculture, no-till farming, cover cropping, integrated crop-livestock systems — these are ways to slow or reverse soil loss. They are not yet the majority practice.

Farming's long-term sustainability is not a question about abstract ecology. It is a question about whether there will be topsoil in the places that feed us, in a century.

## The Animal in the Middle

About 26% of Earth's ice-free land is used for livestock grazing. Another 33% of all cropland grows feed for livestock, not human food. The efficiency of converting plant calories to meat calories is low — very roughly, 10:1 for beef, 4:1 for pork, 2:1 for chicken, 1.5:1 for farmed fish.

This is why meat is environmentally costly: you are farming enough plants to feed the animal before you eat the animal. Most of the environmental conversation about food reduces to this conversion. Cellular agriculture (growing meat without an animal) is one of the Speculative L9 frontiers trying to break this ratio.

## The Cold Chain That Keeps You Alive

Before refrigeration, most people ate what was in season and in reach. Meat spoiled. Milk spoiled. Fish were eaten at the dock. Only grain and some roots kept longer.

Modern refrigeration — ice, then compressor refrigeration (1920s), then global cold-chain logistics — means that food can travel from harvest to consumer across the planet without spoiling. The banana on your counter came from Ecuador or Honduras and has been at 13.3°C for its entire journey. This is not an accident. Someone designed it.

Cold chain is why people eat more fresh produce than any generation in history. It is also why a cold chain failure (war, power grid collapse, pandemic) is a public health crisis within days.

## The First Lesson

Food does not come from a grocery store. It comes from soil, water, sunlight, nitrogen, animals, labor, machines, and logistics. Every meal sits on top of a civilization-scale system that took 10,000 years to build up and is surprisingly dependent on a few choke points (fertilizer plants, staple crops, fossil-fuel-powered logistics).

The most important invention of civilization is not the computer or the wheel. It is the plow. Everything else follows from having enough surplus food to let most people do something other than grow it.
