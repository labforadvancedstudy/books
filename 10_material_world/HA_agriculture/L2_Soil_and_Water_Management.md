# Level 2: Soil and Water Management
*The two non-negotiable inputs — how to keep land fertile and water available*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Two Constraints

You can change seeds, switch crops, adopt new machinery, and shift labor, but two inputs remain non-negotiable for agriculture: **soil** that holds nutrients and allows roots to grow, and **water** that the crop can draw on when it needs it. Get either one wrong over time and productivity collapses, regardless of everything else.

Most of the dramatic productivity gains of the 20th century came from fertilizers (replacing soil nutrients), irrigation (substituting for or supplementing rain), and better genetics (tolerating degraded conditions). Each of these is now under strain. Soil degradation is widespread, groundwater is being depleted in major agricultural regions, and climate change is destabilizing rainfall patterns. The next century of agriculture depends on managing soil and water better than the last.

## What Soil Is

Soil is a mixture of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air, and living organisms. The ratios matter:

- **Mineral solids** (~45% by volume in typical agricultural soils): sand, silt, clay. Particle size controls drainage, aeration, and nutrient retention. Sandy soils drain fast but hold little. Clay soils hold water and nutrients but drain poorly and compact easily. Silty soils are in between. A balanced "loam" is prized for most crops.
- **Organic matter** (typically 2–8% in healthy agricultural soils, much higher in prairie or forest soils): decomposing plant residues, humus, living roots, microbial biomass. Holds nutrients, supports soil structure, feeds the microbial community.
- **Water** (typically 20–30% of volume, varying with rainfall and irrigation).
- **Air** (the rest — critical for root respiration and microbial activity).
- **Living organisms**: bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, arthropods, earthworms, and plant roots themselves. A teaspoon of healthy soil contains billions of microbes and thousands of species.

Soil is a living system, not just dirt. Treating it as inert substrate — just something to support plants with chemistry added — degrades it over time.

## Soil Fertility

Plants need three macronutrients in large amounts — **nitrogen (N)**, **phosphorus (P)**, **potassium (K)** — plus secondary nutrients (calcium, magnesium, sulfur) and micronutrients (iron, zinc, manganese, copper, boron, molybdenum, nickel, chloride).

Nitrogen is the main limit in most agricultural systems. Natural soils get N from atmospheric fixation by legumes (alfalfa, soybean, clover, peas) and by free-living bacteria, plus organic matter decomposition. Industrial N comes from the **Haber-Bosch process** (fixing N₂ from air into ammonia using natural gas) — the single most important invention of 20th-century agriculture by tonnage.

Phosphorus is mined (mostly from Morocco, China, Russia, US). Global P reserves are finite; at current use rates, estimates range from decades to centuries before economic reserves are exhausted. P efficiency improvements and recycling matter long-term.

Potassium is also mined, mainly from Canada, Russia, Belarus, China, Germany. K supplies are more abundant than P.

Soil testing (done properly, on a sampling grid representative of the field) tells the farmer what's present, what's deficient, and what the pH is. The fertilizer prescription follows from that test plus the target yield of the crop.

**Overapplication** — more fertilizer than the crop needs — wastes money and causes environmental damage. Excess N volatilizes (contributing to nitrogen oxides in the atmosphere) or leaches to groundwater (nitrates in drinking water) or runs off (eutrophication of rivers and coastal waters, producing dead zones like the Gulf of Mexico's at the Mississippi's mouth). Excess P similarly runs off, causing freshwater eutrophication. Precision agriculture aims to match inputs to crop need at smaller spatial scales, reducing waste.

## Soil Structure and Tillage

**Soil structure** is the arrangement of particles into aggregates separated by pores. Good structure means stable aggregates with diverse pore sizes — small pores holding water, large pores allowing drainage and root growth.

**Tillage** (plowing, disking, cultivation) was the ancient answer to weed control, seedbed preparation, and incorporating residues and fertilizer. It was also the main cause of soil degradation on agricultural lands:

- Tillage breaks soil aggregates, exposing organic matter to rapid microbial decomposition.
- Tillage leaves soil surface bare, vulnerable to wind and water erosion.
- Repeated tillage forms a **plow pan** — compacted layer below the tilled zone that roots can't penetrate.
- Tillage disrupts soil fungi and earthworms.

**No-till** and **reduced tillage** have spread substantially since the 1980s. Crops are planted directly into stubble from the previous crop, with herbicides replacing tillage for weed control and seedbed prep. Benefits: reduced erosion, better water infiltration, more organic matter preserved, lower fuel and labor. Drawbacks: higher herbicide use, sometimes slower soil warming in spring (especially in cold climates), residue management challenges.

**Cover crops** (grown between cash crops to protect the soil) and **crop rotation** (alternating crops to break pest cycles and rebuild nitrogen) further improve soil health. The combination of no-till, cover crops, diverse rotations, and reduced inputs is sometimes called **regenerative agriculture** — a term more popular than precisely defined, but the practices are well-established.

## Soil Erosion

**Water erosion** — the dominant form worldwide. Raindrops dislodge particles; flowing water carries them to gullies and streams. Rates on bare, sloped agricultural land can be 10–100 tonnes per hectare per year — soil forming at ~1 tonne per hectare per year under natural conditions. Over decades, this is unsustainable.

**Wind erosion** — dominant in dry lands with little vegetative cover. The American Dust Bowl of the 1930s was a catastrophic case. Chinese and Central Asian dust storms continue to transport topsoil. Shelterbelts, cover crops, and reduced tillage all reduce wind erosion.

**Tillage erosion** — the soil moved downslope by plows accumulates in the bottoms of fields. Visible as lighter-colored soil on ridge tops and darker soil in depressions.

**Erosion control practices**: terracing on steep land, contour plowing, strip cropping, waterways to conduct runoff without gullying, buffer strips along streams, windbreaks on cropland margins. USDA conservation programs have paid farmers for decades to adopt these; results are substantial but still inadequate — most US croplands continue to lose soil faster than it forms.

## Irrigation

Only ~20% of world cropland is irrigated, but it produces ~40% of the food. Irrigation stabilizes yields, enables cropping where rainfall is inadequate, and supports higher-value crops that wouldn't tolerate water stress.

Main irrigation types:

- **Surface irrigation**: water flooded across the field by gravity. Simple, low cost, water-inefficient. Most of India's, Pakistan's, and Egypt's irrigation.
- **Sprinkler irrigation**: water pumped through overhead sprinklers or center pivots. Moderate efficiency. Much of US Plains and European irrigation.
- **Drip irrigation**: water delivered in small amounts directly to plant roots through low-pressure tubing. Highest efficiency (80–95% vs. 50–70% for surface). Capital-intensive. Israel pioneered it; widely used in vineyards, orchards, high-value vegetables.

Irrigation water sources:
- **Surface water** from rivers and reservoirs. Affected by upstream uses, seasonal availability, drought.
- **Groundwater** from aquifers. Reliable but depletable. Over-pumping lowers water tables and can cause subsidence or salt intrusion.

Major groundwater depletion zones: US High Plains (Ogallala Aquifer), Northern India, North China Plain, Iran, Saudi Arabia. In some of these, pumping now exceeds recharge by large margins — a finite resource being consumed. Agricultural production in those areas will have to adjust, eventually.

## Salinity

**Soil salinity** — accumulation of salts in the root zone — is a classic failure mode of irrigated agriculture in dry climates.

Mechanism: irrigation water contains dissolved salts (all natural water does). Evaporation and plant uptake remove pure water, leaving salts behind. Without sufficient drainage to flush salts below the root zone, they accumulate. Eventually the soil becomes too saline for crops.

Ancient Mesopotamia collapsed partly from salinization. The Aral Sea basin suffered similar collapse in the Soviet era. California's San Joaquin Valley, India's Punjab, Pakistan's Indus basin, Egypt's Nile Delta, and Australia's Murray-Darling basin all face active salinity management.

Solutions: adequate drainage (tile drains to carry away saline drainage water), periodic flushing, salt-tolerant crops where salinity is already high, and ultimately shifting land out of irrigation where the salt balance can't be maintained.

## Water Quality in Agriculture

Beyond quantity, water quality matters. Problems:

- **Nitrate in groundwater** from fertilizer leaching. Health hazard for infants ("blue baby syndrome"). Regulated drinking water standard: 10 mg/L (US), 11.3 mg/L (EU as NO₃⁻-N). Major aquifers in intensively farmed regions frequently exceed this.
- **Pesticide residues** in groundwater and surface water. Less common in properly managed systems; significant in some hotspots.
- **Sediment** from eroded fields. Major problem for water supply reservoirs and downstream ecosystems.
- **Bacterial contamination** from manure. E. coli, salmonella, cryptosporidium all have come from agricultural runoff.

Regulatory frameworks (US Clean Water Act, EU Water Framework Directive, national equivalents elsewhere) target water-quality impacts of agriculture. Progress is slow because agricultural runoff is **nonpoint-source pollution** — spread across huge areas, hard to monitor individually, politically protected. Voluntary programs, cost-share payments, and sometimes regulation have reduced but not eliminated the impacts.

## Climate-Smart Agriculture

Climate change is shifting rainfall patterns, increasing extreme events, and changing temperatures that crops experience. Adaptation includes:

- **Drought-tolerant varieties**: bred for water efficiency. Common in corn (drought-tolerant hybrids widely adopted in semi-arid regions).
- **Earlier planting and shorter-season varieties**: taking advantage of earlier springs.
- **Shifting crop regions**: corn and soybeans move north in North America; wheat belts shift. Mediterranean and tropical zones face difficult transitions.
- **Water-efficient irrigation**: drip, mulching, deficit irrigation.
- **Soil organic matter**: builds water-holding capacity and buffers weather variability.
- **Agroforestry**: trees in agricultural systems modify microclimate, reduce erosion, diversify income.

**Mitigation** — reducing agriculture's climate footprint — includes reducing fertilizer overuse (N₂O is a potent greenhouse gas), managing manure better, reducing methane from livestock and rice paddies, improving soil carbon storage, and reducing food waste.

Agriculture accounts for ~15% of global greenhouse gas emissions directly, with another ~10% from associated land-use change (mostly tropical deforestation for cattle ranching and palm oil). Reforming it is essential for climate goals.

## Why This Level Matters

Soil and water are the physical substrate of all agriculture. Mismanagement over decades has degraded both in most of the world's major agricultural regions. The next several decades need to preserve, rebuild, and allocate these resources while feeding ~10 billion people.

Every other agricultural technology — better seeds, improved machinery, digital tools — sits on this foundation. Countries and regions that manage soil and water well will prosper agriculturally; those that don't will import food, suffer land degradation, and eventually face crises.
