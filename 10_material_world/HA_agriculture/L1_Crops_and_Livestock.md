# Level 1: Crops & Livestock
*The 20 plants and animals that feed the world*

## The Narrow Diet of Civilization

Humans cultivate about 7,000 plant species and have domesticated roughly 40 animal species. Of these, about 20 crops provide 90% of our calories, and about 5 animals provide almost all of our meat, eggs, and milk.

**The Big Four Grain Crops** (in rough order of global calorie production):
- **Maize (corn)**: about 1.2 billion tons/year. Mostly for animal feed and ethanol; about 15–20% of global production is directly eaten by humans.
- **Rice**: about 780 million tons/year. The calorie base for roughly half the world's population.
- **Wheat**: about 770 million tons/year. Bread, pasta, noodles; the staple of much of Europe, the Middle East, South Asia.
- **Barley**: about 150 million tons/year. Animal feed and brewing.

These four grains alone account for about 60% of global calorie consumption. Then come root crops (potatoes, cassava, sweet potatoes), grain legumes (soybeans, chickpeas, lentils), oil crops (palm, canola, sunflower, soybean oil), sugar crops (sugarcane, sugar beet), and a handful of others.

**The Livestock Big Five**: chickens (~75 billion slaughtered per year globally), pigs (~1.4 billion), cattle (~300 million), sheep (~550 million), goats (~460 million). Plus fish (~175 million tons/year from capture and aquaculture combined).

Nearly every bite of food you've eaten came from this short list. Modern supermarkets hide this by selling thousands of branded products, but trace the ingredients and nearly all of them reduce to the same dozen inputs.

## Why These and Not Others?

Most of the plants and animals humans eat were domesticated in the Neolithic period, roughly 10,000 years ago, in a few specific regions:

- **Fertile Crescent** (Turkey, Syria, Iraq, Iran): wheat, barley, lentils, peas, chickpeas, flax. Sheep, goats, cattle, pigs.
- **East Asia** (Yangtze and Yellow River valleys): rice, millet, soybeans. Pigs, chickens.
- **Mesoamerica**: maize, beans, squash. Turkey.
- **Andes**: potatoes, quinoa, sweet potatoes. Llama, alpaca, guinea pig.
- **New Guinea**: sugarcane, bananas, taro.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa**: sorghum, pearl millet, yams, cowpeas.

Most domestication required specific preconditions: a plant or animal that was dense in nutrients, storable, tolerant of cultivation, and had the right genetic flexibility. Jared Diamond's *Guns, Germs, and Steel* argued that the geographic distribution of these preconditions was a major driver of differential development — some continents (Eurasia) had many domesticable species along east-west biome bands; others (Americas, Africa) had fewer or harder-to-spread options.

This pattern still shapes global agriculture. The modern staples were already selected by 4,000 BCE. Since then, humans have bred thousands of variants within those same species but have added very few new staples.

## How Plants Grow

A plant needs:

- **Light**: for photosynthesis (converting CO₂ + water + light into sugar and oxygen).
- **Water**: as raw material and for transport.
- **CO₂**: the carbon source for all plant biomass.
- **Macronutrients**: nitrogen (for proteins), phosphorus (for DNA, ATP), potassium (for cellular function), plus calcium, magnesium, sulfur.
- **Micronutrients**: iron, zinc, boron, manganese, copper, molybdenum, and others in tiny amounts.
- **Temperature** in a tolerable range.
- **Soil structure** that allows roots to reach water and nutrients.

Limiting-factor analysis: a plant's yield is usually limited by whichever resource is scarcest. Add water to a desert and you still can't grow rice until you address nitrogen. Add nitrogen to an Iowa field and yield rises until water or phosphorus becomes limiting.

Modern agronomy measures limiting factors and adds them. The Haber-Bosch process (industrial nitrogen fixation) removed nitrogen as a limiting factor for most arable land. Phosphorus and potassium are mined and added. Micronutrients are added where soil tests show deficiency. Irrigation addresses water.

The result: maize yields in the US Midwest are about 11 tons/hectare — roughly 5–6× what the same land produced in 1900, and roughly 30× what pre-industrial agriculture achieved. Every doubling required solving a binding constraint (nitrogen, then hybridization, then pesticides, then irrigation, then precision agriculture).

## The Green Revolution

Between 1940 and 1970, a set of coordinated advances — dwarf wheat (Norman Borlaug), dwarf rice (IRRI), synthetic fertilizer, pesticides, irrigation, and extension services — roughly tripled grain yields in the developing world. This is the **Green Revolution**.

It is not an exaggeration to say this prevented mass starvation in South Asia, where population was growing and food supply was failing to keep up. India went from a famine-prone grain importer in the 1960s to self-sufficient and exporting by the 1980s. Similar transformations happened in Mexico, Pakistan, the Philippines, and China.

The Green Revolution also came with costs. It relied on fossil-fuel inputs (fertilizer from natural gas, diesel for machinery, synthetic pesticides). It favored irrigated, fertile land and did less for smallholder rainfed systems in Africa. It contributed to groundwater depletion (Punjab, north China plain, Ogallala aquifer). It reduced genetic diversity — thousands of local rice varieties were displaced by a few high-yielding breeds.

These are the second-order problems that modern agricultural research is still trying to address.

## The Animals

Livestock convert plant matter (often grass or grain) into denser protein and fat. They also provide milk, eggs, fiber, leather, and (historically) traction.

- **Chickens**: the most numerous and fastest-growing protein source. A broiler chicken reaches slaughter weight (~2.5 kg) in about 40 days, with a feed conversion ratio (kg feed per kg live weight gain) of about 1.8. Modern chicken farming is intense, controversial, and dominant.
- **Pigs**: FCR around 3–4. Versatile diet, including kitchen waste historically. Grown in industrial farrowing-to-finish operations in much of the world.
- **Cattle**: FCR around 6–10 for beef, depending on system. Grass-fed cattle have lower FCR measured in grain but require large land areas. Also provide milk; the modern dairy cow produces 8,000–12,000 liters/year (compared to perhaps 1,500 liters/year a century ago).
- **Sheep and goats**: important in pastoralist systems and on marginal land where cattle can't thrive. Global contribution to calories is modest.
- **Fish (aquaculture)**: the fastest-growing sector. FCR 1.0–1.5 for salmon and tilapia. Farmed fish now exceeds wild catch globally.

Livestock is the single largest use of agricultural land, directly and indirectly (through feed crops). It is also a major source of greenhouse gas emissions (methane from cattle, nitrous oxide from manure). Reducing beef and dairy consumption is one of the largest per-capita levers for reducing individual climate impact, though the effect size depends on what you substitute.

## Pests and Diseases

Every crop has pests: insects, fungi, bacteria, viruses, weeds, nematodes. Every livestock has diseases: bacterial, viral, parasitic.

Control measures:

- **Genetic resistance**: breeding crops and animals for resistance to specific pests. Constant coevolutionary arms race.
- **Pesticides**: herbicides (weeds), insecticides (insects), fungicides (fungi), etc. Cheap, effective, and create resistance in targeted species over time.
- **Integrated Pest Management** (IPM): combining biological control, crop rotation, and targeted pesticide use to manage pests with less chemical input.
- **Antibiotics and vaccines**: in livestock, routinely used. Also create resistance over time, with potential public-health spillover.
- **Quarantine and biosecurity**: preventing introduction of foreign pests and diseases. Major focus of modern border regulations.

The fight is continuous. A wheat-rust outbreak (Ug99, first identified in Uganda in 1999) could wipe out a large share of global wheat if it spread to Asia. Plant breeders continuously develop resistant varieties. Without this ongoing work, yields would collapse in many regions.

## Why This Level Matters

Agriculture is where civilization meets biology. Every other human activity — cities, industry, art, science — depends on a small fraction of the population growing enough food to feed the rest. The modern arrangement, where 1–2% of the population in rich countries feeds the other 98–99% plus exports, is historically unprecedented and rests on a stack of specific technologies, crops, livestock, and infrastructure.

Understanding crops and livestock gives you the vocabulary for every food, climate, and land-use policy debate. It also gives you perspective on what agricultural innovation can realistically do and how fast.
