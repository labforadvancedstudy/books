# Level 1: Mass & Energy Balances
*What goes in must come out — and if it doesn't, you have a problem*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## The Accounting Principle

Chemical engineering begins with a simple, powerful idea: whatever enters a system must either leave it, accumulate in it, or react away inside it. Mass is conserved. Energy is conserved. Every chemical process, whether a beaker reaction or a 500,000-barrel-per-day refinery, obeys these conservation laws.

The first thing a chemical engineer does with any process — real or proposed — is write down:

- **Mass balance**: (in) − (out) = (accumulation) + (consumed by reaction) − (generated by reaction)
- **Energy balance**: same structure, applied to energy flows.

These are not theoretical niceties. They catch errors in flow meters, size equipment, compute efficiency, verify measurements, diagnose problems, and plan scale-ups. A process that doesn't balance is a process you don't understand.

## The Control Volume

The first decision in writing a balance: what's the boundary? You choose a **control volume** — a region of space — and track flows across its boundary.

The control volume can be:

- **A single unit** (a reactor, a distillation column, a heat exchanger).
- **A subsystem** (the reactor plus its pre-heater plus its recycle loop).
- **The whole plant** (everything inside the fence, from feed trucks to product railcars).
- **A piece of equipment in time** (the reactor over one hour, or over one batch).

Choose the control volume to include what you care about and to cut boundaries where measurements are simplest. Writing the balance around the whole plant catches overall efficiency. Writing it around a single unit catches unit-level problems.

## Steady State vs. Dynamic

Most balances in continuous operation are done at **steady state**: accumulation = 0. Whatever comes in equals whatever leaves, minus what's consumed by reaction, plus what's generated.

Dynamic balances (transient — startup, shutdown, upset) are harder but necessary for:

- **Startup and shutdown**: equipment is filling or emptying.
- **Batch processes**: the whole point is dynamic — reactants are added, react, then products are withdrawn.
- **Control and stability**: how does the process respond to upsets?

Steady state is the simple case. Dynamic is the real case. A process engineer works in steady state for daily operations and dynamic for anything else.

## A Simple Mass Balance

Consider a continuous reactor where water mixed with salt enters, some water evaporates, and the concentrated brine leaves:

Feed: 100 kg/h of 5% salt solution (5 kg salt/h + 95 kg water/h)
Vapor: unknown, pure water
Product: unknown mass, 15% salt solution

Balance on salt (no reaction, so salt is conserved):
Salt in = salt out
5 = 0.15 × (mass of product)
Mass of product = 33.3 kg/h

Balance on total mass:
100 = vapor + 33.3
Vapor = 66.7 kg/h

That's the basic method. Real problems are bigger — plants with dozens of streams, reactions that convert species, recycle loops — but the logic is the same. You write enough equations, from each relevant species, to solve for the unknowns.

## Energy Balance

Energy enters and leaves a process in several forms:

- **Enthalpy** in flowing streams (mass flow × specific enthalpy). Dominant in most chemical processes.
- **Heat transfer** across boundaries (from heat exchangers, heat losses to ambient, heating or cooling duties).
- **Work** (compressors, pumps — usually small relative to enthalpy in typical chemical processes, but critical in refrigeration and gas processing).
- **Kinetic and potential energy**: usually negligible for chemical processes (important for pipelines with significant elevation changes).

The first law of thermodynamics applied to a control volume:

$\frac{dU}{dt} = \sum (\dot m h)_\text{in} - \sum (\dot m h)_\text{out} + \dot Q - \dot W$

At steady state, $dU/dt = 0$, and the equation simplifies to: enthalpy in − enthalpy out + heat added − work done = 0.

For processes with reaction, the heat of reaction appears as either an enthalpy term (if you use enthalpy of formation as reference) or as a heat generation term. Either convention works, consistently applied.

## Heats of Reaction

Chemical reactions release or absorb energy. **Exothermic** reactions (combustion, polymerization, most oxidations) release heat. **Endothermic** reactions (cracking, reforming, many dissociations) absorb heat.

The heat of reaction at standard conditions ($\Delta H^\circ_{rxn}$) is tabulated for common reactions and can be calculated from enthalpies of formation.

- Combustion of methane (CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O): −802 kJ/mol (exothermic, very).
- Steam reforming (CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂): +206 kJ/mol (endothermic).
- Ammonia synthesis (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃): −92 kJ/mol (exothermic, moderate).

The process designer must plan for this heat: cool exothermic reactors (or risk runaway); heat endothermic reactors (or reaction stops). Large-scale ammonia plants recover reaction heat to preheat feed, making the overall process much more efficient than naive analysis would suggest.

## Conversion, Yield, Selectivity

Three quantities describe how well a reaction is actually proceeding:

- **Conversion**: fraction of a reactant consumed. 80% conversion of A means 80% of A entering left as something other than A.
- **Yield**: fraction of theoretical product obtained. Ideally 100%, but side reactions, losses, and imperfect separation reduce this.
- **Selectivity**: fraction of consumed reactant that went to the desired product vs. byproducts.

For reactions with side products, high conversion and high selectivity are often in tension. Push for higher conversion and selectivity typically drops. A common solution: run the reactor at modest conversion, separate out the product, and recycle unreacted feed. This trades reactor size for separation cost but often gives better overall economics.

## Heat Exchangers

Chemical plants move heat around constantly — heating feeds before reactors, cooling products after reactors, reboiling bottoms of distillation columns, condensing overheads. The devices are **heat exchangers**.

Types:
- **Shell-and-tube**: one fluid in tubes, another in the shell around them. Industry workhorse. Handles high pressure and wide temperature ranges.
- **Plate-and-frame**: stacked corrugated plates with alternating fluids. Compact, easy to service, modest pressure.
- **Air coolers**: fans blow ambient air across finned tubes of process fluid. Used where cooling water is unavailable or expensive.
- **Specialty**: spiral, fired heaters (combustion chambers), cryogenic exchangers.

Heat exchanger design uses the basic relation $Q = U A \Delta T_{lm}$, where $Q$ is duty, $U$ is overall heat transfer coefficient (depends on fluids and geometry), $A$ is heat transfer area, and $\Delta T_{lm}$ is the log-mean temperature difference between streams. Larger areas transfer more heat. Higher $U$ (better heat transfer per unit area) reduces required area but usually costs more.

Energy integration across a plant — arranging heat exchangers so hot streams heat cold streams directly, minimizing external heating and cooling — is the domain of **pinch analysis**, a methodology that can reduce total plant energy consumption by 20–40%.

## Process Flow Diagrams

A **process flow diagram (PFD)** shows the major equipment, streams, and key operating conditions of a plant at a glance. Symbols are standard: circles for pumps, rectangles for heat exchangers, conical shapes for columns, etc. Streams are lines with flow direction arrows and numbers.

A **piping and instrumentation diagram (P&ID)** is the detailed version with every valve, instrument, and control signal. A PFD is a few pages for a whole plant; a P&ID can be dozens.

Reading and drawing PFDs is basic chemical engineering literacy. A process engineer can look at a PFD and immediately see the reactor, the separation train, the recycle loops, and the heat integration. They can estimate operating parameters and spot obvious inefficiencies.

## Measurement and Reconciliation

Real plants don't balance exactly. Flow meters have errors of 1–5%. Composition analyzers drift. Operators mis-read instruments. Material is lost to small leaks, sampling, and instrumentation purges.

**Data reconciliation** is the practice of adjusting measured values to satisfy conservation laws while minimizing the adjustments (weighted by measurement precision). Modern plants with digital control systems do this continuously. The reconciled data is the "true" picture of plant operation, used for performance monitoring, billing, and control optimization.

A large discrepancy in a reconciled balance is often the first sign of a problem — a failing meter, a leak, a reaction going unexpectedly. Balance-based monitoring catches problems before they become failures.

## Why This Level Matters

Mass and energy balances are the irreducible foundation of chemical engineering. Every other topic — reactor design, separation, heat transfer, process control, plant design, economics — builds on them.

An engineer who cannot write a mass and energy balance correctly cannot do chemical engineering. An engineer who is fluent in balances can analyze any process, spot mistakes, size equipment, and estimate costs before ever touching detailed design software.

The method is old (dates to the early 20th century formalization of chemical engineering), well-validated, and universal. The digital tools have changed; the underlying method has not.
