# Level 2: Building Design
*Materials, light, circulation, and program — turning structural possibility into inhabited space*

<!-- Evidence Tier: Textbook -->

## From Structure to Building

L1 covered structural engineering — the forces, members, and load paths that keep buildings standing. L2 turns to the larger problem: designing buildings that people actually use. Structure is necessary but not sufficient. A building must also let light in where needed, let air move, let occupants circulate, accommodate the activities it's meant to support, keep weather out, and (in most cases) be beautiful enough that the people paying for it want it built.

This work sits at the boundary of architecture and engineering. Architects lead on spatial, aesthetic, and programmatic decisions; engineers on structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems. On successful projects they collaborate continuously; on failed ones they work sequentially and reject each other's constraints. Modern integrated design — BIM (Building Information Modeling), integrated project delivery — pushes toward tighter collaboration.

## Program

**Program** is the list of what a building must accommodate: number of occupants, activities, room types, sizes, adjacencies, equipment, storage. A house's program is "three bedrooms, two baths, kitchen, living room, garage." A hospital's program runs hundreds of pages — operating rooms, emergency department, imaging, inpatient wings, support spaces, mechanical rooms, each with specific requirements for ventilation, power, shielding, cleanability.

Good design begins with the program but doesn't accept it uncritically. Programs are often written before design starts and codify assumptions that may not survive analysis. An experienced architect questions:

- Are these sizes based on furniture requirements or just traditional?
- Which adjacencies are actually functional, versus inherited from an old building?
- What operations would happen in this space, and could some be consolidated?
- Where does the client have flexibility, and where is it fixed?

Program refinement during early design — often called **programming** or **predesign** — saves much larger redesign cost later.

## Site and Context

A building exists on a site, in a climate, in a neighborhood. Site analysis covers:

- **Climate**: temperature, humidity, wind, solar path, rainfall, snow load, seismic activity.
- **Topography**: slope, drainage, views, access.
- **Soil**: bearing capacity, water table, expansive clay, rock.
- **Existing conditions**: vegetation, views, neighbors, noise sources.
- **Regulatory**: zoning (allowed uses, setbacks, heights), codes (building, energy, fire, accessibility), historic preservation, environmental review.
- **Utilities**: water, sewer, power, gas, telecom availability and capacity.
- **Access**: roads, transit, pedestrian, service vehicles.

Context also means cultural and architectural fit — does the building belong here? Different traditions give different answers, from strict contextual matching to deliberate contrast. But ignoring context entirely usually produces buildings occupants and neighbors resent.

## Circulation

How people, goods, air, and information move through the building structures everything else. Circulation elements:

- **Entries**: main and service. Control the first impression, control access, manage climate infiltration.
- **Corridors**: horizontal circulation. Width driven by code (egress capacity) and function.
- **Stairs and elevators**: vertical circulation. Egress stairs required by code; elevators required for accessibility and practical use in taller buildings.
- **Public vs. private**: most programs separate visitor zones from staff or resident zones.
- **Service circulation**: deliveries, waste, laundry, maintenance. Usually separated from main circulation.

A well-designed circulation system is legible — occupants find their way without signs. Hospital wayfinding is a standing problem; decades of research inform design patterns (consistent landmarks, color-coded zones, direct sight lines to destinations).

**Egress** is safety-critical. Codes specify minimum number of exits, maximum travel distances, minimum widths, protected stairwells, fire separation. Code compliance is baseline; good design makes exits also functional and pleasant in normal use.

## Light and Views

Natural light has outsized effects on occupant experience and energy use. Daylighting strategies:

- **Orientation**: long axis east-west maximizes north and south exposure; east and west faces receive harsh low-angle sun hard to control.
- **Window size and placement**: more glass allows more light but also more heat gain/loss.
- **Shading**: overhangs, fins, louvers, screens, trees. Southern overhangs in the Northern Hemisphere block high-summer sun while admitting low-winter sun.
- **Light shelves**: horizontal elements that bounce light deeper into a room.
- **Skylights and clerestories**: daylight from above for deep floor plates.
- **Interior finishes**: light-colored surfaces reflect and distribute daylight.

**Glazing** selection trades visibility, solar heat gain, and insulation (U-value). Low-e coatings reflect infrared while passing visible light; spectrally selective glass is tuned for specific climates. Triple glazing is standard in cold climates; single glazing is only acceptable in mild climates or unheated spaces.

**Views** contribute to wellbeing. Hospital patients with views of nature recover faster; office workers with views report less fatigue. Views are a budget constraint — perimeter offices command premium; interior ones with borrowed light or no light are lower-quality space.

## Thermal Comfort and HVAC

Keeping occupants comfortable requires managing temperature, humidity, air movement, and air quality.

**ASHRAE Standard 55** defines comfort zones: temperature 20-26°C, humidity 30-60%, air velocity <0.2 m/s at occupant level. Comfort depends on clothing, activity, and individual variation; even "optimal" settings leave some fraction uncomfortable.

**HVAC systems**:
- **All-air systems**: central air handlers distribute conditioned air via ducts.
- **Hydronic systems**: chilled and hot water circulate to terminal units (radiators, fan coils, radiant floors) at each zone.
- **Mixed systems**: central air handles ventilation and dehumidification; local hydronic or VRF units handle zone heating and cooling.
- **Natural ventilation**: operable windows and stack ventilation, where climate permits.
- **Mixed-mode**: combines natural ventilation with mechanical backup.

**Energy recovery ventilation (ERV)** reclaims heat and humidity from exhaust air to precondition incoming fresh air — essential for energy-efficient buildings in harsh climates.

Building energy codes (ASHRAE 90.1, IECC, Passive House, LEED, Net Zero) drive design. Modern high-performance buildings achieve 60-80% energy reduction from 1980s baselines through improved envelopes, efficient equipment, and controls.

## Envelope

The **envelope** is the boundary between indoors and out — walls, roof, windows, foundation. It must:

- **Keep water out**: roof drainage, flashing at penetrations, weather-resistant cladding, drainage planes behind cladding.
- **Keep vapor in (or out)**: vapor barriers positioned correctly for climate to prevent condensation inside the wall.
- **Resist air leakage**: air barriers reduce energy loss and prevent moisture transport.
- **Insulate**: minimum R-values by climate zone; continuous insulation to break thermal bridges.
- **Transmit light selectively**: glazing systems balanced for solar gain and daylight.
- **Resist fire**: fire-rated assemblies, separation from neighbors.

Envelope failures are the most common construction defect and the source of most water damage claims. Detailing at transitions — parapet copings, window flashing, foundation waterproofing — distinguishes buildings that age well from those that don't.

## Acoustics

Sound travels through air, through structure, and across gaps. Acoustic design:

- **Absorption**: soft, porous surfaces reduce reverberation.
- **Isolation**: heavy walls, staggered studs, resilient mounts block sound transmission between spaces.
- **Masking**: white noise or controlled background sound reduces intelligibility of distant speech.
- **Room acoustics**: shape, volume, surface materials determine reverberation time — critical for concert halls, classrooms, conference rooms, offices.

Open offices have acoustic failure modes — distant conversations intrude, private conversations broadcast. The shift from open to more enclosed offices in the 2020s was partly driven by acoustic complaints.

## Fire Safety

Fire codes specify design to limit fire initiation, spread, and impact:

- **Compartmentation**: fire-rated walls and floors contain fire to limited areas.
- **Egress**: adequate exits, lit and marked, reachable within code travel distances.
- **Sprinklers**: required in most commercial and many residential buildings. Radically reduce fatalities and property loss.
- **Smoke control**: pressurized stairs, smoke exhaust in atria, shaft separation.
- **Fire-resistant construction**: steel protected with spray-on coatings, concrete walls, fire-rated gypsum assemblies. Rating (1-hour, 2-hour, 3-hour) indicates how long the assembly resists fire penetration.
- **Detection and alarm**: smoke detectors, heat detectors, audible and visible alarms, annunciator panels for responders.

Fire loss statistics: commercial building fire deaths in sprinklered buildings are 60-90% lower than in unsprinklered. High-rise building codes after tragedies (MGM Grand 1980, Grenfell 2017) have progressively tightened.

## Accessibility

Buildings must accommodate people with disabilities — a legal requirement in most jurisdictions (ADA in US, similar laws elsewhere) and an ethical baseline. Key requirements:

- **Routes**: accessible paths from arrival to destinations, without stairs as sole option.
- **Doors and hardware**: operable with closed fist (lever handles, not round knobs), adequate clear width.
- **Bathrooms**: accessible stalls, grab bars, maneuvering clearances.
- **Signage**: tactile and visual, consistent placement.
- **Audible and visual**: alarms audible to hearing-impaired (strobes) and visible to sight-impaired (audible).

**Universal design** goes beyond minimum compliance — designing spaces that work well for everyone, including aging occupants, people with temporary injuries, people with strollers, visitors with diverse abilities.

## Materials

Architectural material selection balances structure, performance, cost, appearance, sustainability, and maintenance:

- **Concrete**: durable, fire-resistant, versatile. High embodied carbon from cement.
- **Steel**: strong, fast to erect, recyclable. Requires fire protection, corrosion protection.
- **Wood**: renewable, carbon-storing, warm. Limited height historically; mass timber (CLT) is changing this.
- **Masonry**: brick, stone, concrete masonry units. Durable, good thermal mass, higher labor cost.
- **Glass**: transparent, modern appearance. Poor insulator; requires careful selection.
- **Aluminum**: window frames, cladding, structural for some uses. High embodied energy but recyclable.
- **Composites**: fiber-reinforced polymers, cementitious composites. Specialty uses.

Interior finishes — flooring, wall coverings, ceilings, casework — determine much of what occupants actually see and touch. Maintainability and replacement cycles (carpet 10 years, paint 5-7 years, wood floor 30+) shape long-term cost.

**Embodied carbon** is increasingly a design factor. A building's carbon footprint during construction can equal decades of operational emissions; material choices substantially influence this. Concrete and steel dominate; mass timber, recycled content, and lower-carbon concrete mixes are active areas.

## Sustainability and Resilience

Climate-conscious design increasingly treats buildings as long-term infrastructure that must perform across changing conditions:

- **Net zero energy**: building produces as much energy (usually via rooftop solar) as it consumes annually. Achievable for most building types now.
- **Passive design**: orientation, insulation, thermal mass, shading, daylighting reduce HVAC loads before mechanical systems are sized.
- **Water conservation**: low-flow fixtures, efficient irrigation, greywater, rainwater harvesting.
- **Resilience**: design for flooding (raise critical equipment), wildfire (defensible space, noncombustible materials), power outage (backup generation, passive survivability), extreme heat (shading, thermal mass, ventilation without AC).
- **Adaptability**: floor-to-floor heights, structural grids, and system capacities that allow future changes of use.

Certifications (LEED, WELL, Living Building Challenge, Passive House, BREEAM) provide frameworks. Quality varies widely — some are rigorous; some are primarily marketing. Serious teams treat them as a checklist floor, not a ceiling.

## Why This Level Matters

Most people spend 90% of their lives inside buildings. Good buildings support the activities and wellbeing of their occupants, use resources efficiently, fit their place, and age gracefully. Bad buildings impose continuous small costs on occupants — uncomfortable temperatures, harsh lighting, noise, poor air quality, confusing layouts, premature failures — that accumulate into significant human cost.

Design decisions made in weeks during schematic design shape decades of occupation. The highest-leverage moments in a project's life are before construction starts. Beyond initial design, renovation and adaptive reuse increasingly dominate construction — existing buildings outnumber new construction by orders of magnitude, and designing their second lives is its own skill.
