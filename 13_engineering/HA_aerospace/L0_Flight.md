# Level 0: Flight
*Where everyone meets aerospace: by getting on a plane*

## The Seat You Sat In

You have probably flown commercially. You sat in a metal tube with roughly 200 other people, six miles above the ground, moving at 80% the speed of sound, inside a pressurized shell that keeps you alive at an altitude where the outside air is too thin to breathe and −55°C cold.

You may have read a book. Or slept. Or eaten a pretzel. The routineness is perhaps the most astonishing part. Flight used to be dangerous, rare, and heroic. It is now dangerous enough to be interesting (air crashes dominate news cycles when they happen), rare enough to be remarkable (you remember most of your flights), and heroic only in the sense that the entire system has been engineered to near-perfection so nobody has to be a hero on the flight.

## The Lift That Keeps You Up

An aircraft wing is shaped so that air flowing over it deflects downward. By Newton's third law, the air pushes the wing upward. That upward push is lift. When lift equals weight, the aircraft doesn't fall.

The popular "air travels faster over the top of the wing, so lower pressure, so lift" explanation is sort of true but incomplete. The fuller picture: the wing, through its shape and angle of attack, redirects the oncoming airflow downward. The reaction force is lift. This works at any reasonable angle of attack up to the point where the flow separates (stall), at which point lift collapses.

Bernoulli's principle is real. So is the deflection explanation. They are the same physics viewed from different coordinates. Any textbook that tells you one is right and the other is wrong is oversimplifying.

## The Engine That Pulls You Forward

A commercial airliner today uses turbofan engines. A turbofan takes in air at the front, slows it down and compresses it in a series of blades, adds fuel and ignites, the hot expanding gas spins a turbine that drives both the compressor and a large fan at the front. Most of the thrust comes from the fan accelerating a large mass of air backward (a little); a smaller fraction comes from the hot exhaust (accelerating a small mass of air backward a lot).

This is the bypass principle. High bypass ratio (modern engines: 10:1 or more) gives better efficiency and less noise. It is the reason modern airliners are both quieter and more fuel-efficient than 1960s-era jets. An engine like the GE9X on the Boeing 777X has a bypass ratio near 10 and uses about 10% less fuel than its predecessor.

Jet engines run for tens of thousands of hours between overhauls, burning fuel at hundreds of °C, at speeds that would rip ordinary metal apart. They are quietly one of humanity's highest engineering achievements.

## The Sky You Share

At any given moment, roughly 10,000 commercial aircraft are in the sky globally. Each is tracked by radar, transponder, and satellite. Each is separated from the others by air traffic control, which assigns altitudes and routes in layered airways. Each is obeying the same airspace rules and flight protocols whether it's over Kansas, the South China Sea, or the Atlantic.

The global aviation system is one of the most successful international coordinations in human history. ICAO (the International Civil Aviation Organization) sets standards that work in every country. A Chinese pilot, a French controller, and an American passenger can all get on an Airbus, use English for communication, and execute a flight across three continents without the system hiccuping.

This does not happen by luck. It is the product of a century of careful standardization, agreements, enforcement, and continuous improvement after every incident.

## The Safety Record

Commercial aviation is the safest mode of long-distance travel humans have ever used. The 2020s fatality rate in large jet operations is approximately 1 death per 10-50 billion passenger-kilometers, depending on the year and the region. Car travel is roughly a thousand times more dangerous per kilometer.

This did not happen automatically. Each major crash triggers an investigation, often leading to design changes, procedure changes, or training changes that prevent a specific failure mode from recurring. The system is reactive and continuously learning. A century ago, aviation routinely killed pilots. Now it routinely does not.

## The Rocket That Is Different

Rocket propulsion is aerospace's other half. A rocket carries its own oxidizer because it has to work in vacuum. A rocket's exhaust velocity determines how fast it can ultimately go, via the Tsiolkovsky equation: Δv = v_e × ln(m_start / m_end).

This equation is brutal. To reach low Earth orbit requires a Δv of about 9.4 km/s (the orbital speed plus gravity and drag losses). With a chemical rocket's exhaust velocity of ~3-4.5 km/s, you need your mass at launch to be roughly 10-20× your mass at orbit. Most of the rocket is fuel.

This is why single-stage-to-orbit has been so elusive and why staging (dropping empty tanks mid-flight) was necessary for the first 70 years of the space age. It's also why SpaceX's landing and reusing boosters has been such a big deal — it dramatically cuts the cost by reusing the hardest-to-build portion of the rocket.

## The Orbit You're Already In

Gravity pulls satellites toward Earth. Satellites move sideways fast enough that, while they fall toward Earth, Earth's surface also curves away at the same rate. They fall continuously and never hit the ground. That's an orbit.

Low Earth Orbit (LEO): 160-2000 km altitude, ~90 minute period, orbital speed ~7.8 km/s. The ISS is here. Most satellites now are here. Starlink is here.

Geostationary Orbit (GEO): 35,786 km altitude, 24 hour period, so the satellite appears fixed over one spot on Earth. TV broadcast satellites are here.

You interact with satellites constantly: GPS for navigation, weather prediction, phone connectivity in some places, imagery services, a lot of what used to be landlines. The aerospace engineering that made this affordable is a recent achievement — launch costs have dropped about 10x since 2010, largely because of SpaceX.

## The First Lesson

Aerospace is the engineering of flight: through atmosphere and beyond it. It has taken humans from the Wright Brothers' 1903 12-second flight to 7-hour commercial flights across oceans, to humans on the Moon, to satellites that redefined how we communicate and navigate.

Every flight you take — commercial, drone, even paper airplane — is enabled by 120 years of accumulated aerospace engineering. The discipline is still young. What it does next, in reusable heavy lift and in eventually affordable space access, will reshape what humans can do in space.
